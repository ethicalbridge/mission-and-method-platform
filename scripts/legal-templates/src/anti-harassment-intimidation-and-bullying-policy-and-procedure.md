# Who this policy applies to

This policy applies to, and protects, everyone who works with or for [[Organisation name]]: staff, including during probation; volunteers and interns; board members [[trustees / directors / committee members]] and advisers; contractors and consultants; job applicants; and partners' staff while they work with us. It also protects our team from harassment by partners, suppliers, funders, visitors and members of the communities we work with.

It applies wherever work happens: the office, project sites, homes used for remote work, events, training, work-related travel and accommodation, and work social events. It covers phone calls, email, messaging apps, video calls and social media, including private groups. It also covers conduct outside work, and after someone has left, when it affects a colleague or is linked to their work with us.

Sexual exploitation and abuse of the people we serve, and any concern about a child or adult at risk, follow our Safeguarding Policy and Procedure. If a concern falls under both policies, we follow the stricter steps. If you are unsure, report it and we will choose the right route.

**Law.** [[Organisation name]] is registered in [[country]] and follows its employment and equality laws. When we work in other countries, we also follow the law that applies there. Where this policy and the law differ, we follow the rule that gives people more protection. Nothing in this policy limits anyone's right to go to a labour authority, the police or a court.

::: adapt Law and countries
Check your employment, equality and criminal law on harassment, ideally with a local employment lawyer. International reference: the ILO Violence and Harassment Convention, 2019 (No. 190), and its Recommendation No. 206. It covers harassment across the "world of work", including online communication, work-related travel and commuting. Only some countries have ratified it; check the ILO's NORMLEX database for yours. Even where it is not ratified, it is a good benchmark.

Country notes to confirm locally:

- **India:** the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act 2013 requires employers with 10 or more workers to set up an Internal Committee, chaired by a senior woman and including an external member. Smaller organisations' complaints go to the district Local Committee. If this applies to you, your formal procedure must follow the Act.
- **Kenya:** the Employment Act 2007 requires employers with 20 or more employees to issue a policy statement on sexual harassment, after consulting employees.
- **South Africa:** the Employment Equity Act 55 of 1998 treats harassment as unfair discrimination, and the Code of Good Practice on the Prevention and Elimination of Harassment in the Workplace (2022) sets out what employers should do.
- **United Kingdom:** the Equality Act 2010 prohibits harassment, and since October 2024 employers must take reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment of their employees. From 30 October 2026, the Employment Rights Act 2025 raises this to "all reasonable steps" and makes employers liable for failing to prevent harassment by third parties, such as clients or suppliers.
- **Philippines:** the Anti-Sexual Harassment Act 1995 and the Safe Spaces Act 2019 set duties for employers, including on online harassment.
:::

# What we mean

## 1. Harassment

Unwanted conduct related to a protected characteristic (section 6) that violates someone's dignity, or creates an intimidating, hostile, degrading, humiliating or offensive environment. What matters is the effect on the person, not only the intention. A single serious incident can be harassment. Examples: jokes about someone's religion or accent; mocking a disability; repeatedly misgendering someone on purpose; displaying offensive images.

## 2. Sexual harassment

Unwanted conduct of a sexual nature with the same purpose or effect. Examples:

- sexual comments, jokes or questions about someone's private life or body;
- comments on appearance that continue after they are unwelcome;
- unwanted touching, hugging, kissing or standing too close;
- sending sexual messages, images or links, including by messaging apps;
- repeated invitations to meet, drink or date after a clear "no";
- asking someone to turn their camera on, or dress differently, for no work reason;
- treating someone worse because they rejected, or gave in to, sexual advances.

Linking any work benefit (a job, a contract, a good reference, a training place, travel, a per diem) to sex or a relationship is always sexual harassment and also sexual exploitation.

## 3. Bullying

Repeated, unreasonable behaviour, direct or indirect, that undermines someone's dignity, confidence or ability to do their work. Examples:

- shouting at, belittling or humiliating someone, especially in front of others;
- persistent unjustified criticism, or constantly changing targets so someone is set up to fail;
- deliberately excluding someone from meetings, messages or information they need;
- spreading rumours, or giving someone meaningless or impossible tasks;
- making unreasonable demands outside working hours.

A single incident is not usually bullying, but we still act on it. Honest feedback given respectfully, fair management of performance, and reasonable instructions are not bullying.

## 4. Intimidation and abuse of power

Using status, access or influence to pressure, threaten or coerce someone, or to keep them silent. Power can come from a job title, from controlling contracts, references, visas or pay, from being an international adviser, a funder's representative or a long-serving founder. Examples:

- threatening a bad reference, contract non-renewal or loss of a volunteer role unless someone works unpaid hours or accepts unwanted conduct;
- using someone's insecure position (temporary contract, immigration status, dependence on a stipend) to control them;
- belittling local staff or overriding local leadership because of nationality, education or "expert" status;
- **intimidating people who raise concerns:** pressing someone to withdraw a complaint, warning them it will "damage the organisation", asking who reported, or pressuring witnesses.

## 5. Online harassment

Harassment, bullying or intimidation through digital means: abusive or sexual messages, posting or sharing images without consent, creating fake accounts, doxxing (publishing someone's personal details), coordinated abuse in group chats, or tracking someone's location. It is treated exactly like conduct in person. Keep screenshots, with dates and the sender's details.

## 6. Discrimination and protected characteristics

Treating someone less favourably, stereotyping or excluding them because of their actual or perceived: race, colour, ethnicity, descent, caste, Indigenous identity, national or social origin or nationality; sex, gender, gender identity or expression, sex characteristics or sexual orientation; pregnancy, maternity, marital or family status, or caring responsibilities; age; disability or health status, including HIV status; religion or belief, including none; political opinion; language or accent; migration, refugee or citizenship status; socio-economic status; or any other characteristic protected by law. This list draws on the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the main UN human rights treaties and ILO Conventions 111 and 190.

::: adapt Protected characteristics
Keep this broad list: it reflects international standards and shows funders that you take inclusion seriously. Check that it includes every characteristic your national law protects. In some countries, certain characteristics (for example sexual orientation or gender identity) are not protected by law, or same-sex relationships are criminalised. You can still protect people under your own policy, but think carefully about how you record and share information that could expose someone to legal risk. Your Equality, Diversity and Inclusion Policy can say more.
:::

## 7. Retaliation

Treating someone badly because they raised a concern, supported someone who did, or took part in an investigation. It is a serious breach even if the original concern is not upheld. See "Protection from retaliation" below.

# Our commitments

[[Organisation name]] will:

1. make this policy known to everyone at induction, and discuss it with the whole team every year;
2. expect managers and board members to model respectful behaviour and act early on warning signs;
3. take every concern seriously, whoever is involved, and deal with it fairly, promptly and confidentially;
4. support people affected, and protect them and witnesses from retaliation;
5. include anti-harassment terms in contracts and partner agreements, and act when a partner, funder or community member harasses our team;
6. plan work so that people are not put at avoidable risk: for example, no lone late-night travel with a manager, separate rooms on work trips, and clear boundaries on out-of-hours messages;
7. learn from every concern and change what we do.

Culture, workload, urgency or someone's seniority never excuse harassment.

::: adapt Harassment by people outside the organisation
Think about who outside your team has power over your staff or volunteers: funders on monitoring visits, government officials, landlords, drivers, community leaders, or partners' managers. Say what you will do if one of them harasses your team, for example: remove your team member from the situation, raise it formally with the partner or funder, change who attends future meetings, and end the relationship if needed. For field teams, add practical steps such as travelling in pairs and agreeing a code word for "I need to leave". Your Travel Safety and Duty of Care Policy can cover this in more detail.
:::

# How to raise a concern

## 8. If you are in danger

If you are in immediate danger, contact emergency services on [[local emergency number]], then tell us. Physical or sexual assault is a crime, and you can report it to the police at any time. We will support you if you do.

## 9. The informal route

Many problems can be stopped early. If you feel safe doing so, you can:

- tell the person clearly that their behaviour is unwelcome and ask them to stop, in person or in writing;
- ask a colleague or the responsible person to be with you when you do this;
- ask the responsible person to speak to the other person for you, without starting a formal complaint;
- ask for a facilitated conversation, if both of you agree.

The responsible person keeps a brief, confidential note of informal concerns, so that patterns can be noticed. You never have to use the informal route. It is not suitable for serious matters such as sexual assault, threats or abuse of power, and you can move to the formal route at any time.

::: adapt Informal resolution and mediation
Decide who can facilitate a conversation. In a small team, an outside person may be fairer: a board member, a trained mediator, or someone from a partner organisation. Never pressure anyone into mediation or a face-to-face meeting, and never use mediation for sexual harassment or where there is a large power imbalance unless the person affected asks for it.
:::

Raise a formal concern by writing, emailing or speaking to:

We act on anonymous concerns as far as we can, but we may be limited if we cannot ask questions.

# What happens next

## 12. Assessing and protecting

The responsible person, or the independent contact if the responsible person is involved:

1. acknowledges the concern in writing and explains the process;
2. meets the person raising it, asks what outcome they hope for, and explains the options;
3. checks whether anyone is at risk, whether the concern belongs under another policy (such as safeguarding or fraud), and whether the law requires a report to the authorities;
4. agrees protective steps where needed: for example changing reporting lines, separating work schedules, moving meetings online, or suspending the person concerned on full pay. These steps are precautions, not findings, and should not disadvantage the person who raised the concern.

## 13. A fair investigation

The investigator is someone with no involvement in the matter and no close relationship with either side. For serious or complex cases, or where everyone in the organisation is too close to it, we appoint an external investigator. The investigator:

- tells the person concerned the substance of the allegation in writing, and gives them a real chance to respond and suggest witnesses or evidence;
- interviews each person separately, and never asks the person who raised the concern to meet the other person;
- allows each side to be accompanied at meetings by a colleague or trade union representative, or another supporter where we agree;
- keeps written notes, which each person can check;
- looks at relevant messages, records and documents;
- reaches a view on the balance of probabilities (whether it is more likely than not that the conduct happened) and gives reasons.

No one is presumed to have done wrong until the facts are established. Everyone involved keeps the matter confidential.

::: adapt Investigations and your Disciplinary and Grievance Procedure
Check your employment law. In many countries, an employer must follow a set process before a warning or dismissal: written notice of the allegation, a hearing, the right to be accompanied, and a written decision. In the United Kingdom, for example, workers have a legal right to be accompanied at disciplinary and grievance hearings. Make sure this section matches your employment contracts and Disciplinary and Grievance Procedure. Budget for an external investigator: ask a local employment lawyer, HR consultancy or a peer organisation for names before you need one.
:::

## 14. Outcomes

The decision is "upheld", "partly upheld" or "not upheld". Both sides receive it in writing, with the information they are entitled to, while protecting others' privacy. Depending on the facts and severity, outcomes include:

- no further action, or guidance, training or a facilitated conversation;
- a written warning, or a final written warning;
- changes to roles, reporting lines or working arrangements, which should not disadvantage the person affected;
- dismissal, or ending a volunteer role, board membership, contract or partnership, following our Disciplinary and Grievance Procedure and the law;
- referral to the police or other authorities where the law requires it or someone's safety demands it.

Where the law allows, we may share confirmed findings of sexual harassment with future employers who ask, following the principles of the Misconduct Disclosure Scheme. We also ask what we could change to prevent it happening again.

## 15. Appeals

Either side can appeal within [[10 working days]] of the decision, by writing to [[Name, role]] and saying why: for example an unfair process, new evidence, or an outcome out of proportion to the facts. The appeal is heard by someone not involved in the original case. If no such person exists inside the organisation, we ask an external adviser. The appeal decision is final within [[Organisation name]]. It does not affect anyone's rights under the law.

# Support for the person affected

Raising a concern can be stressful. The responsible person will:

- keep you informed at each stage, at least every [[10 working days]];
- agree with you practical steps that help you feel safe at work, such as flexible hours, working from home or not travelling with the person concerned;
- make sure you lose no pay, hours, training or opportunities because you raised a concern;
- point you to support where you live: [[employee assistance programme / counselling service / helpline / legal aid]];
- give you time off for medical, legal or counselling appointments linked to the matter.

The person concerned is also offered support, and kept informed.

# Protection from retaliation

Nobody will be dismissed, demoted, denied work, given a worse reference, excluded, or treated less well for raising a concern honestly or for helping with an investigation. Examples of retaliation: cancelling someone's contract or shifts; withdrawing a training opportunity; giving a poor reference; hostile messages or exclusion from group chats; pressure through family or community members.

Anyone who retaliates, or threatens to, faces disciplinary action up to dismissal or the end of their agreement. If something harmful happens to you after you raise a concern, we will treat it as retaliation unless there is a clear, separate and legitimate reason. We will reverse its effects where we can.

# False or malicious reports

Please raise what worries you. Nobody is penalised for a genuine concern, even if it is not upheld or some details are wrong. Knowingly making a false allegation, or reporting to damage someone, is misconduct. We decide this only after a review, never just because a concern was not upheld.

# Records, confidentiality and data

We share information only with people who need it to handle the concern, and protect the identity of the person raising it as far as we can. In a small team, the other person may guess who raised a concern: we will discuss this with you before we act. Everyone involved must keep the matter confidential, though anyone may get legal advice or contact a regulator.

The responsible person keeps case notes, evidence and outcomes in [[a restricted, encrypted folder]], separately from personnel files. Only the outcome and any warning go on a personnel file. We keep case records for [[6 years]] after the case closes, or longer if legal proceedings are ongoing or expected, and then delete them securely. Personal data is handled under our Data Protection Policy. We never put case information into AI tools or online services we have not approved.

::: adapt Records and retention
Check local employment and data protection law on how long you may keep grievance and disciplinary records, and on employees' rights to see them. Six years is common where it matches the period for bringing legal claims, but limitation periods differ by country. Add your choice to your Records Retention Schedule.
:::

# If this policy is breached

Breaches are handled through the procedure above and can lead to the outcomes in section 14. A manager who ignores a concern, or discourages someone from raising one, also breaches this policy.

# Monitoring and review

The responsible person gives the board an anonymised summary of concerns, timelines, outcomes and lessons every [[12 months]]. We ask the team every year, through an anonymous survey or conversation, whether they feel safe and respected at work. We review this policy every [[12 months]], after any formal complaint, and when the law changes.

This policy follows ILO Conventions 111 (1958) and 190 (2019), the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) and the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.
