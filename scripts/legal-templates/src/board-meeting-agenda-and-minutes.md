::: adapt Before you use this template
Use this template for every ordinary meeting of your board. Use it also for committee meetings, with fewer standing items. Check your governing document first: it usually sets notice periods, the quorum, how votes are taken, whether members can join remotely and whether the board can decide by written resolution. Where your governing document or local law is stricter than this template, follow it. Use the same numbering and format every time, so that anyone can find a decision years later. Delete the example rows and these orange boxes before use.
:::

# Purpose of this template

This template gives [[Organisation name]] three tools:

- **an agenda** with the standing items our board considers at every meeting, so nothing important is missed;
- **a minutes template**, including a decisions and actions log, which records what the board decided, who will act and by when;
- **a written resolution form** for decisions the board takes between meetings.

It follows the meeting rules in our Governing Body Terms of Reference. Guidance notes on good minutes are at the end.

# How to use it

1. **Four weeks before:** the chair and the [[Executive Director / founder]] agree the agenda (Part 1), marking each item for decision, discussion or information.
2. **[[14 days]] before:** the secretary sends notice of the meeting, with the date, time, place or video link, and the draft agenda.
3. **[[7 days]] before:** the secretary sends the agenda and all papers. Each paper starts with a short cover note saying what the board is asked to do.
4. **At the meeting:** the secretary or minute-taker records the meeting in Part 2, using the headings in the agenda.
5. **Within [[14 days]] after:** the secretary sends draft minutes to the chair for checking, then to all members. Actions go straight to their owners.
6. **At the next meeting:** the board approves the minutes, with any corrections, and the chair signs them (Part 4).

Put decisions early in the agenda when members are fresh, not at the end after long reports. Give each item a realistic time and a clear purpose: if an item is "for information" only, the board may take the paper as read. Group confidential items (staff matters, safeguarding cases, legal claims) at the end so that staff and guests can leave once. Add items from the annual calendar in the Governing Body Terms of Reference, for example approving the budget, the annual accounts or the annual Board Interest Declarations.
:::

::: adapt Quorum and remote attendance
Copy your quorum rule exactly from the governing document, for example "one-third of members or three, whichever is greater". If a member leaves for a conflict, they do not count in the quorum for that item: check the meeting is still quorate before the board decides. Record remote members as present only if your governing document allows remote attendance and they could hear and speak throughout. If the connection drops during a vote, record it and confirm the vote when they return.

## 9. Confidential minutes

Where an item is confidential, such as a named safeguarding case, a staff disciplinary matter, the [[Executive Director / founder]]'s pay or a legal claim, the main minutes record only the heading and the decision in general terms: "The board considered a confidential staff matter. A confidential minute is held by the chair." The full record is kept as a separate confidential minute, approved and signed in the same way, and stored with restricted access.

::: adapt Confidential minutes and access
Decide who keeps confidential minutes (usually the chair or the secretary) and where (an access-restricted folder, not the shared drive). Remember that people may have a right to see personal information about themselves under data protection law, and that a court, regulator or auditor may require minutes to be produced. Write confidential minutes as if the person discussed will one day read them. Check your local data protection law, for example the Kenya Data Protection Act 2019, Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023, South Africa's POPIA or Brazil's LGPD.
:::

# Part 3: Written resolutions between meetings

If a decision cannot wait for the next meeting, and our governing document allows it, the board may decide by **written resolution**: a decision agreed in writing, for example by email or e-signature, without a meeting.

## 10. Procedure

1. The chair agrees that the matter cannot wait and is suitable for a written resolution. Complex or contested matters go to a meeting, which may be held by video at short notice.
2. The secretary sends every voting member the text of the resolution, the supporting paper and a deadline of at least [[3 working days]].
3. Members reply to the secretary in writing: agree, disagree, or ask for a meeting. Any [[two]] members may ask for a meeting instead.
4. Members with a conflict of interest declare it and do not vote.
5. The resolution is passed when [[all eligible members / the majority required by the governing document]] have agreed in writing by the deadline.
6. The secretary tells all members the result, keeps the replies with the minutes and reports the resolution to the next meeting, where it is recorded in the minutes.

::: adapt Written resolutions and your governing document
Many governing documents allow written resolutions only if every member eligible to vote agrees, not a simple majority. Older constitutions may not allow them at all, or may require a signed paper copy. Check yours, and if it is silent, take advice before relying on email decisions, or propose an amendment. Banks and some regulators may ask for a signed or certified copy for decisions such as changing bank signatories.
:::

# Part 4: Approval and signature of the minutes

The draft minutes are not final until the board approves them. At the next meeting the board checks them for accuracy (not to reopen decisions) and agrees any corrections. The secretary records the corrections in the new minutes and in the approved version. The chair then signs and dates the approved minutes.

# Guidance notes: writing good minutes

## 12. Record decisions, not debates

Minutes are not a transcript. Record:

- who was present, who sent apologies, who was in attendance, and when anyone joined or left;
- that the meeting was quorate;
- every declaration of interest and how it was managed;
- each decision, in words clear enough that someone who was not there can act on it, with the main reasons and the options considered;
- the information the board relied on, by referring to the paper;
- votes and recorded dissent where relevant;
- each action, with one named owner and a date.

Leave out who said what, unless a member asks for their view to be recorded. Use neutral language, without opinions or adjectives such as "heated".

## 13. Write clearly and quickly

- Write the draft within [[3 working days]], while memory is fresh. Send it to the chair within [[7 days]] and to members within [[14 days]].
- Use the agenda numbering and headings.
- Write decisions in the past tense: "The board resolved to approve ...".
- Write in [[language]], in plain words, so future readers such as auditors and new members can follow.
- Attach or refer to the papers; do not copy them into the minutes.
- If you are unsure what was decided, ask the chair to summarise it aloud before moving on.

## 14. Confidentiality

Board papers and draft minutes are confidential until approved, and many items stay confidential after that. Do not name people the organisation serves, survivors, complainants or whistleblowers in the main minutes. Refer to safeguarding cases and staff matters by a reference number and keep the detail in confidential minutes or the relevant case file. Share approved minutes outside the board only as our Governing Body Terms of Reference and funder agreements require, and remove confidential items first. Confidentiality never prevents a member from raising a concern under our Whistleblowing Policy and Procedure or with a regulator.

## 15. Storage, the minute book and retention

- Keep approved, signed minutes in a single **minute book**: a bound book, or a secure digital folder with files in date order and numbered pages or references. Do not change them once signed. If an error is found later, correct it in the minutes of a later meeting.
- Keep agendas, papers, written resolutions and the decisions and actions log with the minutes.
- Keep minutes [[permanently]], as set out in our Records Retention Schedule. They are the organisation's history and may be needed many years later.
- Keep two copies in different places, for example a restricted cloud folder and an offline copy.

::: adapt Retention, signatures and filing
Check your local law on how long minutes must be kept and in what form. For example, the UK Companies Act 2006 requires companies to keep board minutes for at least ten years, and many charities keep them permanently. Some company and NGO laws require a physical minute book, minutes in the national language, or certified extracts to be filed with the registrar for certain decisions, such as changes of directors or of the governing document. Check whether your bank, registrar or regulator accepts electronic signatures and certified extracts. If you are unsure, check your local law on board records.
:::
