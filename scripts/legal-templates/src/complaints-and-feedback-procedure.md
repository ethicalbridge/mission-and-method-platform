# Purpose and scope

We want to hear from everyone affected by our work. Feedback helps us do better. Complaints help us put things right. Both help us keep people safe.

- **Feedback** is any comment, question, suggestion or praise about our work. For example: "the training was too short" or "can you hold sessions on Saturdays?".
- A **complaint** is an expression of dissatisfaction about our work, our staff or volunteers, or the way we have treated someone, where the person expects a response. It can be made in writing or out loud, formally or informally. Someone does not need to use the word "complaint".
- A **sensitive complaint** is about abuse, exploitation, harassment, fraud, corruption, or a serious risk to someone's safety. It follows a separate route (section 4).

This procedure follows Commitment 5 of the Core Humanitarian Standard (CHS): people can safely report concerns and complaints and get them addressed. It also reflects the CHS expectation that communities know what to expect from us and how to tell us when we fall short.

**What this procedure does not cover.** Staff and volunteer grievances follow [[our grievance procedure / the Anti-Harassment, Intimidation and Bullying Policy and Procedure]]. Disputes with suppliers under a contract follow that contract. If a complaint is about a partner's work, we pass it to the partner with the complainant's consent and follow up to make sure it is handled.

# 1. Who can complain or give feedback

Anyone can, including:

- people who take part in or use our services, and their families;
- members of communities where we work, including people who do not use our services;
- donors and supporters;
- partners, suppliers and volunteers, about how we work with them;
- members of the public, local authorities and community leaders.

Someone can also complain on behalf of another person, for example a parent for a child or a community leader for a group, with that person's agreement where possible.

**Raising a complaint will never affect the help, services or support anyone receives from us.** Anyone who treats a complainant worse because they complained breaches our Code of Conduct.

::: adapt Choose channels with the community
The best channels are the ones people actually use and trust. Ask community members, including women, older people, young people and people with disabilities, how they prefer to raise concerns before you choose. In low-literacy settings, rely on face-to-face channels, voice notes and community meetings rather than forms. Boxes work only if they are in private places, opened regularly by two people and visibly followed up. A hotline needs a person who answers. Messaging apps are convenient but keep records on phones: use a work phone, not a personal one, and delete messages once logged. Publicise channels on posters, at activities, and on any ID cards or materials you give out.
:::

| Acknowledge, explaining what happens next | Within [[5 working days]] | Within [[24 hours]] |
| Pass to the safeguarding or fraud lead | Not applicable | The same day, within [[24 hours]] at most |
| Full response with outcome | Within [[20 working days]] | Follows the timescales of the relevant policy |
| Appeal | Within [[20 working days]] of our response | As the relevant policy sets out |

If we need more time, we tell you why and give a new date. Simple feedback, such as a question or a suggestion, is answered within [[10 working days]], or at the next community meeting.

::: adapt Timescales
Choose timescales you can really meet with your team and connectivity. Five working days to acknowledge and twenty to respond are common, but a fundraising regulator, funder or partner may set shorter limits. Some fundraising self-regulatory bodies, for example the Fundraising Regulator for England, Wales and Northern Ireland, expect organisations to handle donor complaints first and then allow escalation to the regulator. Check whether your country has a fundraising, consumer or charity regulator that complainants can go to, and add its details to section 7.

# 4. Sensitive complaints: routed out immediately

Some complaints must not be handled as ordinary complaints. Whoever receives one of the following passes it **the same day** to the lead named below, and does nothing else except make people safe:

| The complaint is about | Pass it to | Under |
|---|---|---|
| Abuse, neglect or exploitation of a child or adult at risk; sexual exploitation, abuse or harassment (SEAH) by anyone connected to us | Safeguarding lead, [[Name, contact]] | Safeguarding Policy and Procedure |
| Fraud, theft, bribery, corruption or misuse of funds or aid | [[Fraud lead, Name, contact]] | Fraud, Bribery and Corruption Policy and Procedure |
| Bullying or harassment of staff or volunteers | [[Name, contact]] | Anti-Harassment, Intimidation and Bullying Policy and Procedure |
| Lost or misused personal data | [[Privacy lead, Name, contact]] | Data Breach Response Procedure |
| Any other serious risk to someone's safety | Incident lead, [[Name, contact]] | Incident Reporting Procedure |

When you receive a sensitive complaint:

1. If someone is in immediate danger, contact emergency services on [[number]] first.
2. Listen. Write down what was said, in the person's words, with the date and time.
3. Do not ask for details beyond what you need to pass it on. Do not investigate, and do not contact the person complained about.
4. Do not promise to keep it secret. Explain who you will tell and why.
5. Keep it confidential. Do not log the details in the general complaints log; record only a reference number and "referred".

If a complaint is about the feedback and complaints lead or the director, it goes to [[Name, role]], who reviews appeals.

::: adapt Mandatory reporting and referral
In many countries some forms of abuse, especially of children, must be reported to the police or social services. Check your local law on mandatory reporting and on the age of a child. List local support services (health, legal, psychosocial and shelter) that you can refer survivors to with their consent. If you work alongside other agencies, check whether there is an inter-agency PSEA network or community-based complaints mechanism you should join or refer to.
:::

# 5. Looking into a complaint

For standard complaints, the feedback and complaints lead decides who will look into it. That person:

- has not been involved in the matter complained about, and does not manage anyone who was;
- contacts the complainant, where possible, to understand what happened and what outcome they want;
- tells anyone complained about what the complaint is, gives them a fair chance to respond, and keeps it confidential;
- gathers the information needed, in proportion to how serious the complaint is;
- decides on the balance of probabilities, which means "more likely than not", and records the reasons.

Many complaints can be resolved quickly and informally, for example by explaining a decision, correcting a mistake or apologising. That is fine, as long as it is logged and the complainant is satisfied.

We treat complainants and the people complained about with respect. A complaint is not proof that someone did wrong.

# 6. Outcomes and response

We respond to the complainant in the way they prefer: in writing, by phone, in person or through someone they trust. The response explains what we found, what we will do, and how to appeal. Annex B has a model letter.

Possible outcomes include:

- an explanation of what happened and why;
- an apology;
- putting things right, for example providing a service that was missed or correcting a record;
- a change to how we work;
- action with a staff member or volunteer, under our disciplinary process. We do not share details of disciplinary action, but we can say that action has been taken.

If we find that we did nothing wrong, we explain why, respectfully.

::: adapt Remedies you can offer
Decide in advance what the complaints lead may offer without asking the director, for example an apology, a replacement item or a missed service, and what needs approval, such as refunding a donation or any payment. Many organisations refund a donation made in error if asked within [[a set period, e.g. 30 days]]. Never offer money or favours to persuade someone to drop a complaint, and never as a remedy for a sensitive complaint without advice.
:::

# 7. Appeals

If you are not satisfied with our response, you can appeal within [[20 working days]] to [[Name, role]], by any of the channels above. They were not involved in the first decision. They review how the complaint was handled and the outcome, and reply within [[20 working days]]. Their decision is final within [[Organisation name]].

You can also contact an outside body at any time, for example [[charity regulator / fundraising regulator / ombudsman / funder's complaints line]]. For a data protection complaint, you can contact the [[data protection authority]].

# 8. Closing the loop with communities

We tell people what we have heard and what we have done about it. This builds trust and shows that feedback is worth giving.

- Every [[month / quarter]], the feedback and complaints lead prepares a short anonymised summary: main topics, what we changed, and what we could not change and why.
- We share it at community meetings, on noticeboards, by voice note or WhatsApp broadcast, and in [[languages]], using the format "you said, we did".
- Where we cannot make a change people asked for, we say so and explain why.
- We never share anything that could identify a complainant.

# 9. Records, confidentiality and learning

The feedback and complaints lead keeps a complaints log (Annex A) in [[secure location]]. Only people handling a complaint can see its details.

- Personal information is used only to handle the complaint and is protected under our Data Protection Policy.
- Sensitive complaints are recorded only by reference in this log. Their details stay in the safeguarding or fraud case records.
- We keep complaint records for [[3 years]] after closure, or longer if legal action is possible, in line with our Records Retention Schedule.
- Every [[quarter]], the lead reports trends to the director and the board: number and type of complaints and feedback, how quickly we responded, outcomes, and changes made. The board checks that lessons are acted on.

::: adapt Retention and data
Three years is a common period for standard complaints; safeguarding and fraud records are usually kept longer, as their own policies set out. Check your local data protection law on how long you may keep personal data, and whether complainants have a right to see their records, for example under GDPR-style laws such as the Kenya Data Protection Act 2019, Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023, POPIA in South Africa or Brazil's LGPD.
:::
