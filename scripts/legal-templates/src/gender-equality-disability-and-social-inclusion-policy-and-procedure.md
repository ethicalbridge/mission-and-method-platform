# Who this policy applies to

This policy applies to everyone who designs, delivers, monitors or communicates about our programmes: staff, volunteers, interns, board members, contractors and consultants, and partners' staff working on our projects, through our agreements with partners.

It covers the whole programme cycle: assessment, design, proposals and budgets, delivery, events, research, monitoring, evaluation, reporting, and communications about the people we serve, in every country where we work and online.

**What this policy does not cover.** How we treat our own team, including recruitment, pay, workplace adjustments and anti-discrimination at work, is covered by our Equality, Diversity and Inclusion Policy.

**Law.** [[Organisation name]] is registered in [[country]] and follows its laws, and the law of each country where we work. Where this policy sets a higher standard than the law, we follow this policy, as long as doing so is lawful and safe. Where a funder's or partner's GEDSI requirements are stricter, we follow them on that project.

# What GEDSI means

GEDSI stands for gender equality, disability and social inclusion. It means that everyone our work is meant for can take part in it, have a say in it and benefit from it.

{widths: 28 72}
| Term | What we mean |
|---|---|
| **Gender equality** | Women, men, girls and boys, and people of diverse gender identities where it is safe and lawful to name them, have equal rights, opportunities and power. Gender means the roles and expectations societies attach to being a woman or a man; they differ between places and change over time. |
| **Disability** | We follow the social and rights-based model of the CRPD. Disability results from the interaction between a person's long-term physical, psychosocial, intellectual or sensory impairment and the barriers around them: attitudes, buildings, information, rules. Removing barriers is our job, not the person's. |
| **Social inclusion** | Removing the barriers that exclude people because of age, ethnicity, caste, Indigenous identity, language, religion, migration or displacement status, sexual orientation, where they live, poverty, or other factors that matter locally. |
| **Intersectionality** | People have several identities at once, and barriers add up (see below). |
| **Reasonable accommodation** | A change or support that lets a particular person take part on an equal basis, without a disproportionate burden on us: for example an interpreter, a ramp or a large-print handout. Our Equality, Diversity and Inclusion Policy calls this a "reasonable adjustment" for our team. |
| **OPD and WRO** | An organisation of persons with disabilities (OPD) is led and controlled by people with disabilities. A women's rights organisation (WRO) is led by women and works for women's and girls' rights. |

**Intersectionality in practice.** A training course may welcome women and people with disabilities, yet still leave out a young deaf woman from a minority language group: sessions are in the national language, with no sign language interpreter, at the time she collects water. Looking at one factor at a time would miss her. So we ask: who faces several barriers at once, and what would it take for them to take part?

# Our principles

- **Do no harm.** We check that our work does not increase risk, stigma, violence or workload for anyone, for example where giving women income or assets could lead to a backlash or violence at home, and we plan with them to prevent it.
- **Nothing about us without us.** We involve the people affected in decisions about our work, including through OPDs, WROs, youth groups and other organisations they lead. Meaningful participation means real influence, accessible information and fair payment for people's time.
- **Twin-track approach.** We mainstream inclusion, building it into every part of every project, and add targeted actions for groups facing the greatest barriers, such as a support group for caregivers of children with disabilities.
- **Leave no one behind.** We start with those furthest behind, and check who we are not reaching.
- **Transformative where possible, realistic always.** Where we can, we tackle causes of exclusion, such as harmful norms or unequal decision-making, not only symptoms. We set goals we can deliver and measure, at a pace the community supports.
- **Safety first.** In places where some identities are criminalised or targeted, protecting people comes before visibility, data or publicity.

::: adapt Safety where identities are criminalised
In some countries, same-sex relationships, gender identity, certain religions or beliefs, political views or membership of certain ethnic groups are criminalised or put people in danger. Where this is the case:

- do not ask about, record or publish these identities, even voluntarily, in programme data, reports, photos or case studies;
- use wording that is lawful and safe locally: you may keep "people of diverse gender identities" internally and use "everyone in the community" in public materials;
- follow the advice of trusted local partners and the people concerned, and take local legal advice;
- consider not publishing this policy in full online, while keeping it in force internally.

Never put anyone at risk to make a public statement. See also "Sensitive contexts" in the Equality, Diversity and Inclusion Policy.
:::

# Our commitments across the programme cycle

## 1. Context and GEDSI analysis at design

Before we design a project or write a proposal, we complete a GEDSI analysis (Annex A): who is affected, who is left out and why, what risks each group faces, and what opportunities exist. We use existing data first, such as national statistics, OPD and WRO reports and partners' knowledge, and fill gaps through consultation.

## 2. Consultation and participation

- We consult women, men, girls and boys, people with disabilities and other excluded groups during design, in separate groups where that helps people speak freely.
- We invite OPDs, WROs and other community organisations to advise or partner with us, and we pay them for their time.
- We tell people what we heard and what we changed because of it, and we involve them in reviewing the project.
- We use methods that let quieter people contribute, such as small groups, pictures or one-to-one conversations.
- We consult children only after a safeguarding risk assessment and with consent, under our Safeguarding Policy and Procedure.

## 3. Accessible activities and information

We plan every activity so that everyone it is meant for can take part, using the checklist in Annex D. As a minimum we consider:

- **Venues:** step-free access, accessible toilets, seating, and a safe journey, especially for women and girls after dark.
- **Formats:** plain language, easy-read versions (short sentences with pictures), large print, audio, and documents that work with screen readers.
- **Languages:** [[local languages]], with interpretation, including sign language, where needed.
- **Timing:** times that fit around work, care, school, prayer and seasonal tasks.
- **Practical support:** childcare, transport, and costs for personal assistants or caregivers.
- **Digital:** offline or phone-based options for people without internet.

## 4. Budgeting for inclusion

Every project budget has a dedicated line for accessibility and reasonable accommodation, such as interpreters, accessible formats, transport, childcare and venue changes. As a starting point we set aside [[3–5]]% of activity costs for it. We also budget for GEDSI analysis, consultation, paying OPDs and WROs, and training. If we cannot afford a needed measure, we tell the funder and look for an alternative with the people concerned, rather than dropping it silently.

::: adapt Accessibility budget
The [[3–5]]% figure is an illustrative rule of thumb, not a standard: there is no single international rule. Base your figure on what your activities really need. One sign language interpreter for a two-day workshop can cost more than the venue, and projects targeting people with disabilities will need more. Some funders accept or expect a dedicated accessibility line: check each call for proposals. Keep part of the line unallocated for requests during the project.
:::

## 5. Working with partners

- We assess partners' GEDSI practice as part of our Partner Due Diligence and Sanctions Screening Policy, in proportion to the partnership.
- We do not exclude a partner only because it lacks a written policy: many practise inclusion well without one.
- Where a partner wants to strengthen its practice, we agree support together and fund it, for example training or an accessibility audit.
- Our agreements, such as a Sub-grant Agreement, ask partners to apply this policy, or their own if it is at least as strong, and to report disaggregated data. Their budgets include the accessibility line and the cost of collecting this data.
- We actively seek partnerships with OPDs, WROs and organisations led by excluded groups, and we pay fair overheads.

## 6. MEAL: measuring who benefits

MEAL (monitoring, evaluation, accountability and learning) shows whether our work reaches and benefits everyone it is meant for.

- **Disaggregated data** means figures broken down by group. We collect and report data by sex, age group ([[0–17, 18–29, 30–59, 60+]]) and disability, as a minimum. We add other factors, such as location, language or displacement status, only where they are relevant, lawful and safe.
- **Disability data.** We use the Washington Group Short Set of questions on functioning (see the box below). We do not ask about diagnoses, and answering is voluntary.
- **GEDSI indicators.** Each project has at least [[two]] GEDSI indicators, for example the share of participants with disabilities, or of women in decision-making roles.
- **Participatory feedback.** We use feedback methods that work for people who cannot read, who have disabilities or who may not speak in public, such as voice messages and separate group meetings. See our Complaints and Feedback Procedure.
- **Consent and data protection.** We collect personal data with informed consent, using our Research Participation Consent Form where relevant, and follow our Data Protection Policy. Before collecting disability or other sensitive data, the project lead asks the data protection lead whether a Data Protection Impact Assessment is needed. We report totals only, never groups so small that people could be identified (fewer than [[5]] people).
- **Learning.** We review the data with participants and partners, and act when groups are missing out.

::: adapt Disability data and the Washington Group
The Washington Group Short Set (WG-SS) asks six questions about difficulty in seeing (even with glasses), hearing (even with a hearing aid), walking or climbing steps, remembering or concentrating, self-care (such as washing or dressing), and communicating in one's usual language. Each has four answers: no difficulty, some difficulty, a lot of difficulty, cannot do at all. The Washington Group recommends counting a person as having a disability if they answer "a lot of difficulty" or "cannot do at all" to at least one question. Use the official wording, translated and tested locally, and train data collectors to ask respectfully. The questions are for disaggregating data, not for deciding who is eligible for support. The Short Set misses some psychosocial disabilities; the WG Short Set Enhanced adds questions on anxiety and depression. For children, check the separate Child Functioning Module developed by the Washington Group and UNICEF.
:::

## 7. Communications and storytelling

- We show people with dignity, as active people with their own views, and avoid stereotypes, such as people with disabilities as objects of pity or inspiration.
- We show diversity honestly, and use the words people use for themselves. Where in doubt, we ask.
- We get informed consent for every photo, video and story, using our Photo and Story Consent Form, in a language and format the person understands.
- We never publish images or details that could identify someone at risk, including survivors of violence and people whose identities are criminalised.
- Our materials are accessible: plain language, video captions, image descriptions and good colour contrast. Our Social Media and Communications Policy sets out the details.

## 8. Safeguarding and protection from SEAH

Women, girls, children and people with disabilities often face higher risks of abuse, including sexual exploitation, abuse and harassment (SEAH). Our GEDSI analysis and our safeguarding risk assessment inform each other. Everyone, including people with communication disabilities and people who cannot read, must know how to raise a concern in a format they can use. Any concern about harm is handled under our Safeguarding Policy and Procedure.

## 9. Our team's capability

- Everyone who works on programmes receives a short GEDSI briefing when they join and a refresher every [[2 years]].
- Project leads and MEAL staff are trained in GEDSI analysis and disability-inclusive practice within [[6 months]] of taking on the role, with OPDs and WROs helping to deliver training where we can.
- The GEDSI focal point has about [[X hours a month / X% of their time]] for this work.

## 10. Gender and inclusion markers

Some funders score each project against a gender or disability marker. We score our projects honestly at design and claim only what our design, budget and indicators support.

::: adapt Gender and disability markers
Many funders report their aid to the OECD Development Assistance Committee (DAC) using policy markers. The **DAC gender equality policy marker** scores a project **2 (principal)** if gender equality is its main objective, **1 (significant)** if it is an important, deliberate objective but not the main reason for the project, and **0 (not targeted)** otherwise. The **DAC disability marker**, introduced in 2018, uses the same three levels. A score of 1 or 2 usually needs a gender analysis, disaggregated data and at least one matching objective or indicator. Examples:

- **European Union:** the Gender Action Plan III (2021–2025, extended to 2027) set a target for 85% of new external actions to have gender equality as a significant or principal objective by 2025.
- **Australia (DFAT):** its design standards expect investment designs to draw on GEDSI analysis, and new investments of AUD 3 million and above must include gender equality objectives.
- **United Kingdom:** the International Development (Gender Equality) Act 2014 requires the government to have regard to reducing gender inequality before providing development assistance, and to gender-related differences in needs in humanitarian aid, so FCDO-funded projects are often asked about it.

UN agencies and foundations often use their own markers or scales. Read each funder's guidance and use their definitions.
:::

## 11. GEDSI steps for every project

The project lead applies these eight steps to every new project and any major change. For a short activity, such as a one-day event, steps 1 to 5 can be done together in an hour.

|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Screen | Project lead | At concept stage, before a proposal | Annex B, stage 1; decision on full analysis |
| 2. GEDSI analysis | Project lead, with GEDSI focal point | Before design is final | Annex A completed |
| 3. Consult | Project lead and partners | During design | Notes of who said what, and what changed |
| 4. Design and budget | Project lead and finance | When writing the proposal | Activities, indicators, accessibility budget line |
| 5. GEDSI action plan | Project lead; approved by [[Name, role]] | Before the project starts | Annex C completed and approved |
| 6. Implement and adapt | Project team and partners | Throughout | Annex D used for each activity; access requests met |
| 7. Monitor | MEAL lead or project lead | Every [[quarter]] | Disaggregated data and feedback reviewed; action plan updated |

At each step, ask the same question: who is missing, and why? Add GEDSI risks to the Risk Register, and change the action plan whenever you learn something new.

## 12. Asking for access support or reasonable accommodation

Anyone taking part in our activities, including participants, caregivers and partners' staff, can ask for support to take part. Staff asking for workplace adjustments use our Equality, Diversity and Inclusion Policy.

- **How to ask.** Tell any team member, use the question on our registration form, or contact [[Name, role]] at [[phone / WhatsApp / email]], in person, by voice message or through someone you trust.
- **No proof needed.** You do not need to give a diagnosis.
- **Quick response.** We respond within the time limits below. If we hear on the day, we do what we can on the spot.
- **Confidential.** We share your request only with the people who arrange the support, and keep it separately from other records.
- **Cost.** We pay for reasonable support. If we cannot provide exactly what you asked for, we explain why and agree an alternative.

## 13. Raising a concern about exclusion

If you think our work is excluding or treating some people unfairly, please tell us.

1. Raise it with any team member or the GEDSI focal point, [[Name]], at [[email / phone]].
2. Participants, communities, partners and the public can also use our Complaints and Feedback Procedure, including [[community feedback channels / anonymous route]].
3. If the concern involves harm, abuse, exploitation or harassment, report it to the safeguarding lead within the time limits above, under our Safeguarding Policy and Procedure.
4. Anyone can also use our Whistleblowing Policy and Procedure.

We look into every concern fairly and tell you what we will change. Nobody will be treated badly, or lose access to our services, because they complained.

# Records, confidentiality and data

Each project file holds its GEDSI analysis, consultation notes, action plan and reviews. Access requests and exclusion complaints are kept securely and separately, with access limited to [[Name, role]] and those arranging support. We keep personal data only as long as our Records Retention Schedule allows and handle it under our Data Protection Policy. Data about disability, ethnicity, religion or sexual orientation is sensitive under most data protection laws: we collect it only for a clear purpose, with explicit consent or another condition the law allows, and never where it could put someone at risk.

# If this policy is breached

We take failures to follow this policy seriously, especially if someone is excluded or harmed. Depending on the case, we may give guidance or training, change how a project works, act under our Code of Conduct and Disciplinary and Grievance Procedure, or review or end a partnership. Where our own systems, budget or planning caused the exclusion, we fix the system rather than blame individuals.

# Monitoring and review

Each year the GEDSI focal point gives the board a short report covering:

- who took part in our programmes, by sex, age and disability, compared with the population we aim to reach;
- progress on GEDSI indicators and action plans;
- access requests, how quickly we met them, and spending on the accessibility line;
- complaints about exclusion and what we changed;
- partnerships with OPDs, WROs and organisations led by excluded groups, and training completed.

The board agrees up to [[three]] priorities for the coming year. We review this policy at least every [[two years]].

# Annex D: Accessibility checklist for activities and events

Use this for every activity, meeting, training or event. Not every item applies every time. Ask participants and OPDs what matters most.

### Before the activity

[ ] The invitation asks if anyone needs support to take part, and says how to ask.
[ ] Invitations reach excluded groups, for example through OPDs and WROs.
[ ] The time suits people's work, care duties, school and religious observance.
[ ] Interpreters (spoken and sign language) are booked where needed.
[ ] Materials are in plain language and [[local languages]], with easy-read, large-print or audio versions where needed.
[ ] Transport and childcare are arranged where needed.

### Venue

[ ] Step-free entrance or a ramp, and space for wheelchairs.
[ ] Accessible toilets, and separate toilets for women and men where expected.
[ ] Good lighting, low background noise and seating with backs.
[ ] A safe journey to and from the venue, including after dark.
[ ] A quiet space for breaks, prayer or breastfeeding.

### During and after the activity

[ ] Facilitators speak clearly, face the group and describe images aloud.
[ ] Regular breaks, and methods that let quieter people contribute.
[ ] Everyone knows how to raise a concern or give feedback, in a format they can use.
[ ] Attendance is recorded by sex, age and disability, with consent.
[ ] Feedback on access is collected and acted on, and access costs are recorded.
