Delete any section that clearly does not apply (for example, consumer law if you sell nothing), but think twice before deleting: many small organisations find gaps where they least expect them. If you work in more than one country, complete the checklist once for the country of registration and once, more briefly, for each country where you run activities, employ people or hold a bank account. This checklist is a guide to what to ask, not a statement of the law in any country.

# How to use this checklist

1. Tick each item that is **true and you can show it** with a document, filing or record. Note where the evidence is kept.
2. If the answer is no, or you are not sure, leave it unticked and copy it into the adviser brief at the end, as a question.
3. Prioritise unticked items that could lead to a fine, loss of registration, harm to a person or loss of funding.
4. Take the brief, your governing document and the documents in question to your adviser. Record their answers and the actions agreed.
5. Report the result to the board, and add significant gaps to the Risk Register.

# The checklist

## 1. Registration and legal form

[ ] We know our exact legal form (for example association, trust, society, non-profit company, foundation, co-operative or social enterprise company) and it allows everything we do, including any trading.
[ ] Our registration is current, and our registered name, address and purposes match what we actually do.
[ ] We are registered, or have confirmed we do not need to be, in every country where we run activities, employ people or hold a bank account.
[ ] We know which regulators supervise us and what changes we must report to them (for example a new board member, address or purpose).

## 2. Annual filings

[ ] We keep a compliance calendar of every filing and renewal deadline (annual return, accounts, tax returns, regulator reports, registration renewal), each with a named owner.
[ ] Our last annual return and accounts were filed on time with every authority that requires them.
[ ] Our accounts are audited or independently examined where the law, our governing document or a funder requires it.

## 3. Governing documents

[ ] We hold a signed, current copy of our governing document, and every amendment was approved and filed as required.
[ ] Our purposes cover all current activities, including new programmes, countries and income streams.
[ ] Board appointments, terms of office, meetings and minutes follow the governing document and our Governing Body Terms of Reference.
[ ] Our core policies were approved by the body the governing document requires.

## 4. Signing authority and banking

[ ] We know who may sign contracts, open bank accounts and commit the organisation, and this matches the Delegation of Authority Matrix.
[ ] Our bank mandates require [[two]] authorised signatories and list only current people.
[ ] Any powers of attorney or letters of authority are recorded and withdrawn when the person leaves.

## 5. Tax status and registration

[ ] We know whether we are exempt from income or corporate tax, and we hold written confirmation.
[ ] We are registered for, and pay, the taxes that apply to us: employer withholding on salaries, social security, and VAT or sales tax if our sales exceed the threshold.
[ ] If we tell donors their gifts are tax-deductible, we hold the approval and issue receipts in the required form.
[ ] We deduct and pay any withholding tax due on payments to contractors and foreign suppliers.

::: adapt Tax country notes
Tax exemption and donor relief are separate approvals in many countries. Examples: in India, registration for exemption (long known as 12A or 12AB) and approval for donor deduction (80G), section numbers from the Income-tax Act 1961 that may differ under the Income-tax Act 2025, so check the current provisions; in South Africa, approval as a public benefit organisation and, separately, under section 18A for donor receipts; in the United States, 501(c)(3) status from the IRS for US donors. Tax law changes often, so ask a local accountant or tax adviser to confirm the current position and filing dates.
:::

## 6. Foreign funding and NGO registration

[ ] We have checked whether we need government approval to receive foreign funds, and we hold any approval required.
[ ] Our NGO, non-profit or public benefit registration or permit renewal date is in the compliance calendar, and all reports under it are up to date.
[ ] We have checked the rules on moving money across borders, foreign exchange and passing funds to partners (sub-granting).

::: adapt Foreign funding rules
Several countries regulate foreign funding closely. India is a well-known example: under the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act 2010, as amended in 2020, an organisation needs FCRA registration or prior permission to receive foreign funds, registration must be renewed every five years, foreign funds must first be received in a designated FCRA account at the State Bank of India, New Delhi Main Branch, and passing foreign funds to other organisations is restricted. Other countries require NGO permits that must be renewed regularly, for example under Uganda's NGO Act 2016, or set rules on foreign funding and registration categories, for example Ethiopia's civil society proclamation of 2019. Rules change and are sometimes applied strictly, so confirm current requirements with a local lawyer.
:::

## 7. Employment, volunteers and contractors

[ ] Every employee has a written contract that meets local legal minimums (pay, hours, leave, notice, probation).
[ ] People we treat as contractors or volunteers are genuinely contractors or volunteers under local law, not employees in disguise.
[ ] We are registered as an employer and pay the required contributions (social security, pension, health, work injury).
[ ] Anyone who is not a national has the right to work, and we hold copies of permits.
[ ] Our disciplinary, grievance and termination procedures follow local employment law.

::: adapt Employment status
Misclassifying staff as contractors or volunteers is one of the most common legal risks for small organisations. Courts usually look at the reality, not the label: who controls the work and hours, whether the person is part of the team, whether they work only for you, and who provides equipment. The ILO Employment Relationship Recommendation 2006 (No. 198) sets out these indicators. Volunteers who receive regular payments beyond real expenses may count as employees and be owed the minimum wage in some countries. Check your local employment law.
:::

## 8. Safeguarding legal duties

[ ] We know whether the law requires us to report abuse of children or adults at risk, to whom and how quickly.
[ ] We know which background or criminal record checks we may, or must, carry out for roles with children or adults at risk, and we carry them out.
[ ] We know the local law on age of majority, child labour and consent for children's photos and data.

## 9. Data protection

[ ] We know which data protection laws apply to us: where we are registered, where the people whose data we hold are, and where our data is stored.
[ ] We have registered with the data protection authority, or paid any fee, where required.
[ ] We have a Privacy Notice, a lawful basis for each use of personal data, and consent forms where we rely on consent.
[ ] We know the rules on transferring data abroad and the deadline for reporting a data breach.

::: adapt Data protection registration
Some laws require organisations to register or pay a fee, with exemptions that vary. Examples: Kenya requires registration with the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner under the Data Protection Act 2019 and its 2021 registration regulations; South Africa requires the information officer to be registered with the Information Regulator under POPIA; Nigeria requires data controllers and processors "of major importance" to register with the Nigeria Data Protection Commission under the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023; in the UK most organisations pay a data protection fee to the Information Commissioner's Office, with some exemptions for not-for-profits. Check whether exemptions for small organisations apply to you.
:::

## 10. Sanctions and counter-terrorist financing

[ ] We know which sanctions lists our funders require us to check (usually the UN Security Council consolidated list, and often US OFAC, EU and UK lists).
[ ] We screen partners, sub-grantees, major suppliers and [[staff]] before contracting, and record the result, as set out in our Partner Due Diligence and Sanctions Screening Policy.
[ ] We understand the counter-terrorism clauses in our grant agreements and apply a risk-based approach, as FATF Recommendation 8 expects.

## 11. Funder conditions

[ ] We have mapped the conditions of every current grant (eligible costs, procurement, reporting, audit, branding, data, safeguarding, notification of incidents) with deadlines and owners.
[ ] We pass on ("flow down") funder conditions to partners in our Sub-grant Agreement where the funder requires it.

## 12. Contracts register

[ ] We keep a register of all contracts, leases, memoranda of understanding and grant agreements, with renewal and termination dates.
[ ] Our key contracts have been checked for governing law, liability, indemnities, intellectual property, data and termination.

## 13. Insurance

[ ] We hold every insurance the law requires (for example employer's liability or workers' compensation, and vehicle third-party cover).
[ ] We have considered, and decided in writing, on public liability, professional indemnity, trustee or directors' liability, property and equipment, travel and medical evacuation, and cyber insurance.
[ ] Our policies cover volunteers, all the countries where we work and all our activities, including events and work with children.

::: adapt Insurance
Compulsory cover varies. For example, employer's liability insurance is compulsory for most employers in Great Britain; Kenya's Work Injury Benefits Act 2007 requires employers to insure employees; in South Africa employers register under the Compensation for Occupational Injuries and Diseases Act 1993. Trustee or directors' liability (sometimes called D&O) is optional but protects board members personally. Travel and medical evacuation cover matters most where staff travel to areas with limited health care. Some funders specify minimum cover in the grant agreement. Ask a broker for quotes and record decisions not to insure.
:::

## 14. Intellectual property and brand

[ ] We have checked that our name and logo do not conflict with others, and registered them as trade marks where useful, and our domain names are held in the organisation's name.
[ ] We own, or have a licence for, materials created by staff, contractors and volunteers, and we hold licences for the software, images, music and fonts we use.

## 15. Fundraising

[ ] We hold any permits needed for public collections, raffles, lotteries or fundraising events, and follow any fundraising code or registration that applies.
[ ] Our fundraising claims are accurate, and we follow the law on contacting donors and using their data.

## 16. Selling goods or services

[ ] If we sell goods or services, our terms of sale, refunds, product safety, labelling and pricing (including tax) meet consumer law.

## 17. Records

[ ] Our Records Retention Schedule meets the legal minimum periods for accounting, tax, employment and safeguarding records, and our records are stored securely.

# Guidance: getting low-cost legal advice

You do not need an expensive retainer to get good advice. Prepare well, ask specific questions and use these routes:

- **Pro bono programmes.** TrustLaw, run by the Thomson Reuters Foundation, connects NGOs and social enterprises in many countries with law firms that give free advice. Advocates for International Development (A4ID) brokers free legal help for organisations working on development. PILnet supports pro bono networks in several regions. Each has eligibility criteria; check them before applying.
- **University law clinics.** Many law faculties run clinics where supervised students help non-profits with registration, governance documents and employment questions.
- **National NGO umbrella bodies and networks.** They often offer template reviews, legal helplines, training on new laws and collective advice, and know how regulators apply the rules in practice.
- **Bar associations and law societies.** Some run pro bono schemes or referral services.
- **Reference sources.** The International Center for Not-for-Profit Law (ICNL) publishes country information on the laws that govern civil society.
- **Accountants and auditors** for tax and filing questions, and **insurance brokers** for cover.

When you use free advice, write down the answers, confirm them by email with the adviser, and note the date: the law changes. For anything with high stakes, such as dismissal, a dispute, a regulator's investigation or a serious safeguarding case, pay for specialist advice if you must.
