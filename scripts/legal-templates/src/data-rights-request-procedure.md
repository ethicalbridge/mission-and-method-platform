# Purpose and scope

This procedure explains how [[Organisation name]] handles requests from people who want to use their rights over their personal information. Personal information, also called personal data, is any information about a person who can be identified. It includes names, phone numbers, photos, voice recordings, case notes, survey answers, donation records and opinions written about someone.

It covers information in any form: databases, spreadsheets, email, shared drives, messaging apps, paper files, photos and records held for us by service providers. Anyone can make a request: participants and their families, donors, staff and former staff, volunteers, job applicants and people mentioned in our records.

**Law.** [[Organisation name]] is registered in [[country]] and follows [[name of the national data protection law and year]]. When we hold information about people in other countries, we also follow the law that applies to them. We use the principles of the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) as our benchmark, because many funders expect it. Where rules differ, we follow the one that gives people more protection.

::: adapt Law and countries
Name your national law and regulator. Examples: Kenya, Data Protection Act 2019 (Office of the Data Protection Commissioner); Nigeria, Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 (Nigeria Data Protection Commission); South Africa, Protection of Personal Information Act 2013, POPIA (Information Regulator); Brazil, LGPD, Law 13.709 of 2018 (ANPD); India, Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (Data Protection Board of India); Indonesia, Law 27 of 2022 on Personal Data Protection; Argentina, Law 25.326; Peru, Law 29733. If you hold data about people in the EU or UK, the GDPR or UK GDPR may also apply. If your country has no data protection law yet, keep the GDPR benchmark and say so.
:::

# The rights people have

The names and scope of these rights differ between countries. The table follows the GDPR, which most modern laws resemble.

{widths: 22 42 36}
| Right | What the person can ask | Common limits |
|---|---|---|
| Access | Confirmation that we hold their information, a copy, and how we use it | Other people's information and some safeguarding records may be withheld |
| Correction | That we correct inaccurate information or complete what is missing | An opinion recorded accurately as an opinion may get a note instead |
| Deletion | That we delete their information | Not where we must keep it by law, for a grant audit or a legal claim |
| Restriction | That we keep their information but stop using it, for example while we check it | We may still store it and use it for legal claims |
| Objection | That we stop using it where we rely on our legitimate interests, and always for direct marketing | We may continue for compelling reasons, never for marketing |
| Portability | Information they gave us, in a common electronic format | Only for automated processing based on consent or contract |
| Withdraw consent | That we stop any use that relies on their consent | Past use stays lawful |
| Automated decisions | Not to face a significant decision made only by a computer, and to have a person review it | Limited exceptions set by law |

People can also complain to the data protection regulator, and in many countries to a court.

::: adapt Rights in your country
Check which rights your law gives, and use the same names as your Privacy Notice. Examples: India's DPDP Act 2023 gives rights to a summary of data, correction, completion, updating and erasure, grievance redressal and nominating someone to act for you, but no general right to portability or objection. Brazil's LGPD adds the right to know who data has been shared with, and to anonymisation or blocking. Kenya's Data Protection Act 2019 includes rights to be informed, access, object, correction, deletion and portability. South Africa's POPIA links access requests to the Promotion of Access to Information Act 2000. Argentina's Law 25.326 focuses on access, rectification, updating and suppression.
:::

# How requests arrive

A request does not have to mention the law or be in writing. These are all requests:

- "Can you send me everything you have on file about my son?"
- A former volunteer says on the phone: "Please delete my details."
- A participant tells a field officer that they no longer want their photo on the website.

**If you receive a request**, through any channel, in any language:

1. Thank the person and say you will pass it to the person who handles it.
2. Write down what they asked, their name, contact details and the date. For a verbal request, read it back to check you understood.
3. Send it to the data protection lead at [[privacy@yourorganisation.org]] within [[1 working day]].
4. Do not answer the request, send information or delete anything yourself.

People may ask through someone else: a relative, lawyer, interpreter or partner organisation. We act only when we are satisfied that the person has authorised them, or that they have the legal right to act. Parents and guardians can usually make requests for young children. We consider the child's best interests and, as they grow older, their own views.

::: adapt Channels and languages
List the channels people actually use with you, such as a phone line, WhatsApp or a community office. If participants have low literacy, say that staff will write down verbal requests for them. Decide the age at which a child can make their own request. In Scotland, for example, the law presumes that a child aged 12 or over can usually exercise their own rights. Other countries set different ages or leave it to judgement, so check your local law.
:::

# Step-by-step procedure

## 1. Log and acknowledge

The data protection lead opens an entry in the request log (Annex A) on the day they receive it, and sends an acknowledgement within [[3 working days]] using Letter 1 in Annex B.

## 2. Verify identity and clarify

We must not give information to the wrong person, but we must not create unnecessary barriers.

- If we know the person and they write from the email or phone number we hold, that is usually enough.
- If we have real doubt, we ask one or two questions only they could answer, or ask to see an identity document. We record that we saw it and do not keep a copy.

If a request is very broad, we may ask which information or period they mean. We still answer if they decline to narrow it. Under the UK GDPR, as amended in 2025, the deadline starts once we have what we need to confirm identity, and pauses while we wait for a clarification we need. Other laws differ, so check your local guidance.

## 3. Set the deadline

{widths: 45 55}
| Stage | Time limit |
|---|---|
| Pass request to the data protection lead | Within [[1 working day]] of receipt |
| Acknowledge | Within [[3 working days]] |
| Answer | Within [[one month]] of receipt, or less if local law requires |
| Extension for complex or numerous requests | Up to [[two further months]], telling the person why within the first month |
| Stop direct marketing | Within [[5 working days]] |
| Internal review of a refusal | Within [[20 working days]] of the person asking |

::: adapt Deadlines by country
Use the shortest deadline that applies to the people whose data you hold. GDPR and UK GDPR: one month, extendable by two further months. Argentina's Law 25.326: 10 calendar days for access and 5 working days for rectification or deletion. Brazil's LGPD: 15 days for a complete statement of the data held. South Africa's Promotion of Access to Information Act 2000: 30 days for access, with a possible extension. Indonesia's Law 27 of 2022 sets very short limits for many requests, in some cases 3 x 24 hours; check the current rules. For Kenya, Nigeria, India, Peru and other countries, check the regulations made under the main law, because deadlines are often set there.
:::

## 4. Search our systems

The data protection lead coordinates a reasonable and proportionate search of: databases and spreadsheets; the email of everyone who worked with the person; shared drives; messaging apps on work phones; paper files and field registers; photos and recordings; systems run for us by providers, such as payroll or survey tools; and partners who hold information on our behalf.

From the moment a request arrives, nobody may delete, change or hide information because of it. Routine deletion under our Records Retention Schedule continues. In some countries, changing records to avoid disclosing them is a criminal offence.

## 5. Review and redact

Before sending anything, the data protection lead reads it all and removes what the person is not entitled to see. This is called redaction.

- **Other people.** We remove other people's details unless they agree, or disclosure is reasonable without their agreement. Staff names in their work role are usually disclosed.
- **Safety.** We never disclose information that could put anyone at risk, or that would identify someone who raised a safeguarding or whistleblowing concern in confidence.
- **Method.** We redact a copy, never the original, and make sure electronic redaction removes the text rather than covering it.

## 6. Apply any exemptions

Most laws allow information to be withheld in limited cases, such as legal advice, crime prevention, serious harm to someone's health, confidential references, or records we must keep by law. We apply exemptions case by case, record which one we used and why, and take legal advice in complex cases.

::: adapt Exemptions
Exemptions differ widely between countries and are often in separate regulations. List those relevant to your work, for example for social work, research or safeguarding records. If you are unsure, keep the general wording and seek advice when a case arises.
:::

## 7. Act on the request

- **Access.** We send a copy and explain why we use the information, who we share it with, how long we keep it, where it came from, whether it goes abroad, and the person's rights.
- **Correction.** We correct the record and tell anyone we shared the wrong information with.
- **Deletion.** We delete it from every system, including providers'. Backups are put beyond use until overwritten. We keep a minimal note so we do not contact the person again by mistake.
- **Restriction.** We mark the record as not to be used, and tell the person before lifting it.
- **Objection.** We stop unless we have compelling reasons. We always stop direct marketing.
- **Portability.** We send the data in a common format such as a spreadsheet file.
- **Withdrawal of consent.** We stop the use, for example a photo in new materials. Material already printed may remain in circulation.
- **Automated decisions.** A person reviews the decision, hears the person's views and confirms or changes it.

## 8. Respond

The data protection lead replies by the deadline using Letter 2 or 3 in Annex B, in plain language and, where we can, in the person's language. We send information securely: a password-protected file with the password sent separately, a secure link, or by hand. For people who cannot read, we offer to go through it in person.

## 9. Refusals and complaints

We refuse a request, or charge a fee, only where it is manifestly unfounded or excessive: for example, made only to cause disruption, or repeated every week. Every refusal, extension or partial answer tells the person:

- our reasons, unless telling them would cause harm;
- that they can ask [[Name, role]], who was not involved, for an internal review within [[20 working days]];
- that they can complain to [[name of the data protection regulator]] at [[website]], and may be able to go to court.

## 10. Close the request

The data protection lead records the date closed, what was done and any exemptions used, and keeps a copy of what was disclosed.

# Fees

We do not charge for requests. We may charge a reasonable administrative fee only where a request is manifestly unfounded or excessive, or for further copies of the same information.

::: adapt Fees
Under the GDPR, requests are free except in the cases above. South Africa allows prescribed fees for access requests to private bodies under the Promotion of Access to Information Act 2000. Argentina's Law 25.326 makes access free at intervals of six months, unless a legitimate interest is shown. Even where a fee is allowed, many small organisations choose not to charge, because it can discourage people with little money.
:::

# Records

The log, correspondence and a copy of what we disclosed are kept for [[3 years]] after closure, then deleted, unless a complaint or legal claim is open. Only the data protection lead and deputy can see them.
