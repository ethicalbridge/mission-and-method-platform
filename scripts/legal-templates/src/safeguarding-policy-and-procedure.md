# Our commitments

## 1. Our principles

We put the safety, dignity and best interests of children and adults at risk first. Our response is survivor-centred: the survivor's safety, wishes, confidentiality and dignity guide what we do. We aim to do no harm, including through our response. We pay special attention to people who find it harder to speak up. Culture is never an excuse for harm.

## 2. Protection from sexual exploitation, abuse and harassment

[[Organisation name]] follows the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) Six Core Principles relating to Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (2019):

1. Sexual exploitation and abuse are gross misconduct and grounds for dismissal or ending any agreement with us.
2. Sexual activity with anyone under 18 is prohibited, whatever the local age of majority or consent. Mistaken belief about age is no defence.
3. Exchanging money, employment, goods, services or assistance for sex, sexual favours or any other humiliating, degrading or exploitative behaviour is prohibited. This includes assistance due to the people we serve. [[We also ban paying for sex while working for us, even where it is legal.]]
4. Sexual relationships with the people we serve that involve improper use of rank or position are prohibited. They undermine the integrity of our work. Any relationship with a person we serve must be declared to the safeguarding lead.
5. Anyone with a concern or suspicion about sexual exploitation or abuse by anyone connected to us must report it.
6. Everyone must help keep an environment that prevents sexual exploitation and abuse. Managers and board members have a particular duty to build and support the systems that do this.

Sexual harassment of anyone, including colleagues and partners' staff, also breaches this policy. Our Anti-Harassment, Intimidation and Bullying Policy and Procedure explains how we handle it between colleagues.

## 3. Safe activities

Before any activity involving children or adults at risk, the activity lead and safeguarding lead complete a short risk assessment: who takes part, who has contact with them, what information we collect, how participants can raise a concern, and where we can refer someone (Annex B). Minimum rules:

- At least two adults are present, or the activity happens where others can see. Any one-to-one contact is agreed beforehand with the safeguarding lead and recorded.
- No child is left with a volunteer or visitor who has not been through safer recruitment.
- We get informed consent from participants, and from a parent or guardian for children, before activities, photographs or collecting personal data.
- We do not transport participants in private vehicles unless the safeguarding lead agrees and a second adult is present.
- We do not organise visits to residential care for children or short-term volunteering in orphanages.

::: adapt Your activities

If you have no direct contact with children or adults at risk (for example, you only fund or train others), say so, but keep the duty to report and the partner rules in section 7. ## 4. Safer recruitment

For every role, paid or unpaid, with contact with children or adults at risk or access to their personal information, we:

1. state our commitment to safeguarding in the advert and role description, and ask a safeguarding question at interview;
2. ask for a signed self-declaration that the candidate has not been dismissed, convicted, or left a role during an investigation, for sexual exploitation, abuse, harassment or offences against children or adults at risk, and is not barred from such work;
3. take at least two references, one from the most recent employer, and ask referees directly whether the candidate was investigated for, or found to have committed, sexual exploitation, abuse or harassment;
4. verify identity, and qualifications where they matter;
5. request a police or criminal record check where one is available, lawful and reasonably reliable;
6. make sure the person signs this policy and the Code of Conduct and completes induction before starting contact work.

We follow the principles of the Misconduct Disclosure Scheme: when asked for a reference, we share confirmed findings of sexual exploitation, abuse or harassment where the law allows. No check is a guarantee. Supervision and a culture of speaking up matter as much. Our Safer Recruitment Procedure sets out each step, with checklists, a reference request and a self-declaration form.

::: adapt Police checks by country
Decide which roles need a check and from which country. Notes to confirm locally:

- **United Kingdom:** Disclosure and Barring Service (DBS) checks; enhanced checks with barred list checks only for eligible roles.
- **Kenya:** a Police Clearance Certificate (often called a Certificate of Good Conduct) from the Directorate of Criminal Investigations.
- **South Africa:** a police clearance certificate, plus checks of the National Child Protection Register and the National Register for Sex Offenders for people working with children. Check your current duties under the Children's Act 38 of 2005.
- **India and Brazil:** police verification and criminal record certificates are issued by state (and in Brazil also federal) authorities; processes differ.

Where checks are unreliable or unavailable, rely more on references, self-declaration and supervision, and record why. Criminal record data is highly sensitive under most data protection laws: collect only what you need and delete it after the decision.
:::

## 5. Behaviour, online safety and images

Everyone follows our Code of Conduct, which sets out the behaviour we expect. Harm can also happen through phones, messaging apps and social media. We:

- use organisation accounts and devices to contact children or adults at risk. Where a messaging app is needed, the safeguarding lead agrees which one and a second team member is in the group;
- never contact children through private messages or personal social media, or follow them from personal accounts;
- run online sessions with children with two adults, parental consent and moderated chat, and record them only with consent for a clear purpose;
- switch off location tags and never publish anything that reveals where a child, survivor or shelter can be found;
- never put names, stories, images or case details of children, survivors or adults at risk into AI tools or online services we have not approved.

We tell people's stories only with informed consent, using our Photo and Story Consent Form, and never in a way that could identify a child at risk or endanger anyone. Report online harm, including grooming, sextortion (threats to share sexual images unless the person pays or does something) and the sharing of intimate images, like any other concern.

## 6. Training

Everyone completes safeguarding induction within [[their first two weeks]], before unsupervised contact work, and a short refresher every year. The safeguarding lead and deputy complete recognised training and refresh it every [[2 years]]. We tell the people we serve, in their language and an accessible format, what behaviour to expect from us, that our help is free, and how to raise a concern.

## 7. Partners, contractors and suppliers

Our standards apply to everyone working on our projects. We check partners' safeguarding arrangements under our Partner Due Diligence and Sanctions Screening Policy. Our agreements require partners, sub-grantees and contractors to:

1. follow this policy, or their own if it is at least as strong, and the IASC six core principles;
2. name a safeguarding focal point and use safer recruitment for our projects;
3. tell us of any safeguarding concern connected to our work within [[24 hours / 48 hours]], protecting the survivor's identity;
4. cooperate with any review, and accept that we may end the agreement for a breach;
5. pass the same requirements to anyone they subcontract.

::: adapt Flow-down to partners
Match the requirement to the partner. A community group running a one-off event may need only a signed code of conduct and a briefing. A sub-grantee working with children needs a full policy, a focal point and reporting duties written into the Sub-grant Agreement. Check your funders' terms: many institutional funders require safeguarding clauses to be passed down to every downstream partner, sometimes with fixed reporting deadlines.
:::

# How to respond and report

## 8. If someone is in immediate danger

Contact emergency services on [[local emergency number]], or take the person to [[nearest health facility]], if it is safe for you and for them. Then tell the safeguarding lead within [[2 hours]].

## 9. If someone tells you about harm

- **Listen** calmly. Let them speak in their own way and at their own pace.
- **Reassure** them that they were right to tell you and that it is not their fault. Check they are safe now.
- **Be honest** about what happens next. Do not promise secrecy: say you will share only what is needed to help.
- **Do not investigate.** Do not ask leading questions ("Did he touch you?"), examine injuries, look for evidence or confront anyone.
- **Record** as soon as possible what they said, in their own words, with the date, time and place. Facts, not opinions.
- **Report** to the safeguarding lead within [[24 hours]]. Do not discuss it with anyone else.

## 10. How to report

Report any concern, suspicion or disclosure about anyone connected to our work within [[24 hours]]. You do not need proof. Use the form in Annex A, or report by phone or in person.

## 12. What happens after a report

The safeguarding lead, or the deputy if the lead is involved:

1. **Acknowledges** the report and gives a reference number.
2. **Assesses risk:** is anyone in danger, is a child involved, does the law require a report? They seek specialist advice where unsure.
3. **Protects** people straight away where needed: for example moving the person concerned away from contact work, suspending them, or pausing an activity. This is a precaution, not a finding.
4. **Talks to the survivor,** where safe, about their options and wishes, and offers referral to support (Annex B).
5. **Refers** to child protection services, the police or others where the law requires it, a child is at risk, or an adult survivor agrees (section 13).
6. **Looks into it.** We never investigate crimes ourselves. For serious or complex internal cases we appoint a trained external investigator. If the authorities investigate, we cooperate and pause our own work if asked.
7. **Decides** on the balance of probabilities (whether the concern is more likely true than not) and acts under our Disciplinary and Grievance Procedure or agreements.
8. **Closes and learns:** tells the survivor and reporter as much as confidentiality allows, records the outcome and what we will change.

The person concerned is told the substance of the allegation when it is safe, and can respond. Nobody is presumed to have done wrong.

## 13. Putting the survivor first

- **Safety.** Before any referral, we consider how the person who caused harm, the family or the community might react.
- **Adults choose.** We explain the options and respect their decisions. We do not report an adult survivor's experience to the police or community leaders without their consent, unless the law requires it, a child or another person is at serious risk, or their life is in danger.
- **Children are protected.** Where a child may be at risk of significant harm, we act even if they or their family disagree, and follow any mandatory reporting duty. We explain to the child what will happen, in a way they understand.
- **If we act without consent,** we tell the survivor why and what we will share, and share only what is needed.
- **Telling it once.** Only the safeguarding lead, or their appointee, takes a full account.
- **Practical support,** such as safe transport or interpretation.

Referral can put adult survivors at risk. In some places, reporting gender-based violence (GBV) to the police can lead to retaliation, stigma, forced marriage, or prosecution of the survivor, for example for same-sex relationships, sex work, immigration status or sex outside marriage. Where this risk exists, we focus on confidential health, psychosocial and legal support chosen by the survivor.

::: adapt Mandatory reporting
Find out what the law requires where you work and replace this box with two or three clear sentences. Examples to confirm locally:

- **India:** the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act 2012 requires anyone who knows of, or suspects, a sexual offence against a child to report it to the police or the Special Juvenile Police Unit. Failing to report is an offence.
- **South Africa:** the Children's Act 38 of 2005 (section 110) requires many professionals, including teachers, health and social workers, to report suspected abuse or deliberate neglect of a child. Sexual offences legislation also requires anyone who knows of a sexual offence against a child to report it to the police.
- **Brazil:** the Statute of the Child and Adolescent (ECA, Law 8.069 of 1990) requires suspected maltreatment of a child to be reported to the Conselho Tutelar.
- **Elsewhere:** check the children's law, the penal code and any gender-based violence law. Some duties apply only to certain professions.

Decide who makes the report: usually the safeguarding lead, so that individual team members do not carry it alone. If the law places a personal duty on certain staff, tell them.
:::

# Records, confidentiality and data

The safeguarding lead keeps a confidential record of every concern, including those that lead to no action: reference, date and reporter (or "anonymous"); what was reported, in the reporter's words; people involved; protective actions; the survivor's wishes; referrals made, or why not; decisions, reasons and any review; and the outcome.

Records are kept in [[an encrypted folder / a locked cabinet]], with access limited to the safeguarding lead and deputy, separately from personnel files. Records about the lead are kept by the deputy. We share information only on a need-to-know basis. Data protection laws generally allow sharing information to protect someone from serious harm: they are never a reason not to act.

We keep case records for [[6 years]] after the case closes, or longer if legal proceedings are ongoing or expected. Cases involving a child are kept until the child turns [[25]] or for [[6 years]] after closure, whichever is later. We then delete them securely and log the deletion.

::: adapt Retention and data protection
Check your local data protection law and add these periods to your Records Retention Schedule. Safeguarding records often hold sensitive data (health, sex life, criminal allegations). The GDPR is a widely used benchmark; local laws include the Kenya Data Protection Act 2019, the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023, South Africa's POPIA, Brazil's LGPD and India's DPDP Act 2023. Never keep safeguarding records on personal devices or general shared drives.
:::

# If this policy is breached

A breach of this policy, including failing to report a concern, is serious. Depending on the case, we may give a warning; suspend someone while we look into it; dismiss an employee or end a role, contract or partnership, following our Disciplinary and Grievance Procedure and the law; refer the matter to the authorities; and share confirmed findings with future employers who ask, where the law allows.

Retaliation against anyone who reports or helps with a review is a separate, serious breach. So is knowingly making a false report: we decide this only after a review, never just because a concern was not upheld. Anyone affected by a decision can ask for a review within [[30 days]] by writing to [[Name, role]], who was not involved in the original decision.

# Monitoring and review

The safeguarding lead reviews open cases every [[week / two weeks]] and gives the board an anonymised summary every [[6 / 12]] months: number and type of concerns, time taken, outcomes and lessons. We review this policy every [[12 months]], after any serious incident, and when the law or our activities change.

This policy follows the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989), the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006), the IASC Six Core Principles (2019), the UN Secretary-General's Bulletin on protection from sexual exploitation and sexual abuse (ST/SGB/2003/13), the Core Humanitarian Standard on Quality and Accountability and the Keeping Children Safe standards.
