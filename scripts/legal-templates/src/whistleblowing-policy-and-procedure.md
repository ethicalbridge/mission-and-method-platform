# Who this policy applies to

Anyone can raise a concern under this policy, and it protects:

- staff, including former staff, people on probation and job applicants;
- volunteers, interns, board members [[trustees / directors / committee members]] and advisers;
- contractors, consultants and suppliers, and their staff;
- partners and sub-grantees, and their staff;
- the people and communities we serve, and members of the public.

It also protects people who help a reporter (such as a colleague who confirms what they saw) and people close to the reporter who could be affected, such as family members who also work with us.

It covers wrongdoing connected to [[Organisation name]], our projects, our partners' work on our projects, and anyone acting for us, wherever it happens.

**Law.** [[Organisation name]] is registered in [[country]] and follows its laws. When we work in other countries, we also follow the law that applies there. Legal protection for whistleblowers depends on where you are and your relationship with us. This policy protects you within [[Organisation name]] regardless, and where it and the law differ, we follow the rule that gives you more protection.

::: adapt Law and countries
Check whether your country has a whistleblower protection law, who it covers (often only employees), and what it requires. Country notes to confirm locally:

- **European Union:** Directive (EU) 2019/1937 requires organisations with 50 or more workers to have internal reporting channels, acknowledge reports within 7 days and give feedback within 3 months. National laws implementing it are often broader. The timescales in this template are within these limits; do not set an acknowledgement time longer than 7 days.
- **United Kingdom:** the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 protects workers who make a disclosure they reasonably believe is in the public interest. "Good faith" is no longer a condition of protection.
- **South Africa:** the Protected Disclosures Act 26 of 2000 protects employees and workers who disclose wrongdoing.
- **Ghana:** the Whistleblower Act 2006 (Act 720) protects people who disclose wrongdoing to listed bodies.
- **United States:** non-profits filing IRS Form 990 are asked whether they have a written whistleblower policy, and some federal anti-retaliation rules apply to every organisation.
- **Kenya, Nigeria and many other countries:** check whether a dedicated whistleblower law exists. Protection may come instead from anti-corruption, labour or witness protection laws, or from government whistleblowing schemes with limited scope.

If no law protects whistleblowers where you work, this policy becomes the main protection, so the internal and independent routes matter even more.
:::

# Policy

## 1. Our commitment

We can only be trusted if problems are found and fixed early, including problems with how we ourselves are run. We commit to:

1. clear, confidential channels, including an anonymous one and one that does not go to the director;
2. assessing every concern fairly and on time, and investigating where needed;
3. protecting the identity of reporters and of the people named;
4. acting firmly on any retaliation;
5. telling the reporter what we have done, as far as we can;
6. learning from every concern.

## 2. What you can raise

You can raise any wrongdoing connected to our work that has happened, is happening or is likely to happen, where it affects others or the public interest. For example:

- a crime, such as fraud, theft, bribery, money laundering or forgery;
- misuse of funds, grants, equipment or donations, including using restricted funds for another purpose;
- a breach of a legal or regulatory duty, including tax, employment, data protection or sanctions rules;
- a danger to someone's health, safety or wellbeing;
- a safeguarding concern that is being ignored, mishandled or covered up;
- misleading funders, partners or the public, for example by inventing results, inflating participant numbers or hiding a problem from an auditor;
- a hidden data breach, or someone accessing personal information without reason;
- decisions on jobs, contracts or grants influenced by bribes, favours or undeclared personal links;
- serious damage to the environment;
- retaliation against someone who raised a concern;
- any attempt to hide or destroy evidence of the above.

## 3. What this policy is not for

Whistleblowing is about wrongdoing that affects others. Some concerns are better handled through other routes:

{widths: 34 66}
| If your concern is about | Use |
|---|---|
| Your own pay, contract, workload, performance review or a disagreement with your manager | Your grievance procedure, or a conversation with [[role]] |
| How a colleague treats you (harassment, bullying, discrimination) | Anti-Harassment, Intimidation and Bullying Policy and Procedure |
| Harm or risk to a child, adult at risk or person we serve | Safeguarding Policy and Procedure |
| Fraud, bribery or corruption | Fraud, Bribery and Corruption Policy and Procedure, or this policy |
| A service we provided, from someone we serve or a member of the public | Complaints and Feedback Procedure |

A personal grievance can become a whistleblowing concern when it shows a wider problem: for example, if many staff are being paid below the legal minimum, or if your manager punished you for reporting fraud. If you are unsure, raise it under this policy anyway. You are still protected, and we will tell you which route applies.

## 4. Honestly and with reasonable belief

You should raise a concern honestly and with a reasonable belief that the information shows wrongdoing. You do not need proof, and you are protected even if you turn out to be mistaken. Your reasons for reporting do not matter: we look at the information, not the motive. Do not investigate yourself, and do not break rules or the law to gather evidence. Knowingly making a false report is misconduct, and we decide this only after a review, never just because a concern was not upheld.

## 5. Protection from retaliation

Retaliation means treating someone worse because they raised a concern or helped with one. Examples: dismissal, demotion, cutting hours or shifts, ending a contract or partnership, refusing a reference or giving a poor one, excluding someone from meetings or training, harassment, threats, revealing their identity, or pressure through family or community members.

Anyone who retaliates, or threatens to, faces disciplinary action up to dismissal or the end of their agreement. If something harmful happens to you after you raise a concern, we will treat it as retaliation unless there is a clear, separate and legitimate reason. We will reverse its effects where we can, for example by reinstating a contract or correcting a record. Raising a concern does not protect you from action for your own wrongdoing, but we will take into account that you came forward.

## 6. Confidentiality and anonymity

**Open:** you share your name and are happy for it to be known. **Confidential:** you share your name with the person handling the concern, and we protect it. **Anonymous:** you do not tell us who you are.

We share your identity, or anything that could identify you, only with people who need it to handle your concern, unless you agree. If the law requires us to disclose it, we tell you first, unless that would harm an investigation or legal proceedings. In a small team, others may guess who raised a concern: we will discuss this with you before we act. We also protect the identity of people named in a concern.

We follow up anonymous concerns as far as we can. Without a way to contact you, we may be unable to ask questions or give feedback, so consider using an anonymous email address that lets us reply.

::: adapt Your anonymous channel
Choose at least one route that does not reveal who reports. Options for a small organisation: a dedicated email address read only by the independent contact (reporters can write from an anonymous account); a simple online form that does not record names or IP addresses; a locked feedback box at project sites; or a phone number held by the independent contact. Larger organisations, or those with significant institutional funding, can buy an external hotline service that offers anonymous two-way messaging. Whatever you choose, test it, and make sure nobody who could be named in a concern can read it.
:::

# Procedure

## 7. How to raise a concern

{widths: 30 36 34}
| Channel | Use it for | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Whistleblowing officer | Most concerns | [[Name]], [[speakup@yourorganisation.org]], [[phone]] |
| Independent contact | Concerns about the whistleblowing officer, the director, or anything they are involved in | [[Name]], [[email]], [[phone]] |
| Board chair | Concerns about the board | [[Name]], [[email]] |
| Anonymous | Any concern | [[Anonymous form / email / feedback box]] |
| In person or by call | If you prefer to talk | Ask through any channel. We arrange it within [[7 days]]. |

You can write or speak in [[languages]]. If you talk to us, we write down what you say and offer you the chance to check it. Tell us what happened, when and where, who was involved, how you know, and whether you have reported it elsewhere. Share evidence you already have.

## 8. When the concern is about the director or the board

The director usually has access to every system and mailbox. So concerns about the director, or anything the director is involved in, go directly to the independent contact, outside the organisation's shared systems. The independent contact:

- assesses the concern, decides next steps and gives feedback to the reporter;
- can ask for any records needed, which the director must provide without delay;
- can appoint an external investigator or adviser, paid for by [[Organisation name]];
- keeps case records separately from the organisation's systems;
- reports the outcome to the board, without identifying the reporter.

The director does not see these records and takes no part in the decision. Concerns about the board chair go to [[another board member / the independent contact]].

## 9. Raising concerns outside the organisation

We encourage you to come to us first so that we can act quickly, but you do not have to. You can also go to:

- a regulator or public authority responsible for the issue, such as [[NGO regulator]], the tax authority, or the data protection authority for a data breach;
- the funder of the project concerned. Many funders run their own channels for reporting fraud, corruption or sexual exploitation in projects they fund;
- the police, especially if someone is in danger or a crime has been committed;
- a lawyer or trade union, for advice.

Going public, for example to the media or on social media, is protected by law only in limited circumstances, which vary by country. Seek advice first, and never share personal information about the people we serve. If someone is in immediate danger, contact emergency services first.

::: adapt External routes
List the bodies people can go to in your country, with contact details: your NGO or charity regulator, the anti-corruption commission, the tax authority, the data protection authority, the labour inspectorate and the police. Then add each major funder's reporting channel. Examples: the Global Fund's Office of the Inspector General, the World Bank's Integrity Vice Presidency for Bank-financed projects, the European Anti-Fraud Office (OLAF) for EU-funded projects, and the oversight or investigation offices of UN agencies. Many government donors publish an address for reporting fraud and safeguarding concerns: check your grant agreements. Do not list a route you have not checked.
:::

## 10. How we assess a concern

The whistleblowing officer, or the independent contact for concerns about the director, assesses each concern within [[14 days]] of acknowledging it:

1. **Is anyone at risk?** If so, we act straight away: for example suspending someone's access to funds or systems, pausing an activity, or protecting evidence.
2. **Is it within this policy?** If it belongs under another policy, we tell the reporter and pass it on with their agreement, keeping their protection.
3. **Is there enough information?** If not, we ask the reporter, where we can.
4. **Must we tell anyone?** We check whether the law, our funders or our insurers require us to report it, and to whom.
5. **What response is proportionate?** Options: no further action (with reasons), an internal review, a full investigation, an external investigation, or referral to the authorities.

## 11. How we investigate

- The investigator is independent of the matter and of the people involved. For serious or complex concerns, concerns about senior people, or where specialist skills are needed (for example forensic accounting), we appoint an external investigator.
- We agree clear terms of reference: the questions to answer, the scope, the timeline and who receives the report.
- People named in a concern are told of the allegation when this will not put evidence or anyone at risk, and have a fair chance to respond.
- Interviews are held separately, notes are kept, and each person can check theirs.
- The investigator decides on the balance of probabilities (whether something is more likely than not to have happened) and gives reasons.
- If the police or another authority investigates, we cooperate and pause our own investigation if asked.

::: adapt Investigations and legal advice
Decide in advance who you would call for a serious investigation: a local law firm, an audit firm, or a peer organisation with investigation experience. Ask your main funders whether they must approve the investigator or see the terms of reference: some do. For possible crimes or large losses, take legal advice early. In some countries, communications with a lawyer can be protected from disclosure, but the rules differ widely: check before you rely on this.
:::

## 12. Timescales

{widths: 58 42}
| Step | Time limit |
|---|---|
| Acknowledge the concern, with a reference number | [[2 working days]]; same day if someone may be at risk |
| Protective action, where needed | Straight away |
| Assessment and decision on next steps | [[14 days]] from acknowledgement |
| Report to authorities, funders or insurers where required | Within the deadline set by law or agreement |
| First feedback to the reporter | [[3 months]] from acknowledgement |
| Further updates until the case closes | Every [[3 months]] |
| Investigation completed | Target [[60 / 90]] days, depending on complexity |

## 13. Feedback to the reporter

We tell you what we have done or plan to do, and why, as far as confidentiality, the law and the rights of others allow. We may not be able to share the details of an investigation or of disciplinary action. If we decide to take no further action, or that the concern belongs under another process, we tell you our decision and reasons in writing. If you are not satisfied, you can ask the independent contact to review how your concern was handled.

## 14. Outcomes and closing

Depending on the findings, outcomes may include: changes to our controls or procedures; recovering lost funds; disciplinary action; ending a contract or partnership; reporting to the police, regulators or funders; or no action, where the concern is not upheld. We record lessons, close the case, and tell the reporter where appropriate.

# Support for reporters

Speaking up can be stressful. The person handling your concern will:

- keep you updated at least every [[3 months]], and sooner when something changes;
- agree practical steps with you to prevent retaliation, such as a change of reporting line, working from home or not attending certain meetings;
- point you to support: [[employee assistance programme / counselling service / legal aid / national whistleblower advice service]].

| **Whistleblowing officer** | Receives and assesses concerns, arranges investigations, gives feedback, keeps the register and reports to the board. |

# Records, confidentiality and data

The person handling a concern keeps a secure register (see the annex): reference, date received, acknowledgement, assessment, actions, feedback given, outcome and lessons. Anonymous concerns are recorded too. Records are kept in [[a restricted, encrypted folder]]. Records of concerns about the director are kept by the independent contact, separately.

We keep case records for [[6 years]] after the case closes, or longer if legal proceedings are ongoing or expected. Cases involving a child are kept until the child turns [[25]] or for [[6 years]] after closure, whichever is later. We delete personal data that is not relevant to the concern straight away. Personal data is handled under our Data Protection Policy. Some data rights, such as a named person's right to see information about them, may be limited to protect a reporter's identity or an investigation, where the law allows.

::: adapt Records and data rights
Check how your data protection law treats whistleblowing records. Under the GDPR and many similar laws (for example South Africa's POPIA, Brazil's LGPD and the Kenya Data Protection Act 2019), people named in a report usually have a right to access information about them, but exemptions may protect a reporter's identity or an ongoing investigation. Check the exact exemptions in your law before you refuse a request. Add your retention period to your Records Retention Schedule.
:::

# If this policy is breached

Retaliating against a reporter, revealing a reporter's identity without a lawful reason, discouraging someone from speaking up, destroying evidence or knowingly making a false report are all breaches of this policy. They can lead to disciplinary action up to dismissal or the end of an agreement and, where required, referral to the authorities.

# Monitoring and review

Every [[12 months]], the whistleblowing officer gives the board an anonymised summary: number and type of concerns, channels used, time taken, outcomes, any retaliation, and lessons. We check each year that every channel works and that our team and partners know about it. We review this policy every [[12 months]], after any serious concern, and when the law changes.

This policy follows international good practice, including the principles in the EU Whistleblowing Directive, ISO 37002 (whistleblowing management systems) and the UN Convention against Corruption.
