::: adapt Before you use this guide and model
**Part 1** is a guide for the founders: use it to choose your legal form, then delete it. **Part 2** is a model constitution for a small non-profit **association** or **company limited by guarantee** with members and an elected board. You adapt it, adopt it at a founding meeting and file it with your registrar.

Do not use the model as it stands if your registrar insists on its own form; if you are setting up a trust, foundation, co-operative or company limited by shares (many clauses can still be reused); or if your tax authority prescribes exact wording for tax-exempt status.

The main risks are a document the registrar rejects, clauses that local law makes void, purposes too narrow for your work, and a missing dissolution clause, which can cost you tax-exempt status. Have a lawyer in your country check the final text against the Legal Review Checklist.
:::

::: glance
- A governing document is the founding legal rulebook of an organisation. Its name depends on your legal form and country.
- Choose your legal form first. It decides what the document must contain, who registers it and how much personal risk founders carry.
- Every governing document covers the same core: name, purposes, powers, members, meetings, the board, money, changes and dissolution.
- In the model, members elect the board. The board governs and delegates day-to-day management.
- Income and assets are used only for the purposes, never shared among members or board members. On closure they go to a similar organisation.
- Board members are unpaid unless the constitution and the law allow payment. Conflicts are declared and managed.
- Local law and the registrar's requirements always come first.
:::

# Part 1: A guide to governing documents

## 1. What a governing document is

A governing document is the legal text that creates an organisation and sets its basic rules. It answers five questions:

1. **Why do we exist?** Our purposes, also called objects.
2. **Who controls us?** Members, founders or trustees.
3. **Who decides, and how?** The general meeting, the board, quorum and voting.
4. **How is money handled?** Powers, the ban on distributing profits, accounts and audit.
5. **How can we change or close?** Amendments, dissolution and where remaining assets go.

Registrars check it before they register you. Tax authorities read it to decide on exemption. Banks use it to see who may sign. Funders read it to check that you are independent and that their money cannot end up in private hands. If the board acts outside it, the decision may be invalid and board members may be personally liable.

## 2. The names you will meet

The same kind of document has many names. What matters is its function.

{widths: 28 72}
| Name | Where you will meet it |
|---|---|
| Constitution | Associations, societies and community groups; also charitable incorporated organisations (CIOs) in England and Wales. |
| Articles of association | The internal rules of a company: members, directors, meetings. Used by non-profit companies limited by guarantee in many countries. |
| Memorandum | A founding statement, sometimes with the objects. India's Section 8 companies have a memorandum and articles; registered societies file a memorandum with rules. South African companies have one Memorandum of Incorporation (MOI). |
| Statutes (estatuto, statuts, anggaran dasar) | Associations and foundations in civil-law countries: Latin America, francophone and lusophone Africa, Indonesia. Often adopted at a founding assembly, sometimes as a notarial deed. |
| By-laws | In the US, the internal rules a non-profit corporation adopts alongside the articles filed with the state. Elsewhere, secondary rules made under a constitution (in Indonesia, anggaran rumah tangga). |
| Trust deed | Creates a trust: a settlor gives property to trustees to hold for stated purposes. Usually no members. |
| Rules | The registered rules of a co-operative, mutual society, credit union or UK community benefit society. |

## 3. Primary and secondary rules

Keep the governing document to the basic rules. Put practical detail in secondary documents that the board can update without a members' vote: the Governing Body Terms of Reference, the Conflict of Interest Policy, the Delegation of Authority Matrix, the Financial Controls Policy and the Reserves Policy. The order of priority is always the law, then the governing document, then secondary rules and policies. A lower document never overrides a higher one.

# Choosing your legal form

## 4. Questions to answer first

1. **Do we need limited liability?** If you will sign leases, employ staff or receive grants, founders should not carry the debts personally.
2. **Do we want a wide membership** that elects the board, or a small board only?
3. **Will we trade,** and might we ever want investment?
4. **Do we need charitable, tax-exempt or public-benefit status?**
5. **What will our funders and government partners accept?**
6. **What can we manage?** Every form brings filings, accounts and meetings.

## 5. Common legal forms

The table gives the usual picture. Details vary, so check your local law.

{widths: 19 31 22 28}
| Legal form | What the governing document usually needs | Liability | Who registers it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unincorporated association or community group | Constitution: name, purposes, members, committee, meetings, money, dissolution | Often no separate legal personality: committee members may be personally liable | Often no registration needed to exist; may register with a registrar of societies or local authority to bank or receive funds |
| Registered society or incorporated association | Statute or memorandum and rules, plus the general assembly powers, audit body and amendment rules the law requires | Separate legal entity; members not usually liable for its debts | Registrar of societies, civil registry of legal entities, or a ministry or provincial authority |
| Non-profit company limited by guarantee | Articles (sometimes a memorandum or MOI): purposes, non-distribution, members' guarantee, directors, meetings, dissolution | Separate entity; members liable only up to a small guarantee | Companies registrar |
| Foundation | Statute or deed: initial assets, purposes, board, often a supervisory body; usually no members | Separate entity; liability limited to its assets | Civil registry, ministry or court; often supervised by a public authority |
| Trust (public or charitable) | Trust deed: settlor, trustees, purposes, powers, appointment and removal of trustees, winding up | Often not a separate entity: trustees hold the assets and may be personally liable | Trust registrar, court officer or charity regulator |
| Co-operative | Registered rules: common bond, member shares, one member one vote, use of surplus, reserves | Separate entity; usually limited to members' shares | Co-operative registrar or authority |
| Social enterprise company (limited by shares) | Articles with a mission clause; optionally a dividend cap or asset lock | Limited to unpaid share capital | Companies registrar; usually not tax-exempt, but can raise investment |
| Community interest company (UK) | Articles with a community interest statement, statutory asset lock and, if it has shares, a dividend cap | Limited | Companies House, with the CIC Regulator |
| Charitable incorporated organisation (England and Wales; SCIO in Scotland) | Constitution in the regulator's form: foundation model (trustees only) or association model (wider membership) | Limited; separate entity | Charity Commission for England and Wales, or OSCR in Scotland |

::: adapt Where to register: Africa and Asia
Rules change often. Check the current law and your registrar's checklist before you choose.

- **Kenya:** non-governmental organisations now register as public benefit organisations under the Public Benefit Organizations Act 2013, in force since 2024 and administered by the Public Benefit Organizations Regulatory Authority. Alternatives are a society under the Societies Act, a company limited by guarantee under the Companies Act 2015, or a trust.
- **Nigeria:** most non-profits register as incorporated trustees under Part F of the Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020, or as companies limited by guarantee, both with the Corporate Affairs Commission. Check its current document requirements.
- **South Africa:** a non-profit company files a Memorandum of Incorporation with the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission under the Companies Act 2008. A voluntary association is created by its constitution and can have its own legal personality if the constitution provides for it. Trusts register with the Master of the High Court under the Trust Property Control Act 1988. Any of these may register under the Nonprofit Organisations Act 1997, and may apply to SARS for public benefit organisation status, which requires specific constitution clauses.
- **India:** a public charitable trust (trust deed, registered with the sub-registrar or under a state public trusts law where one applies); a society under the Societies Registration Act 1860 or a state version; or a Section 8 company under the Companies Act 2013. Tax registration and the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act 2010 set further conditions. India's income tax law was rewritten with effect from 2026, so check the current provisions for charitable registration.
- **Indonesia:** a foundation (yayasan) under Law 16 of 2001 as amended by Law 28 of 2004, or an association (perkumpulan), each created by notarial deed in Indonesian and approved through the ministry responsible for legal entities. A yayasan must have three organs (pembina, pengurus, pengawas) and may not distribute assets to them. Check also the law on mass organisations (ormas).
:::

::: adapt Where to register: Latin America, the UK and the US
- **Brazil:** an association or foundation under the Civil Code (Law 10.406 of 2002). The statute (estatuto social) is approved at a founding assembly and registered at the Civil Registry of Legal Entities, usually with a lawyer's endorsement, then for a CNPJ number. The Civil Code lists what an association's statute must contain (check article 54). Foundations are supervised by the Public Prosecutor's Office. Public titles such as OSCIP (Law 9.790 of 1999) and the rules on partnerships with government (Law 13.019 of 2014) add statute requirements.
- **Argentina:** a civil association or foundation under the Civil and Commercial Code in force since 2015, with foundations also under Law 19.836. Register with your jurisdiction's authority: the Inspección General de Justicia in the City of Buenos Aires, or the provincial authority for legal entities. Civil associations need a governing commission, an assembly and an audit body. Tax exemption is requested from ARCA, which replaced AFIP.
- **United Kingdom:** the Charity Commission for England and Wales publishes model governing documents for CIOs, charitable companies, unincorporated associations and trusts. Use them if you plan to register as a charity. Charities above an income threshold must register (check the current figure), and CIOs always register. Companies register with Companies House under the Companies Act 2006. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own charity regulators.
- **United States:** a non-profit corporation files short articles of incorporation with the Secretary of State of its chosen state, then adopts by-laws, which are not usually filed. For section 501(c)(3) exemption, the IRS requires the articles to limit purposes to exempt purposes and dedicate assets to exempt purposes on dissolution; IRS Publication 557 gives sample wording. Apply on Form 1023 or, if eligible, Form 1023-EZ. In the US, put clauses 3, 5 and 36 of the model in the articles and the rest in the by-laws.
:::

# What a good governing document covers

## 6. The essential clauses

Whatever your form, check that your document covers each point below.

{widths: 30 48 22}
| Clause | Why it matters | Model clause |
|---|---|---|
| Name and legal form | Tells others what kind of body you are | 1–2 |
| Purposes | Defines and limits what you may do; read first by registrars and funders | 3 |
| Powers | Lets you bank, employ, contract and borrow | 4 |
| Non-distribution | Shows no one profits privately; essential for tax-exempt status | 5 |
| Members | Who controls you, how people join and leave | 6–9 |
| General meetings | Electing the board, approving accounts, changing the rules | 10–15 |
| The board | Size, appointment, terms, officers, removal, conflicts, payment | 16–22 |
| Board decisions | Meetings, quorum, written resolutions, online meetings | 23–26 |
| Delegation | Sharing work without losing control | 27–28 |
| Finance and accounts | Accountability to members, regulators and funders | 29–33 |
| Amendments and dissolution | How the rules change, and where assets go if you close | 34–36 |

Common problems to avoid: constitutions borrowed from another country; purposes that are too narrow or too vague; a founder written in as permanent board member with a veto; a quorum you cannot reach; no rules for online meetings or email decisions; and assets that return to the founders on closure.

# How to adapt, adopt and register

## 7. Steps

1. **Choose your legal form** and get your registrar's checklist and any model it requires.
2. **Make the key choices** as a founding group: name, purposes, membership, board size and terms, quorum, payment of board members, online meetings, and who receives your assets if you close.
3. **Adapt the model.** Fill every [[placeholder]], delete unused options and all ADAPT THIS boxes, and renumber.
4. **Check it** with the Legal Review Checklist and a lawyer in your country, and a tax adviser if you will seek tax-exempt status.
5. **Hold a founding meeting** to adopt the constitution, appoint the first board and officers, and sign (agenda in Annex A).
6. **Register,** then register for tax, open a bank account and apply for any tax-exempt status.
7. **Adopt your secondary documents** at the first board meeting: Governing Body Terms of Reference, Conflict of Interest Policy, Board Interest Declaration and Delegation of Authority Matrix.
8. **Keep it safe.** Keep the signed original, every amendment and the certificate permanently, as your Records Retention Schedule sets out.

If you already have a governing document, compare it with section 6. Change it only by the procedure it sets out, and tell your registrar.

# Part 2: Model constitution

**Constitution of [[Organisation name]]**

Adopted on [[date]] at [[place]] by [[the founding members / special resolution of the general meeting]]. Last amended on [[date]]. Registered with [[registrar]] under number [[number]].

## 1. Name

The name of the organisation is **[[Organisation name]]** ("the organisation"). It may also use the short name [[short name]]. The name may be changed by special resolution, with the registrar's approval where the law requires it.

## 2. Legal form and registered office

1. The organisation is a [[non-profit association / company limited by guarantee / non-profit company / registered society]] under the law of [[country]], in particular [[name of law]].
2. Its registered office is in [[town or region, country]]. The board may move it within [[country]] and will tell the registrar within [[number]] days.
3. The organisation is independent of any government, political party or religious body.

## 3. Purposes

1. The purposes of the organisation are, [[for the public benefit / exclusively for charitable purposes]]:
  1. [[to advance the education of young people in X, in particular by ...]];
  2. [[to relieve poverty and hardship among ... in ..., in particular by ...]];
  3. [[to promote the health and well-being of ... by ...]].
2. The organisation pursues its purposes in [[country / region / any country]].
3. The organisation does not support or oppose any political party or candidate for public office.

::: adapt Writing your purposes
Purposes say what you exist to achieve and for whom, not every activity. Write two to four. Each should name the benefit, the people who benefit and, if useful, the area. "In particular by" gives examples of activities without limiting you.

- **General wording:** "The purposes of the organisation are, for the public benefit, ..." suits most associations and non-profit companies.
- **England and Wales:** charitable purposes must fall within the descriptions in the Charities Act 2011 (for example relief of poverty, advancement of education, health, community development or human rights) and be for the public benefit. Copy wording from the Charity Commission's models where you can.
- **United States:** for section 501(c)(3), use wording such as "organised exclusively for charitable and educational purposes within the meaning of section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code, or the corresponding section of any future tax code". See IRS Publication 557.
- **South Africa:** for public benefit organisation status, your activities must fall within the list of public benefit activities issued under the Income Tax Act. Check the current list with SARS.
- **Elsewhere:** many tax and registration laws list approved purposes. Use their words, and check your local law.

Avoid purposes that serve a closed group, and anything a registrar might read as political campaigning unless your law allows it.
:::

## 4. Powers

To pursue its purposes, and for no other reason, the organisation may:

1. raise funds and receive grants, donations, legacies and contracts;
2. buy, rent, hold and sell property and equipment;
3. borrow money and give security, with board approval and within any limits the law sets;
4. employ staff and engage volunteers, contractors and advisers;
5. work with, fund, join or set up organisations with similar purposes;
6. open and operate bank and mobile money accounts;
7. invest money not immediately needed, in line with the Reserves Policy;
8. insure its property and people, including under clause 33;
9. do anything else lawful that helps it pursue the purposes.

## 5. Non-distribution of income and assets

1. The income and property of the organisation are used only to pursue its purposes.
2. No part of them may be paid or transferred, directly or indirectly, to any member or board member by way of dividend, bonus or profit share.
3. This does not prevent payment in good faith of:
  1. reasonable pay to a member who is an employee and not a board member;
  2. reasonable payment for goods or services, or reasonable interest or rent, approved by the board and, where a board member benefits, under clause 22;
  3. reasonable out-of-pocket expenses;
  4. support to a member as one of the people the organisation serves, on the same terms as others.
4. On dissolution, remaining assets are dealt with under clause 36.

::: adapt Non-distribution and asset lock
This clause makes you a non-profit in the eyes of funders and tax authorities. Keep it strong; clause 34 protects it. In the UK, a community interest company has a statutory asset lock set by its articles and the CIC Regulator's rules. In Indonesia, the foundations law forbids distributing a yayasan's assets to its founders and organs. In the US, no part of a 501(c)(3)'s earnings may benefit insiders, and excess benefit rules apply to unreasonable pay. Elsewhere, check your local law on non-distribution and related-party payments. A social enterprise company limited by shares needs a dividend cap and mission lock drafted with a lawyer instead.
:::

# Members

## 6. Membership

1. The organisation has [[one class of member / full members and associate members]].
2. The founding members are the people who signed this constitution.
3. Membership is open to any [[individual aged 18 or over / individual or organisation]] who supports the purposes, applies in the form the board sets and pays any fee.
4. **Full members** may attend and vote at general meetings and stand for the board. [[Associate members may attend and speak, but not vote.]]
5. An organisation that is a member names one person to represent it.
6. Membership cannot be transferred.

## 7. Admission and register of members

1. The board decides each application within [[two months]]. It may refuse only if it reasonably believes admission would harm the organisation. It gives written reasons, and the applicant may ask it to reconsider.
2. The board applies admission rules fairly, in line with our Equality, Diversity and Inclusion Policy.
3. The secretary keeps a register of members with names, contact details, class and dates of joining and leaving.
4. The board may set an annual fee [[and reduce or waive it for people who cannot afford it]].

## 8. Members' rights and duties

Each full member has one vote and receives notice of general meetings, the annual accounts and the annual report. Members support the purposes, follow this constitution and our Code of Conduct when taking part in our activities, and must not act in a way that harms the organisation.

## 9. Ending membership

1. Membership ends when a member resigns in writing; dies or, for an organisation, ceases to exist; has not paid the fee within [[three months]] of a written reminder; or is removed under clause 9.2.
2. The board may remove a member whose conduct has seriously harmed the organisation, by a resolution of [[two-thirds]] of board members present and voting. The member first receives at least [[14 days']] written notice of the reasons and a chance to respond. They may appeal to the next general meeting.
3. A former member has no right to a refund of fees or a share of the organisation's property.
4. [[Company limited by guarantee only:]] each member undertakes to contribute up to [[amount and currency]] to the organisation's debts if it is wound up while they are a member, or within one year after they stop being one.

::: adapt Membership model
Decide who will control your organisation.

- **Association model:** a wider membership elects the board each year. Closer to your community, but needs a register, notices and an AGM with a quorum.
- **Board-only model:** the board members are the only members. Simpler for a small team, but funders may ask how communities have a voice; consider an advisory group. The Charity Commission's CIO "foundation model" works this way.

Many civil-law countries require an association to have a general assembly with fixed powers, and a foundation to have no members. Keep clause 9.4 only for a company limited by guarantee; the guarantee is usually a token amount. Some registrars set a minimum number of founding members; check your local law.
:::

# General meetings

## 10. Annual general meeting

1. The organisation holds an annual general meeting (AGM) within [[six months]] after the end of each financial year, and no more than [[15 months]] after the last one.
2. At the AGM the members receive [[or: approve]] the annual report and accounts; elect board members under clause 18; appoint the auditor or examiner where the law requires; and decide any other business in the notice.

## 11. Other general meetings

The board may call a general meeting at any time. It must call one within [[28 days]] of a written request from at least [[10%]] of full members stating the business. If it does not, those members may call it themselves, at the organisation's reasonable cost.

## 12. Notice

1. The secretary gives every member at least [[14 clear days']] notice, by email, post, message or another method the member has agreed to.
2. The notice states the date, time, place, how to join online if available, and the business. It sets out in full any special resolution or proposed amendment.
3. A meeting is not invalid because a member did not receive notice by accident.

## 13. Quorum

1. The quorum is [[the greater of 5 full members or 10% of full members]], present in person or online.
2. If there is no quorum within [[30 minutes]], the meeting is adjourned to a date at least [[7 days]] later set by the chair. The members present at the adjourned meeting form a quorum.

## 14. Chair and voting

1. The chair of the board chairs general meetings. If they are absent, the members present choose a board member, or failing that a member, to chair.
2. Each full member has one vote. Votes are by show of hands, or its online equivalent, unless the chair or [[three]] members ask for a secret ballot.
3. An **ordinary resolution** needs a simple majority of votes cast. A **special resolution** needs at least [[two-thirds / 75%]] of votes cast, and is needed to change this constitution, remove a board member, merge or dissolve.
4. If votes are equal, the chair [[has a casting vote / has no casting vote and the proposal fails]].

## 15. Proxies, postal and online voting

1. A member [[may not vote by proxy / may appoint another member as proxy by written notice received at least 48 hours before the meeting; no one may hold more than two proxies]].
2. The board may allow members to vote [[by post, email or a secure online system]] before a meeting, if the notice explains how.
3. Members may take part online or by phone if they can hear and be heard. They count towards the quorum and may vote.
4. [[Company only:]] members may pass a written resolution without a meeting where the law allows, except to remove a board member or auditor.

::: adapt General meetings, proxies and online voting
Proxies help members who live far away; banning them prevents a few people collecting votes. Postal or online voting is a fair middle way. In the UK, company members have a legal right to appoint a proxy under the Companies Act 2006, which articles cannot remove. In Brazil and Argentina, the law gives the general assembly fixed powers, such as electing the board and approving accounts; follow them. Not every law allows online general meetings or electronic votes, so check your local law before relying on clause 15.3. Set a quorum you can reach every year: many small associations fail their AGM because it is too high.
:::

# The governing body

## 16. Role and powers of the board

1. The organisation is governed by a [[board of trustees / board of directors / management committee]] ("the board").
2. The board manages the organisation's affairs and may use all its powers, except those this constitution or the law reserves to the members.
3. Board members act in the best interests of the organisation and its purposes, within this constitution and the law, with reasonable care and skill, and avoid conflicts of interest.
4. The board sets out how it works in its Governing Body Terms of Reference.

## 17. Number and composition

1. The board has at least [[3]] and no more than [[9]] members.
2. Most board members must be independent: not paid staff and not closely related to paid staff.
3. If the number falls below the minimum, the remaining members may act only to appoint new members, call a general meeting or deal with an emergency.
4. The board aims to include the skills it needs and people from the communities the organisation serves.

## 18. Appointment and terms

1. Board members are [[elected by the members at the AGM / appointed by the board]] from among the full members.
2. The board may co-opt up to [[two]] people for their skills until the next AGM, and may fill a casual vacancy until the next AGM.
3. The first board members are named in the minutes of the founding meeting.
4. Each board member serves [[3]] years and may serve up to [[two]] further consecutive terms. After a break of [[one year]] they may stand again.
5. Before appointment, each person confirms in writing that they are willing to act and not disqualified by law, and completes a Board Interest Declaration.

## 19. Officers

1. The board elects a **chair**, a **treasurer** and a **secretary** from its members for terms of [[one / two]] years. They may be re-elected.
2. The chair and treasurer must be different people. A founder who is a paid employee may not be chair or treasurer.
3. Officers' duties are set out in the Governing Body Terms of Reference.

## 20. Ending board membership and removal

A person stops being a board member if they:

1. resign in writing, provided at least [[three]] board members remain;
2. become disqualified by law;
3. miss [[three]] board meetings in a row without reasons the board accepts, and the board resolves that they should leave;
4. are removed by special resolution at a general meeting, after notice and a chance to be heard; or
5. are removed by [[three-quarters]] of the other board members for serious misconduct, after notice of the reasons and a chance to respond.

## 21. Conflicts of interest

1. A board member declares any direct or indirect interest, or conflict of loyalty, in a matter as soon as they become aware of it.
2. A board member with a conflict leaves the discussion and vote on that matter, unless the other members decide the conflict is minor and record why. They do not count in the quorum for that matter.
3. The board follows its Conflict of Interest Policy. Each board member completes a Board Interest Declaration on appointment and every year.
4. The secretary keeps a register of interests, and the minutes record every declaration and how it was managed.

## 22. Payments and benefits to board members

1. Board members are not paid for serving on the board. The organisation reimburses reasonable expenses under the Expenses and Per Diem Policy.
2. A board member, or a connected person, may be paid for goods or services, interest on a loan, or rent only if:
  1. the payment is reasonable and no more than the market rate;
  2. the other board members approve it without the person concerned and record their reasons;
  3. at no time does a majority of board members receive such payments; and
  4. the law allows it and any regulator's consent required is obtained.
3. A board member may not be employed by the organisation [[unless the law allows it and the members approve]].
4. The organisation discloses payments to board members and connected persons in its accounts where the law or accounting standards require.

::: adapt Board size, terms and payment
Most regulators and funders expect at least three board members, with independent members in the majority. In South Africa, a non-profit company needs at least three directors. In England and Wales, charity trustees may be paid for services (not for being a trustee) only under the conditions in the Charities Act 2011 or with the Charity Commission's consent. In Indonesia, the foundations law sets conditions on paying the pengurus. In Brazil, paying directors of an association may affect tax benefits. In the US, reasonable pay is allowed but excess benefits to insiders are taxed. Check your local law and copy any minimum number, term limit or officer titles it sets. If a paid founder sits on the board, say so openly and keep independent members in the majority.
:::

# Board meetings and decisions

## 23. Board meetings

The board meets at least [[four]] times a year. The chair or any [[two]] board members may call a meeting. The secretary gives at least [[7 clear days']] notice with an agenda, or [[48 hours']] in an emergency.

## 24. Quorum and voting

1. The quorum is [[three board members / one-third of board members, whichever is greater]], most of them independent.
2. Decisions need a simple majority of board members present and voting. If votes are equal, the chair [[has / does not have]] a casting vote.
3. If fewer non-conflicted members than the quorum remain on a matter, the board may decide it only if [[the members approve by ordinary resolution / the law allows]].

## 25. Written resolutions and online meetings

1. A written resolution, including by email or a secure platform, agreed by [[all / a majority of]] board members entitled to vote, is as valid as one passed at a meeting. It is passed when the last required agreement arrives, and is recorded in the next minutes.
2. Board members may take part by video or phone if everyone can hear each other and take part. They count towards the quorum and may vote.

## 26. Minutes

The secretary keeps minutes of every general and board meeting, recording attendance, declarations of interest, decisions and the reasons for important ones. The chair signs them [[on paper or electronically]] once approved. Minutes are kept permanently.

# Delegation

## 27. Committees

The board may set up committees with written terms of reference and at least [[one]] board member, and delegate powers to them. It may not delegate approval of the annual accounts, the budget, borrowing or any matter reserved to the board or members. Committees report to the next board meeting, and the board may end any delegation at any time.

## 28. Executive director and staff

1. The board may appoint an [[Executive Director / Coordinator]] to manage the day-to-day work and delegate powers to them.
2. The board records all delegated authority, with financial limits, in a Delegation of Authority Matrix, reviewed at least [[once a year]].
3. The board remains responsible for delegated decisions and receives regular reports on them.

# Finance, records and accounts

## 29. Money and banking

1. The organisation's money is kept in accounts in its own name with [[a regulated bank / a regulated bank or mobile money provider]].
2. Payments need approval by [[two]] unrelated authorised signatories, as set out in the Delegation of Authority Matrix and the Financial Controls Policy.
3. The board approves an annual budget before each financial year and reviews income, spending and reserves at least [[quarterly]], in line with the Reserves Policy.

## 30. Records and registers

The organisation keeps proper financial records and registers of members, board members and officers, interests, and any others the law requires, for the periods in the Records Retention Schedule and the law.

## 31. Accounts and audit

1. The board prepares annual accounts complying with the law of [[country]] and any accounting standard that applies, and an annual report on activities and achievements.
2. The accounts are [[audited by a qualified auditor / independently examined by a suitably qualified person]] each year, or as the law and our funding agreements require. The auditor or examiner is independent and appointed by [[the members at the AGM / the board]].
3. The board files the accounts, report and any annual return with [[registrar / regulator / tax authority]] on time.

## 32. Financial year

The financial year runs from [[1 January to 31 December / 1 April to 31 March]].

::: adapt Accounts and audit
Whether you need an audit, an independent examination or neither usually depends on your income, legal form and registration. Some regulators set income thresholds (the Charity Commission for England and Wales does), some require audited accounts from every registered body, and foreign funding rules may require a specific audit. Thresholds change, so check them each year with your registrar or tax authority. Many funders ask for an audit even where the law does not. If possible, match your financial year to your main funders' or government's year.
:::

## 33. Indemnity and insurance

The organisation may indemnify board members against liability properly incurred in acting for it, and insure against it, except for fraud, dishonesty, wilful default or criminal fines, or where the law forbids it.

# Changes and dissolution

## 34. Amendments

1. This constitution may be changed only by special resolution at a general meeting, with the change set out in full in the notice.
2. Unless the [[regulator / tax authority]] has given written consent where the law requires it, no change may:
  1. change the purposes so that the organisation stops being [[a non-profit / charitable / a public benefit organisation]];
  2. allow income or property to be distributed to members or board members; or
  3. weaken clauses 5, 22 or 36, or this clause.
3. A change takes effect when passed or, where the law requires, when the registrar approves it. The secretary files each change within [[number]] days and keeps an up-to-date copy of the constitution.

## 35. Dissolution

The organisation may be dissolved by special resolution at a general meeting called for that purpose, or as the law provides. The board then stays in office to wind up its affairs, pay its debts and deal with remaining assets under clause 36.

## 36. Transfer of remaining assets

1. After all debts are paid, remaining assets must not be distributed to members or board members.
2. They go to one or more [[non-profit / charitable / tax-exempt]] organisations with purposes similar to ours, which also prohibit distributing their income and property to their members.
3. The members choose the recipients in the resolution to dissolve; if they do not, the board chooses; if neither does, the assets go as the law of [[country]] provides.
4. Assets held for a restricted purpose are returned to the donor, or used for that purpose, as the grant or donation requires.

::: adapt Amendments and dissolution
Registrars and tax authorities look closely at these clauses. In the US, the IRS requires a dissolution clause dedicating assets to exempt purposes, for example to another 501(c)(3) organisation or to government for a public purpose. In South Africa, SARS requires a public benefit organisation's remaining assets to go to a similar approved organisation or to government. In Brazil, the Civil Code sets what happens to an association's net assets if the statute is silent, and some public titles require transfer to an entity with the same title; check the current rules. Many laws require the registrar to approve amendments, especially to purposes, before they take effect. Name a likely recipient now if you can, and check your grant agreements on returning unspent funds.
:::

# Interpretation and other matters

## 37. Disputes and secondary rules

1. Members or board members in dispute about this constitution first try to settle it through [[mediation by a person both sides accept]] before going to court.
2. The board may make by-laws or standing orders on matters such as membership and meeting procedures. They must be consistent with this constitution and the law, and the members may change or cancel them.
