::: adapt Before you use these terms
**What they cover:** the use of your website, and, if you choose, user accounts, content people share, online donations and a simple shop. Delete the optional sections you do not need: clauses 3, 6, 7 and 8 are marked as optional.

**What they do not cover:** how you handle personal data and cookies. Those belong in your Privacy Notice and Cookie Policy, which these terms refer to.

**Consumer law always applies.** Website terms cannot take away rights that consumer protection law gives to the people who use your site, and terms that try to do so are usually unenforceable and may be unlawful. These terms are written to respect those rights.

**Selling online** needs more: many countries require specific information before purchase (such as your full identity, total price, delivery costs and cancellation rights) and a confirmation afterwards. Clause 8 gives a basic version. If you sell regularly, have it checked by a lawyer in [[country]].

**Publishing:** link to these terms from every page footer. Where people create an account, donate or buy, ask them to tick a box confirming that they accept the terms, and keep a dated copy of each version.
:::

::: adapt Selling online
Delete this clause if you do not sell anything. If you do, check your local consumer law, which may set information duties, cancellation periods and refund times that cannot be reduced. **Country notes:** in the EU, the Consumer Rights Directive (2011/83/EU) gives a 14-day right to cancel most online purchases, and from 19 June 2026 online sellers must also offer a clearly labelled withdrawal function (Directive (EU) 2023/2673); the UK applies a similar rule through the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013. ::: adapt Consumer law limits
Consumer protection laws usually stop businesses and organisations from excluding liability for negligence that causes death or injury, or from removing statutory consumer rights. Many also treat unfair terms in consumer contracts as not binding. Examples are the UK Consumer Rights Act 2015 and the EU Unfair Contract Terms Directive (93/13/EEC). Most countries have similar rules. Keep clause 11 as it is unless a lawyer advises otherwise, and do not add wide exclusions: they can be unenforceable and harm trust.
:::
