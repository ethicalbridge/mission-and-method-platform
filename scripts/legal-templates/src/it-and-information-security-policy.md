- **[[Name, role]] is our IT lead.** They manage our accounts and devices, keep the approved tools list (Annex A), run the joiner and leaver checklist (Annex B) and receive reports of security incidents.
- **[[Name, role]] is our backup administrator.** They hold a second administrator account for every key system, so that we never depend on one person, and cover when the IT lead is unavailable.

::: adapt Who holds these roles
The IT lead need not be a technical expert, but must be organised and have time for the role; often it is the operations manager or founder. The backup administrator should be a second trusted person, such as a board member, so that a lost phone or a departure never locks you out of your own email, bank or social media. If a volunteer or freelancer runs your IT, name them as technical support, but keep the main administrator accounts inside the organisation.

# Who this policy applies to

This policy applies to everyone who uses [[Organisation name]] accounts, devices, systems or information: staff, volunteers, interns, board members, contractors and consultants. It covers:

- devices we provide, and personal devices used for our work;
- our email, cloud storage, messaging, finance, database, website, domain and social media accounts;
- information in any form, including paper files and notebooks.

It applies wherever you work, including at home, in the field and when travelling. It works alongside our Data Protection Policy and Code of Conduct.

**Law.** Most data protection laws, including the GDPR (Article 32), require "appropriate" security measures in proportion to the risk. This policy is how we meet that duty. Where a funder, partner or law sets stricter security rules for a project, the stricter rules apply.

# Our rules

## 1. Accounts belong to the organisation

- Every work account is created in the name of [[Organisation name]], using an organisational email address on our domain [[yourorganisation.org]]. We do not run work on personal email or personal cloud storage.
- Each person has their own account. Where a tool allows only one log-in, its password is kept in the password manager's shared vault for named people.
- Recovery emails and phone numbers for key accounts belong to the organisation, not to personal phones.
- At least two people have administrator rights for each key system: email and cloud, domain and website, bank and payment platforms, social media and the password manager. Nobody uses an administrator account for everyday work.
- The IT lead keeps a list of key accounts, administrators and renewal dates, including our domain name: a lapsed domain takes our email and website with it.

::: adapt Choosing your tools
Pick one main platform for email, documents and calendars, rather than a mix of free personal tools. Many technology providers offer free or discounted plans for registered non-profits; eligibility often depends on your country and registration documents. If you are not yet registered, use a small-business plan rather than personal accounts, so the organisation owns its data from the start.
:::

## 2. Passwords and the password manager

- Use the organisation's password manager, [[name of password manager]], for every work password.
- Use a unique, long random password for every account, created by the password manager. For the few you must remember, such as your device, use a passphrase of at least [[four random words / 14 characters]].
- Never write passwords on paper, in documents, in email or in chat messages.
- If someone else may know a password, change it at once and tell the IT lead.

## 3. Multi-factor sign-in

Multi-factor sign-in (two-step verification) asks for a second proof, such as an app code, as well as your password. It stops most account takeovers.

- Turn it on for every account that offers it, and always for email, cloud storage, finance and banking, social media, the password manager and the website.
- Use an authenticator app or a security key where possible. Text message codes are better than nothing, but SIM swaps can intercept them.
- Store backup codes in the password manager, never on the same phone.
- Never share a sign-in code with anyone, even someone claiming to be from IT or the bank.

## 4. Devices

Every device that holds or accesses our information, whether provided by us or your own, must:

- be **encrypted**. Most modern phones are encrypted once a PIN is set. On laptops, turn on the built-in encryption ([[BitLocker on Windows / FileVault on Mac]]);
- lock automatically after [[5 minutes]] without use, with a PIN of at least [[6 digits]], a password or biometrics;
- install **updates** for the operating system and apps within [[7 days]] of release, and have built-in malware protection switched on;
- run only software from official app stores or approved sources, and be able to be **located and wiped remotely** if lost.

Lock your screen when you step away, and do not let others use a device signed in to work accounts. Organisation devices remain our property and are returned when you leave.

## 5. Using your own device

You may use your own phone or laptop for work if it meets the device rules in section 4 and you agree that:

- work information stays in work apps and accounts, not in your personal photos, downloads or personal cloud storage;
- you will use a separate work profile or account where your device supports it;
- if the device is lost, stolen or sold, or when you leave, we may remove work accounts and data from it. We will not access your personal content;
- you will remove work information and accounts when you leave, and confirm this to the IT lead.

::: adapt Personal devices
Decide whether to allow personal devices, and for what. Small organisations rarely give every volunteer a laptop, so clear rules are often more realistic than a ban. For highly sensitive information, such as case files on survivors of violence, consider organisation devices only. A phone allowance can have tax or employment implications in some countries; check with your accountant.
:::

## 6. Mobile phones and participant information

Phones often hold the most sensitive information we have: photos of participants, contact lists, messages about cases. When you use a phone for work with the people we serve:

- collect information through [[our approved data collection app]], not in notes, photos or chat messages;
- upload data as soon as you have a connection, then delete it from the phone;
- do not keep participants' photos in your phone's camera roll. Move them to [[the approved shared folder]] and delete them from the phone and any automatic personal backup;
- do not discuss individual cases, or share names, photos or documents of people we serve, in group chats. If a messaging app is the only practical option, use [[an approved app with end-to-end encryption]], set messages to disappear after [[X days]] and limit the group to the people who need to know;
- set a SIM PIN as well as a screen lock, and turn off message previews on the lock screen;
- if a phone with participant information is lost, stolen or taken from you, report it immediately, as set out in Reporting a security incident.

::: adapt Field work and messaging apps
Adjust the rules to how your team really works in the field. If you rely on messaging apps, agree the app, settings, group members and what may never be shared. Messaging apps and their backups may store data abroad, and some automatic backups are not end-to-end encrypted. If staff may face checkpoints or device searches, keep as little as possible on the device and agree a plan with the safeguarding lead.
:::

## 7. Approved tools and AI tools

We use only the tools listed in Annex A for work information. Ask the IT lead before using a new tool, especially a free one. They check where it stores data, whether it trains AI on our data, and whether it needs a data processing agreement under our Data Protection Policy.

**AI tools.** Anything you type or upload into an AI tool may be stored, reviewed or used to train models.

- Use only the AI tools listed as approved in Annex A, signed in with an organisational account and with training on our data turned off where the tool allows.
- Never enter personal information about the people we serve, safeguarding or case information, staff records, passwords, bank details or confidential information into an AI tool, unless Annex A approves that tool for it. Remove names and identifying details first.
- Check everything AI produces for accuracy, bias and invented facts. You remain responsible for the result.
- Do not use AI to make decisions about people, such as selecting participants or job candidates, without human review and a Data Protection Impact Assessment.

::: adapt AI tools
Decide which AI tools you allow and for what. A simple approach: one tool on a business plan whose terms say your data is not used for training, approved for drafting and anonymised information only. Check the terms yourself, as they change often. If you work with children, survivors or others at high risk, consider banning their information from AI tools completely.
:::

## 8. Email safety and phishing

Phishing is a fake message designed to trick you into clicking a link, giving a password or sending money. It is the most common attack on small organisations.

**Stop and check** before you act on any message that:

- is urgent, threatening or too good to be true;
- asks you to sign in, open an unexpected attachment, or give a code;
- asks to change bank details, pay a new supplier or buy gift cards, even if it seems to come from the founder, a partner or a funder;
- comes from an address that is slightly different from the usual one.

**Payments.** Before changing any supplier's, partner's or staff member's bank details, or making an unusual or urgent payment, confirm the request by phone, using a number you already hold, not one in the message. Our Financial Controls Policy sets out who approves payments.

If you clicked a link or entered your password by mistake, report it immediately. Nobody is blamed for reporting.

## 9. File storage and sharing

- Store work files in our shared [[cloud drive name]], in the folder for the relevant team or project, not on your device desktop, on USB sticks or in personal accounts.
- Each shared folder has an owner, who checks at least every [[6 months]] who has access and removes people who no longer need it.
- Share files with named people. Do not use "anyone with the link" for anything containing personal or confidential information.
- Keep sensitive information, such as case files, HR records and bank details, in restricted folders with access limited to named people.
- Use USB drives only when necessary, encrypted, and wipe them after use. Lock away paper files and shred them when no longer needed.

## 10. Backups

- Important information is backed up automatically. We follow the "3-2-1" approach where we can: three copies, on two different types of storage, one of them away from the office or in a separate cloud account.
- The IT lead tests that we can restore files from backup at least every [[3 months / 6 months]], and records the result.
- We back up our website and key databases too.

## 11. Wi-Fi, remote work and travel

- Secure office and home Wi-Fi with a strong password, and change the router's default administrator password.
- On public Wi-Fi, such as in hotels, cafés and airports, use your phone's hotspot or [[our approved VPN]] for sensitive work, and do not access bank accounts.
- Never leave devices or papers unattended in vehicles or public places.
- Travel with only the information you need, on devices that are encrypted, updated and backed up.

::: adapt Travel and countries
Some countries restrict encryption tools or VPNs, and border officials in some countries can inspect devices. Check the rules for each destination, and take advice if your work is politically sensitive. For frequent trips with sensitive information, consider a travel device that holds only what the trip needs. Our Travel Safety and Duty of Care Policy covers personal safety.
:::

## 12. Social media, website and domain accounts

Losing access to our social media, website or domain can stop our communications and let someone impersonate us.

- Social media pages are owned by [[Organisation name]], registered with an organisational email and managed through the platform's business tools where available.
- At least two people from the organisation are administrators of each page. A volunteer, freelancer or agency can be given editor rights, never sole ownership.
- Passwords for social media accounts are kept in the password manager, with multi-factor sign-in turned on.
- Access is removed the day someone leaves or a contract ends, using Annex B.
- What we post, and who may speak for us, is covered by our Social Media and Communications Policy.

# Reporting a security incident

A security incident is anything that could put our accounts, devices or information at risk: a lost device, a suspicious link you clicked, a password someone else may know, a file shared with the wrong people, malware, or a hijacked social media page.

**Report it immediately** to [[Name, role]] at [[phone]] or [[email]]. If they cannot be reached within [[1 hour]], contact [[backup administrator]]. Do not try to fix it yourself, and do not delete evidence such as emails or messages.

{widths: 26 44 30}
| When | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Immediately | Report the incident. If a device is lost, change your passwords from another device. | The person who discovers it |
| Within [[1 hour]] | Contain it: lock or wipe the device, reset passwords, end sessions, remove access, recover accounts. | IT lead or backup administrator |
| Within [[24 hours]] | Find out what information is affected. If personal information may be involved, tell the data protection lead at once and follow the Data Breach Response Procedure. | IT lead with the data protection lead |
| Within [[72 hours]] of becoming aware | Decide whether the regulator, the people affected, funders, partners, the bank or the police must be told, and notify the regulator where required. | Data protection lead, with [[Name, role]] |
| Within [[30 days]] | Record lessons learned and fix the cause. | IT lead |

If personal information may be exposed, our Data Breach Response Procedure applies, because laws set strict deadlines: under the GDPR, 72 hours to tell the regulator. If money is involved, call the bank immediately: fast action can sometimes recover a payment. Other incidents are recorded under our Incident Reporting Procedure.

# Joining and leaving

The IT lead uses the checklist in Annex B for every joiner and leaver, including volunteers and contractors. New people get access only to what their role needs. When a role changes, access no longer needed is removed. On or before someone's last day, the IT lead removes all their access, recovers devices and transfers files and accounts they owned. If someone leaves on difficult terms, access is removed before they are told, where lawful and safe.

# Records

The IT lead keeps the key accounts list, Annexes A and B, backup test and access review records, and the incident log in [[secure location]], with access limited to the IT lead and backup administrator.

# If this policy is breached

If you break these rules, we first try to understand why: often a rule is unclear or a tool does not fit. Depending on the case, we may give guidance or training, remove access, or act under our disciplinary procedure, up to dismissal or ending a contract or volunteer role. Deliberate unauthorised access may also be a criminal offence.

Nobody is penalised for reporting an incident or a mistake promptly. Hiding one makes the harm worse.

# Monitoring and review

Every [[6 months]] the IT lead checks multi-factor sign-in, device updates, access lists and backups. They review this policy every year, after any serious incident and when we adopt a major new system, and report to the board.
