# Who this policy applies to

This policy applies to everyone who works with or for [[Organisation name]]: staff, volunteers, interns, board members, contractors and consultants. It protects them, job and volunteer applicants, participants in our programmes, partners, and members of the public who deal with us.

It applies to everything we do: recruitment, pay, training, promotion and the end of employment; how we design and deliver programmes; our communications; our choice of partners and suppliers; and our board.

**Law.** [[Organisation name]] is registered in [[country]] and follows its equality and employment law. Where we work in other countries, we also follow the law there. Where this policy sets a higher standard than the law, we follow this policy, as long as doing so is lawful and safe.

::: adapt Equality law in your country
Name the main laws that apply. Examples:

- **Kenya:** Article 27 of the Constitution of Kenya 2010 (equality and freedom from discrimination) and section 5 of the Employment Act 2007.
- **South Africa:** the Employment Equity Act 1998 and the Promotion of Equality and Prevention of Unfair Discrimination Act 2000.
- **Brazil:** the Brazilian Law on the Inclusion of Persons with Disabilities (Law 13.146 of 2015), and the quota for employing disabled people that applies to employers with 100 or more employees (Law 8.213 of 1991, article 93).
- **United Kingdom:** the Equality Act 2010, which lists nine protected characteristics.
- **India:** the equality rights in the Constitution and the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016.

International benchmarks include ILO Conventions 100 (equal pay) and 111 (discrimination in employment) and the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. Some laws require written policies, workplace plans or reports once you reach a certain number of employees: check your local law on thresholds.
:::

# Our commitments

## 1. We do not discriminate

We do not treat anyone less favourably, directly or indirectly, because of who they are. This includes, but is not limited to:

- age;
- disability, including physical, sensory, intellectual and psychosocial disability;
- sex, gender, gender identity and gender expression;
- sexual orientation;
- marital, civil partnership or family status, and caring responsibilities;
- pregnancy, maternity and breastfeeding;
- race, colour, ethnicity, tribe, clan, caste and descent;
- nationality, national or social origin, and migration, refugee or citizenship status;
- language and accent;
- religion or belief, or having no religion;
- political opinion;
- health status, including HIV status;
- socio-economic status, education, and where someone lives;
- Indigenous identity;
- trade union membership;
- and any other characteristic protected by local law.

**Direct discrimination** means treating someone worse because of one of these characteristics. **Indirect discrimination** means applying a rule that seems neutral but puts a group at a disadvantage without good reason: for example, requiring a university degree for a community facilitator role that does not need one. We also prohibit **harassment** related to any of these characteristics, and **victimisation**, which means treating someone badly because they complained or supported a complaint.

## 2. We recruit fairly

- We advertise roles openly, in places that reach a wide range of people, including [[local radio, community notice boards, partner networks and disability organisations]], as well as online.
- Each role has a description and a short list of the skills and experience it really needs. We do not ask for qualifications the role does not require.
- We welcome applications in [[languages]], and we tell applicants how to ask for adjustments at any stage.
- At least two people shortlist and interview, using the same questions and scoring for each candidate. Where possible, the panel is mixed in gender and background.
- We do not ask about marital status, pregnancy, family plans, religion, health or other personal matters unless the law requires it or it is directly relevant to the role (for example, health checks needed for a role, done after an offer).
- We keep short notes of why each candidate was or was not selected, and we give feedback when asked.

## 3. We pay fairly

- Pay is based on the role, set against a simple pay scale that the board approves, not on the person's sex, nationality or background.
- People doing work of equal value receive equal pay.
- We do not pay staff hired locally and staff hired internationally differently for the same role, unless there is an objective reason, such as an allowance for relocation, which we explain in writing.
- We check our pay once a year for unexplained gaps, for example between women and men, and fix any we find.
- We pay at least the legal minimum wage and aim to pay a [[living wage]] for the place where each person works.

::: adapt Pay fairness
Decide how you will set pay: a simple scale with [[3–5]] levels works for most small organisations. If you employ people in several countries, explain how you set pay in each (for example, local market rates benchmarked against similar NGOs). Some countries require pay gap reporting above a certain size, for example the UK for employers with 250 or more staff. Check whether such rules apply to you.
:::

## 4. Reasonable adjustments and accessibility

A **reasonable adjustment** is a change that removes a barrier for a disabled person, or for someone with another need, so they can work with us or use our services on an equal footing. Examples include flexible hours, a different start time, a screen reader, a quieter workspace, materials in large print or audio, a sign language interpreter, or holding a meeting in an accessible room.

- Anyone may ask for an adjustment at any time, and does not need to give a diagnosis. Ask [[Name, role]].
- We discuss the request with the person within [[10 working days]], agree what will help, and record it.
- We pay for reasonable adjustments. If cost is a barrier, we look for funding or a lower-cost alternative together, before refusing.
- We consider accessibility when we choose offices, venues, websites, documents and transport for activities.
- We make reasonable adjustments for caring responsibilities, religious observance and health conditions too, where we can.

## 5. Inclusive programmes and participation

The people we serve are experts in their own lives. We:

- involve them in designing, running and reviewing our programmes, and pay them fairly for their time where they act as advisers or researchers;
- find out who is not taking part and why, for example women with young children, older people, disabled people, people from minority groups or people in remote areas, and change how we work to reach them;
- choose times, places and formats that suit them: childcare, transport costs, sessions near home, separate spaces where culture requires it;
- consider how our work could exclude or harm some groups before we start a new activity;
- give everyone a safe way to give feedback or complain (see our Complaints and Feedback Procedure), in ways that work for people who cannot read or write.

::: adapt Your programmes
Add one or two concrete commitments that reflect your work, for example "At least half of our community committee members are women" or "Every training is offered in [[local language]] and in sign language on request". Make sure your targets are realistic and that you can measure them.
:::

## 6. Language access

- We communicate with the people we serve in the language they understand best. We use [[languages]] in our main activities, and arrange interpretation where we can.
- We write in plain language, use pictures and spoken explanations for people with low literacy, and read consent forms aloud where needed.
- We use qualified interpreters for sensitive conversations, such as safeguarding, health or legal matters. We use children as interpreters only in an emergency.
- Our main documents for participants, such as consent forms and the complaints procedure, are available in [[languages]].

## 7. Respectful communications

We show people with dignity. We do not use images or stories that reinforce stereotypes, present people as helpless, or reveal identities that could put them at risk. We follow our Social Media and Communications Policy and get informed consent through our Photo and Story Consent Form.

## 8. Our board, partners and suppliers

We aim for a board that reflects the communities we serve (see the Governing Body Terms of Reference). We ask partners and major suppliers to share our commitment to non-discrimination, and we include it in our partnership agreements. We prefer to work with organisations led by people from the communities where we work.

## 9. Harassment, bullying and sexual harassment

Harassment related to any characteristic in section 1, including sexual harassment, is never acceptable. Our Anti-Harassment, Intimidation and Bullying Policy and Procedure explains what this means, how to raise a concern and how we respond. Where the concern involves a child or an adult at risk, our Safeguarding Policy and Procedure applies.

# Sensitive contexts

In some countries, some identities, beliefs or relationships are criminalised or put people in danger. These may include same-sex relationships, gender identity, some religions or none, political opinions, or membership of certain ethnic or minority groups. In these places we protect people first:

- We do not ask about, record or share such characteristics, even voluntarily, where doing so could expose anyone to harm or prosecution.
- Our non-discrimination commitment still applies in full. We treat everyone fairly whether or not we know their identity.
- We think carefully before publishing statements, images or data about such groups, and we follow the advice of local partners and the people concerned.
- If staff are at risk because of who they are, we discuss safety with them privately and apply our Travel Safety and Duty of Care Policy, including adjustments to travel or postings.
- We take legal advice where a law could require us to report or disclose information about people's identity, and we choose the course that keeps people safe and is lawful.

::: adapt Working where identities are criminalised
Check local law carefully, especially laws on sexual orientation, gender identity, religion, "promotion" of particular identities, or registration of organisations that work with certain groups. Some recent laws, for example in Uganda in 2023, create offences that may affect organisations, not only individuals, so take specialist legal advice. In these contexts it may be safer not to publish this policy in full on your website, while keeping it in force internally. Never put staff or participants at risk to make a public statement.
:::

# Monitoring diversity

We collect diversity information only to check that our recruitment, pay and programmes are fair and reach the people they are meant for.

- Giving diversity information is **voluntary**. Every question has a "prefer not to say" option. Nobody is treated differently for not answering.
- We keep diversity data **separate** from application forms and personnel files. Shortlisting and interview panels never see it.
- Only [[Name, role]] can see individual answers. We report only totals, and we do not report groups so small that individuals could be identified (fewer than [[5]] people).
- We ask only questions that are lawful, relevant and safe in each country (see "Sensitive contexts"). Some information, such as health, ethnicity, religion or sexual orientation, is sensitive personal data under most data protection laws, so we follow our Data Protection Policy and, where needed, ask for explicit consent.
- For programmes, we usually collect only the information we need to check reach, such as sex, age group, disability (using simple, recognised questions such as the Washington Group Short Set) and location.

::: adapt What to monitor
For a small organisation, a short anonymous form for job applicants and an annual look at your team, board and participants is enough. The Washington Group Short Set of questions on functioning is widely used to measure disability without asking about diagnoses. Check what your data protection law says about special categories of data, for example the Kenya Data Protection Act 2019, South Africa's POPIA or Brazil's LGPD.
:::

# Raising a concern

If you think you, or someone else, has been discriminated against, harassed or victimised by anyone connected to us:

1. If you feel able, raise it informally with the person or your manager. You do not have to.
2. Or raise it with [[Name, role]] at [[email / phone]], or with [[Name, role]] if it concerns them or the founder.
3. Staff and volunteers can use the formal steps in our Anti-Harassment, Intimidation and Bullying Policy and Procedure. Participants, partners and the public can use our Complaints and Feedback Procedure. Anyone can use our Whistleblowing Policy and Procedure, including [[an anonymous route]].
4. We acknowledge every concern within [[2 working days]] and aim to resolve it within [[30 working days]], or the time limits of the procedure that applies. We tell you if it will take longer, and why.

Nobody will be treated badly for raising a genuine concern, even if it is not upheld. You may also have the right to complain to an external body, such as [[national equality body, labour office or human rights commission]].

# If this policy is breached

We take every breach seriously. Depending on the case, we may give guidance or training, take disciplinary action up to dismissal, end a volunteer role, contract or board membership, or end a partnership. Discrimination, harassment and victimisation are disciplinary matters, and serious cases may be gross misconduct. We also look at whether our own rules or practices caused the problem, and change them.

# Records, confidentiality and data

We keep records of adjustment requests, complaints and diversity data securely, separately from other files, and only for as long as set out in our Records Retention Schedule. We share them only with those who need to know. We handle all personal information under our Data Protection Policy.

# Training

Everyone receives a short briefing on this policy when they join. People who recruit or manage others receive training on fair recruitment, unconscious bias and reasonable adjustments within [[3 months]] of taking on that role.

# Monitoring and review

Each year the inclusion lead reports to the board on:

- the diversity of applicants, staff, volunteers and the board, in totals only;
- adjustment requests and how we responded;
- who takes part in our programmes, and who does not;
- pay checks and any gaps;
- complaints about discrimination and what we learned.

The board agrees up to [[three]] actions for the coming year. We review this policy at least every [[two years]].
