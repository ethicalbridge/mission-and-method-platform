In a small team the director often recruits and the safeguarding lead is the same person. If so, ask a board member or your safeguarding deputy to review any concern raised by a check, so that one person never decides alone. When you recruit a new director or safeguarding lead, the board runs the process.

::: glance
- Every role gets a risk level: the checks depend on it, not on whether the role is paid.
- Adverts and job descriptions state our commitment to safeguarding and our Code of Conduct.
- We ask everyone to explain gaps in their history and, for higher-risk roles, to sign a self-declaration.
- At least two people shortlist and interview, using fair criteria and a safeguarding question.
- We take at least two references, including the most recent employer, and ask directly about safeguarding and misconduct.
- We check identity and right to work, and qualifications that matter for the role.
- Police checks are used where lawful and reliable, but they are never enough on their own.
- Nobody starts unsupervised work with children, adults at risk or money until checks are complete. Speed is never a reason to skip a step.
:::

# Purpose and scope

This procedure applies to every role at [[Organisation name]]: staff, volunteers, interns, board members [[trustees / directors / committee members]], consultants and contractors, including drivers, translators, photographers and researchers. It also applies to people we place with partners, and to partners recruiting for projects we fund, through our agreements with them.

It sets minimum steps, proportionate to risk: a one-day event volunteer with no contact with participants needs fewer checks than a field worker visiting families alone.

**Law.** [[Organisation name]] recruits in [[country / countries]] and follows the employment, equality and data protection law in each. Where a funder or partner requires stricter checks, we follow them for their project. Where the law forbids a check described here, we do not carry it out, and record what we did instead.

::: adapt Law and countries
Check three areas of local law:

- **Discrimination in recruitment.** Most countries protect applicants as well as employees. Kenya's Employment Act 2007 (section 5) prohibits discrimination against prospective employees, including on HIV status and pregnancy. In South Africa, the Employment Equity Act 1998 treats applicants as employees for its rules on unfair discrimination and medical and psychological testing (section 9), and prohibits medical testing unless legislation permits it or it is justifiable (section 7). In the UK, the Equality Act 2010 (section 60) restricts questions about health or disability before a job offer, with limited exceptions.
- **Criminal record information.** The GDPR (Article 10) allows criminal conviction data to be processed only under official authority or where law authorises it with safeguards. South Africa's POPIA treats criminal behaviour as special personal information. The Philippines Data Privacy Act 2012 includes offences and related proceedings in sensitive personal information. Kenya's Data Protection Act 2019 does not list criminal records as sensitive data, but treat them as sensitive anyway. Elsewhere, check your local law on collecting criminal records.
- **Right to work and immigration,** covered in Step 7.
:::

# Role risk levels

The safeguarding lead sets the risk level when the role is planned, using this table.

{widths: 18 42 40}
| Level | Examples | Minimum checks |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Regular or unsupervised contact with children or adults at risk; home visits; overnight activities; access to case records; approving payments or handling cash over [[amount]]; the director and safeguarding lead | All steps, including a self-declaration, two references with safeguarding questions, and a police check where lawful and reliable |
| **Medium** | Occasional supervised contact with participants; access to their personal data; handling small amounts of cash; board members | Interview, two references (one with safeguarding questions), self-declaration, identity check; police check if lawful and justified |
| **Low** | No contact with participants, personal data or money: for example design work or one-off event help | Conversation, one reference, identity check, Code of Conduct signed |

::: adapt Setting risk levels
Adjust the examples to your work, thinking about the setting as well as the task: a driver who transports children alone is high risk, and so is an online tutor working one-to-one with teenagers. Set a cash threshold that fits your Financial Controls Policy. Contractors who work directly with participants need the same checks as staff.
:::

# Step 1: Plan the role

Before advertising, the recruiting manager prepares:

1. **A role description** with the purpose, tasks, reporting line and safeguarding responsibilities: "Follow our Safeguarding Policy and Procedure and Code of Conduct, and report any concern within [[24 hours]]." For high-risk roles, describe the contact with children or adults at risk.
2. **A person specification:** the essential and desirable skills, experience and values. Avoid qualifications the role does not need.
3. **The risk level,** agreed with the safeguarding lead, and the checks it requires.
4. **A plan:** who shortlists, interviews and takes references, and when.

# Step 2: Advertise

We advertise openly, in places that reach a wide range of people, as set out in our Equality, Diversity and Inclusion Policy. Every advert and information pack includes this statement:

::: statement Safeguarding statement for adverts
[[Organisation name]] is committed to keeping children, adults at risk and everyone we work with safe from harm, including sexual exploitation, abuse and harassment. Everyone who works with us follows our Code of Conduct. All appointments are subject to safer recruitment checks, including references and, where relevant and lawful, criminal record checks. We welcome applications from everyone and make reasonable adjustments at any stage.
:::

# Step 3: Application and self-declaration

We use an application form, or ask for a CV with a short form, so that we get the same information from everyone. It asks for:

- full employment, volunteering and education history, with reasons for leaving;
- an explanation of any gaps of more than [[6 months]];
- the names of at least two referees, including the most recent employer;
- for medium- and high-risk roles, consent to safer recruitment checks.

For medium- and high-risk roles, the candidate signs the self-declaration in Annex C, where the law allows it. It asks whether they have been convicted of relevant offences, dismissed or disciplined for misconduct, left a role during an investigation, or barred from working with children or adults at risk. A false declaration is grounds to withdraw an offer or end the appointment.

We ask for it [[at application / after shortlisting / with the conditional offer]], and keep it separate from the shortlisting papers.

::: adapt Asking about criminal records
Rules differ widely. In England and Wales, under the Rehabilitation of Offenders Act 1974, applicants generally need not disclose spent convictions, and a spent conviction is not a proper ground for excluding someone, except in exempt roles that qualify for standard or enhanced DBS checks. In the United States, many states and cities have "ban the box" or fair chance laws that limit when and how you can ask. In Brazil, the Superior Labour Court (TST) ruled in 2017 that requiring a criminal record certificate is lawful only where a law, the nature of the role or a special degree of trust justifies it, for example caring for children, older people or people with disabilities; otherwise it can lead to damages. Where you may not ask about all convictions, limit the declaration to what the law allows.
:::

# Step 4: Shortlist fairly

At least two people shortlist, using a scoring grid based only on the person specification. Gaps or inconsistencies are explored at interview, not used to exclude automatically. We do not use information about protected characteristics, such as age, marital status, religion or health, unless the law requires it or it is a genuine requirement of the role. We keep short notes of why each candidate was or was not shortlisted.

# Step 5: Interview

At least two people interview, in person or online; for high-risk roles, one has had safeguarding training. Every candidate is asked the same core questions, including:

- at least one question about **values**, such as respect, honesty and accountability;
- at least one **safeguarding** question, exploring attitudes to children, adults at risk, boundaries and reporting concerns;
- questions about **gaps** and reasons for leaving previous roles.

Annex D gives a question bank. Interviewers score answers against the person specification and note warning signs: vague answers about boundaries, blaming others for past problems, or an unusual interest in unsupervised contact with children.

# Step 6: References

We take at least two references, and three for high-risk roles where possible. One must come from the most recent employer, or the most recent role involving children or adults at risk. References from family members or friends are not accepted.

We send the request in Annex B to the referee's work email, or confirm their identity by phone. It asks directly about safeguarding concerns, misconduct investigations and disciplinary sanctions. We do not rely on open letters the candidate gives us, and we follow up vague or inconsistent answers by phone.

We follow the principles of the Misconduct Disclosure Scheme. Its signatories ask previous employers, with the candidate's knowledge, whether the candidate was found to have committed, or was under investigation for, sexual exploitation, abuse or sexual harassment, and answer such requests themselves.

::: adapt References and the Misconduct Disclosure Scheme
Humanitarian, development and other civil society organisations can join the Misconduct Disclosure Scheme, which provides standard forms and wording: use its current versions. If previous employers are small local organisations, call them: many will answer a direct question by phone that they would not put in writing. Check your local data protection law on asking for and giving misconduct information.
:::

# Step 7: Identity, right to work and qualifications

Before anyone starts, we check:

- **identity,** from an original photo identity document, checked against the person in person or by video call; where someone has none, for example a refugee, we use other reliable evidence and record what we saw;
- **right to work** in [[country]], following the official process, including any permit's conditions and expiry date;
- **essential qualifications and professional registration,** such as a nursing or social work registration or a driving licence, by seeing the original or checking with the issuing body.

We record what we saw, when and by whom, and keep copies only where the law requires it.

::: adapt Right to work
Check your local law on who may work, what documents prove it and what you must keep. In the UK, for example, employers who do not carry out the required right to work checks face civil penalties of up to £45,000 per illegal worker for a first breach and £60,000 for repeat breaches (for checks from 13 February 2024). Elsewhere, check your local law on work permits, the documents you must see and the penalties for employers. Volunteers from abroad may also need a permit.
:::

# Step 8: Criminal record and police checks

For high-risk roles, and medium-risk roles where the law allows and the role justifies it, we ask for a criminal record or police check from each country where the person has lived for [[12 months]] or more in the last [[5 years]], where such a check is available.

We are realistic about police checks. In many countries they are slow, incomplete or easy to forge, and most abuse is never reported to the police. A clear check does not prove someone is safe, so references, the self-declaration, a good interview and supervision matter more. Where a check is unavailable or unreliable, we record why and add steps: an extra reference, longer supervision, or a call to a previous employer's safeguarding lead. We verify certificates where the issuing body allows it, and pay for checks we require.

::: adapt Police checks by country
Decide which roles need a check and from which countries. Notes to confirm locally:

- **United Kingdom:** Disclosure and Barring Service (DBS) checks in England and Wales: basic (unspent convictions, any role), standard and enhanced (eligible roles only), and enhanced with barred list checks (eligible roles only). Enhanced checks are free for eligible volunteers. Scotland uses Disclosure Scotland and Northern Ireland uses AccessNI, with different rules.
- **Kenya:** a Police Clearance Certificate, still often called a Certificate of Good Conduct, issued by the Directorate of Criminal Investigations through eCitizen with fingerprinting. Allow several weeks.
- **South Africa:** a police clearance certificate, plus checks of the National Child Protection Register and the National Register for Sex Offenders for people working with children.
- **Nigeria:** a Police Character Certificate from the Nigeria Police Force.
- **Philippines:** a National Bureau of Investigation (NBI) clearance.
- **India and Brazil:** police verification and criminal record certificates are issued by state (and in Brazil also federal) authorities; see the Brazil note in Step 3 on when you may ask.
- **United States:** if you use a background screening company, the Fair Credit Reporting Act requires a stand-alone written notice and the candidate's written permission, and notices before and after any adverse decision. Check state and city fair chance laws.

For international hires, ask for checks from each country of residence, and allow extra time.
:::

# Step 9: When a check raises a concern

A disclosure, a poor reference or an unexplained gap is not an automatic rejection. The safeguarding lead, with [[a second person, such as a board member]], carries out a short, confidential risk assessment:

1. Is the information relevant to the role? For example, a conviction for violence or fraud is relevant to a high-risk role; an old minor traffic offence usually is not.
2. How serious was it, how long ago, and was it a single event or a pattern?
3. Did the candidate disclose it honestly?
4. What has changed since, and what does the candidate say? We always give them the chance to explain.
5. Can the risk be managed, for example through supervision or a different role, or not?

We record the decision and reasons. We reject a candidate where there is a real risk to people or money that cannot be managed, or where they deliberately hid relevant information. A conviction or finding of sexual exploitation, abuse or harassment, or of an offence against a child or adult at risk, rules out contact roles, and we never appoint anyone to work the law bars them from. We tell the candidate the outcome and, where the law allows, the reason.

# Step 10: Conditional offer and starting work

Offers are made in writing, conditional on the outstanding checks, which the letter lists. Nobody starts unsupervised work in a high-risk role until all checks are complete and the safeguarding lead has signed the checklist (Annex A).

If, exceptionally, someone must start before a check arrives, the safeguarding lead agrees it in writing. The person works only under direct supervision, with no unsupervised contact with children or adults at risk and no authority over money, as the Employment Contract and Volunteer Agreement allow.

# Step 11: Induction and probation

Within [[their first two weeks]], and before any contact work, every new team member:

- reads and signs the Code of Conduct and the Safeguarding Policy and Procedure;
- completes safeguarding induction, including how to recognise and report concerns;
- is introduced to the safeguarding lead and knows how to raise a concern;
- learns the data protection, financial and IT rules that apply to their role.

During probation, usually [[three to six months]], the manager meets the new team member at least [[monthly]] and reviews their conduct with participants and colleagues as well as their work. Concerns are handled under the Safeguarding Policy and Procedure, and any action under the Disciplinary and Grievance Procedure or the person's agreement.

# Recruiting quickly in an emergency

In an emergency, we can shorten timelines but never skip safeguarding steps. We:

- use a short application and a phone or video interview with two people, including a safeguarding question;
- get the self-declaration signed before any work starts;
- take at least one reference by phone before the person starts, and the second within [[2 weeks]];
- start the person under supervision, with no unsupervised contact with children or adults at risk, until all checks are complete;
- record any delayed step and close it within [[30 days]].

# Volunteers, board members and contractors

**Volunteers and interns** in the same type of role follow the same steps as staff. For low-risk roles a conversation can replace a formal interview. Volunteers under 18 need written consent from a parent or guardian and are never placed in roles with unsupervised responsibility for others.

**Board members** sign the Code of Conduct and a self-declaration, give two references and complete a Board Interest Declaration. The chair reviews any concern.

**Contractors and consultants** in medium- or high-risk roles go through the checks for their risk level, or show that their employer has done equivalent checks.

# Records and data protection

Our Privacy Notice tells candidates what we collect, why and for how long. We collect only what the role needs.

- **Unsuccessful candidates:** applications and interview notes are kept for [[6 months]] after the decision, then deleted.
- **Successful candidates:** the application, references, checklist and outcome of checks go on the personnel file and are kept for the length of the role and [[6 years]] after it ends.
- **Criminal record certificates:** we record the date, type, reference number and outcome ("check completed, no concerns" or "concern assessed, decision recorded"), and delete the certificate once the decision is made, and within [[6 months]] of the check at the latest, unless the law requires otherwise. Risk assessments are kept with the personnel file.
- **Self-declarations** are seen only by the safeguarding lead and records holder, and kept in [[an encrypted folder / locked cabinet]].

Records are handled under our Data Protection Policy and listed in our Records Retention Schedule. We never put recruitment or check information into AI tools or online services we have not approved.

::: adapt Retention of recruitment records
Check your local law on how long you must, or may, keep recruitment records and criminal record information. Some countries limit how long criminal record data can be kept or require prior authorisation to process it: South Africa's POPIA, for example, requires prior authorisation from the Information Regulator for some processing of criminal information. Set the period for unsuccessful candidates to cover the time limit for discrimination claims where you recruit. Add your periods to your Records Retention Schedule.
:::

# Monitoring and review

The safeguarding lead reviews the recruitment checklists for high-risk roles every [[6 months]] and gives the board a short yearly summary: roles filled, checks completed, steps delayed, and any concerns raised. We review this procedure every [[12 months]], and after any safeguarding incident involving someone we recruited.

# Annex D: Interview question bank

Choose two or three questions from each section. Listen for concrete examples, an understanding of power and boundaries, and willingness to report concerns.

### Values

- Tell us about a time you had to choose between what was quickest and what was right. What did you do?
- Describe a mistake you made at work. How did you handle it?

### Safeguarding and boundaries

- Why do you want to work with [[children / young people / adults at risk / communities]]?
- A participant gives you their phone number and asks to stay in touch privately. What do you do?
- What would you do if a child told you they were being hurt, and asked you to keep it secret?
- What would you do if you saw a colleague behaving in a way that made you uncomfortable with a participant?

### Honesty and money

- Tell us about a time you noticed something wrong with money, records or reports. What did you do?
- A supplier offers you a gift for choosing their quote. How do you respond?

### History

- Can you talk us through this gap in your history?
- Why did you leave your last role?
- Is there anything about your previous work that we will hear from your referees and that you would like to explain first?
