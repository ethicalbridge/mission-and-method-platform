# Purpose and scope

This procedure explains what to do when an incident happens in connection with [[Organisation name]]: at work, at our activities, when travelling for us, and online.

An **incident** is an event that has caused, or could cause, harm to people, money, property, personal information or our reputation. A **near miss** could have caused harm but did not. Report near misses too: they show us where to improve.

Some incidents are then investigated under their own policy:

- harm, abuse or exploitation: Safeguarding Policy and Procedure;
- fraud, theft, bribery or corruption: Fraud, Bribery and Corruption Policy and Procedure;
- lost, stolen or wrongly shared personal data: Data Breach Response Procedure;
- complaints from the people we serve, supporters or the public: Complaints and Feedback Procedure.

If you are not sure which applies, report under this procedure. The incident lead will route it. You can also raise a concern confidentially under our Whistleblowing Policy and Procedure.

**Law.** [[Organisation name]] is registered in [[country]] and follows its laws, and the law of any other country where we work. Where a funder's or partner's rules are stricter than this procedure, the stricter rule applies to their project.

# Severity levels

The incident lead sets the level on receiving a report. If unsure, choose the higher level: it can go down later.

{widths: 13 27 36 24}
| Level | What it means | Examples | Who is told, and when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Low** | Minor harm or a near miss. No lasting effect. | A minor cut at an activity; a small payment error, found and fixed; a rude comment on social media. | Incident lead within [[24 hours]]. Logged. |
| **Medium** | Some harm or loss, or a clear risk of more. Needs a planned response. | An injury needing medical treatment; theft of equipment; an email with personal data sent to the wrong person; a formal complaint about a staff member. | Incident lead within [[24 hours]]. Director within [[2 working days]]. |
| **High** | Serious harm or loss, or serious risk to people, funds or our reputation. May need outside reporting. | Any safeguarding concern; an allegation of sexual exploitation, abuse or harassment; suspected fraud over [[amount]]; a data breach affecting the people we serve; a serious injury; a threat against staff; a serious complaint from a community; negative media coverage. | Incident lead (safeguarding lead for safeguarding) **immediately**. Director and board chair the **same day**, unless involved. |
| **Critical** | Life-threatening, or a threat to our ability to operate. | A death; kidnapping, arrest or detention of anyone working for us; a sexual assault; large-scale fraud; a serious breach of sensitive data; a ban on our work; a national media crisis. | Incident lead **immediately** by phone. # Step 1: Make people safe

1. **If anyone is in immediate danger**, call emergency services on [[emergency number]] or the police on [[police number]]. Get medical help for anyone injured.
2. **Protect yourself.** Do not put yourself at risk to protect property or evidence.
3. **Stop the harm if you safely can**, for example by moving people away from a hazard or disconnecting a compromised device from the network.
4. **Look after the person affected.** Listen, stay calm, and tell them they were right to speak up. Do not ask leading questions or promise to keep secrets.
5. **Preserve evidence.** Do not delete messages, clean up or change records. Never photograph a person who has been harmed.
6. **Do not investigate or confront anyone** you think is responsible. It can put people at risk.

::: adapt Local emergency contacts
List real numbers for each place you work: police, ambulance, fire, the nearest hospital or clinic, and security contacts. Common numbers include 112 (EU and many other countries), 999 (UK, Kenya and others) and 911 (US and several Latin American countries), but coverage and response vary: check locally and test the number. Where calling the police could put survivors or staff at risk, write down who decides and how. Keep the list in everyone's phone.
:::

# Step 2: Report it

Report every incident to the incident lead, even if it seems small or someone else may already have reported it.

| How serious | How to report | Time limit |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | Phone the incident lead on [[number]]. No answer within [[30 minutes]]: phone the deputy on [[number]]. Follow up in writing. | Immediately |
| High | Phone or message the incident lead, then send the report form (Annex A). | Immediately; form within [[24 hours]] |
| Medium or low | Send the report form to [[incidents@yourorganisation.org]]. | Within [[24 hours]] |
| Near miss | Send the form, or raise it at the next team meeting. | Within [[5 working days]] |

You do not need proof. Report what you saw, heard or were told, in plain words. If you cannot use the form, report by phone or in person and the incident lead will complete it with you. Do not put names or sensitive details in group chats.

**If the incident involves the incident lead,** report to the deputy, [[Name, role]], at [[email / phone]]. If it involves the director, report to the board chair, [[Name]], at [[email / phone]]. **Safeguarding concerns** go to the safeguarding lead, not the incident lead, within the time limits of the Safeguarding Policy and Procedure: [[2 hours]] if someone is in danger or it is serious, otherwise [[24 hours]]. The safeguarding lead logs only a reference number and level here, and tells the director and board chair only what they need, without names. **Partners and contractors** report incidents connected to our work to our incident lead within [[24 hours]], as their agreement with us requires.

# Step 3: Assess and escalate

When the incident lead receives a report, they:

1. **Acknowledge it**: the same day for high and critical incidents, within [[2 working days]] for others.
2. **Set the severity level** and open an entry in the incident log (Annex B) with a reference number.
3. **Agree immediate protective action**, for example medical care, moving staff to safety, blocking access to systems or bank accounts, or suspending a person from their role. A suspension is a precaution, not a finding of wrongdoing.
4. **Escalate**: medium incidents to the director within [[2 working days]]; high incidents to the director and board chair the same day; critical incidents to both within [[2 hours]].
5. **Pass the case** to the lead under the policy that applies (safeguarding, fraud, data breach or complaints), if that is someone else.
6. **Check external reporting duties** (Step 4), and record each decision, including any decision not to report and why.

For a critical incident, the director, incident lead and one board member form a response team that talks at least daily until the situation is stable. The board chair decides when the full board is told; for critical incidents, within [[48 hours]].

# Step 4: External notifications

The incident lead makes these reports, or agrees who will, and records the date, time, person contacted and any reference. Reporting early and updating later is better than reporting late.

{widths: 20 32 20 28}
| Who | When to tell them | Typical timescale | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Police or emergency services** | A crime, a death, a threat to life, or where the law requires it (for example some child abuse cases). | Immediately | In safeguarding cases, think first about the safety and wishes of the person affected. |
| **Charity or NGO regulator** | Serious incidents such as significant fraud or loss, safeguarding incidents or a criminal investigation, where the regulator has a reporting duty. | [[Promptly / within X days]] | Usually the board's duty; the incident lead prepares the report. |
| **Data protection authority** | A personal data breach likely to put people's rights and freedoms at risk. | Often within 72 hours of becoming aware | Follow the Data Breach Response Procedure. Tell the people affected without undue delay if the risk is high. |
| **Funders** | What each grant agreement lists: usually safeguarding concerns, fraud or suspected fraud, misuse of funds, sanctions or terrorism-financing concerns, and serious delays. | Often 24 to 72 hours; check each grant | Share only the personal data the funder needs. |
| **Insurers** | Any event that may lead to a claim: injury, theft, damage, legal action, cyber incident. | As the policy says, usually as soon as reasonably possible | Late notice can invalidate a claim. Do not admit liability first. |
| **Others the law requires** | For example a labour authority for serious workplace injuries, or a financial intelligence unit for suspected terrorism financing or sanctions matches. | As the law requires | See the Partner Due Diligence and Sanctions Screening Policy. Never warn the person concerned. |

::: adapt Charity or NGO regulator reporting
Check whether your regulator has a formal "serious incident" or "notifiable event" duty, what counts and the deadline. For example, the Charity Commission for England and Wales expects trustees to report serious incidents promptly, and the Scottish Charity Regulator (OSCR) asks charities to report notifiable events. Many regulators elsewhere have no formal incident route. If you are registered as an NGO, trust, society, non-profit company or public benefit organisation, check your local law on reporting duties to the registering body. Rules change: check current guidance.
:::

::: adapt Data protection deadlines
Under the EU and UK GDPR, a personal data breach must be reported to the supervisory authority within 72 hours of becoming aware of it, unless it is unlikely to result in a risk to people. The Kenya Data Protection Act 2019 and the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 also use 72 hours. South Africa's POPIA requires notice to the Information Regulator and the people affected as soon as reasonably possible. Brazil's LGPD, India's DPDP Act 2023 and Indonesia's Law 27 of 2022 set timescales through their rules and regulations: check your local law on breach notification, including what the notice must contain.
:::

::: adapt Funder conditions and workplace injuries
Keep a table of each active grant: what must be reported, deadline and contact. Many institutional funders require safeguarding allegations and suspected fraud to be reported within 24 to 72 hours, even when unproven. Add each new agreement's reporting clause to the table the day you sign it. For workplace injuries, many countries require employers to report serious accidents to a labour authority or a work-injury insurer (in the UK, under the RIDDOR Regulations 2013): check your local law on workplace accident reporting.
:::

# Step 5: Respond and investigate

For most low and medium incidents, the response means fixing the cause, supporting anyone affected and recording what was done. For high and critical incidents, the incident lead and the director decide:

- **who investigates**, under the policy that applies. Nobody involved, or who manages someone involved, investigates. For serious cases we may use an external investigator;
- **what support people need**, such as medical or psychological support or time off. Staff exposed to distressing events are offered [[support, e.g. counselling sessions]];
- **how we communicate.** Only [[Name, role]] speaks to the media, as set out in the Social Media and Communications Policy.

People named in a report are treated fairly and can respond. Retaliation against anyone who reports a genuine concern is a serious breach of our Code of Conduct. If the authorities investigate, we cooperate and may pause our own work if asked.

# Step 6: Close and learn

The incident lead closes an incident when people are safe, causes are dealt with, external reports are complete and any investigation has ended. For high and critical incidents, and repeated smaller ones, they lead a short **learning review** within [[30 days]]. It asks:

1. What happened, and what were the causes, beyond the people involved?
2. What went well in our response?
3. What would we do differently, including how quickly we reported?
4. What will we change, who will do it, and by when?

The incident lead updates the Risk Register and any policy that needs to change. The review is about learning, not blame; any disciplinary matter follows its own process.

# Records and confidentiality

The incident lead keeps the log and all reports in [[secure location, e.g. a restricted folder only the incident lead and director can open]]. Paper forms are scanned and then locked away or securely destroyed.

- Information is shared only with people who need it to respond, investigate or meet a legal or funder duty.
- Safeguarding case records are kept separately by the safeguarding lead. The incident log shows only a reference number and the level.
- Board reports use reference numbers, not names, unless the board needs names to decide.
- Personal data is handled under our Data Protection Policy.
- We keep records for [[6 years]] after closure, or longer if legal proceedings or an insurance claim are open or expected. Records involving a child are kept until the child turns [[25]], or [[6 years]] after closure if later. Then they are deleted securely, following our Records Retention Schedule.

::: adapt Retention periods
Retention depends on local limitation periods for legal claims, insurance and funder conditions, and data protection law. Six years is common in many common-law countries; others differ. Check your local law and align these periods with your Records Retention Schedule.
:::

# Monitoring and review

The incident lead reviews open incidents [[every week]] and gives the board an anonymised summary [[every quarter]]: number, type and level of incidents, time to report and close, and lessons. Once a year we test this procedure with a practice scenario, such as a lost laptop.
