# Who this policy applies to

This policy applies to everyone who works with or for [[Organisation name]]:

- **Staff:** everyone we employ, full-time, part-time or temporary.
- **Volunteers and interns**, from the day they start.
- **Board members** [[trustees / directors / committee members]] and advisers.
- **Contractors and consultants** we pay to deliver work for us.
- **Partners, sub-grantees and suppliers** who receive, spend or handle our funds, or act on our behalf. Their agreements require them to follow this policy or an equivalent one.

It covers all money and assets we hold or control: grants, donations, earned income, cash, mobile money, equipment, vehicles, stock and data. It applies everywhere we work, at any time, including online.

**Law.** [[Organisation name]] is registered in [[country]] and follows its laws on fraud, bribery, corruption, money laundering and sanctions. When we work in other countries, or with funders based in other countries, we also follow the laws that apply there. Where rules differ, we follow the stricter one. If a funder's or partner's policy is stricter than this one, the stricter rule applies to their funds.

# Our position

Every unit of money we hold was given to us in trust. Fraud and corruption take resources away from the people we exist for, and can end funding relationships and community trust.

1. We have **zero tolerance** of fraud, theft, bribery, corruption, extortion, money laundering and terrorist financing. This applies whether it is done directly or through someone else, such as an agent, partner or supplier.
2. Zero tolerance means we **always act** on a reasonable suspicion. It does not mean we assume guilt.
3. We **never pay bribes or facilitation payments**, even if this causes delay or lost opportunities.
4. Everyone **helps prevent and report** wrongdoing. Anyone who knew of fraud and did nothing may also face action.
5. We **tell funders and authorities** where agreements or the law require it, and we **try to recover** every loss.
6. **Nobody is penalised** for refusing a bribe or for reporting a genuine concern, even if it proves unfounded.

# What we mean

{widths: 22 40 38}
| Term | What it means | Example in our work |
|---|---|---|
| Fraud | Deceiving someone to gain something or cause a loss, even if it fails. | Inflating an expense claim, inventing participants on an attendance list, forging a receipt, reporting activities that did not happen. |
| Theft | Taking money or property without permission. | Taking petty cash, keeping a laptop after leaving, selling donated supplies. |
| Bribery | Offering, giving, asking for or accepting any advantage, not only money, so that someone acts improperly in their role. | Paying an official to approve a registration, or a supplier offering a staff member a phone to win a contract. |
| Facilitation payment | A small unofficial payment to an official to speed up a routine action they should do anyway. A form of bribery under this policy. | Paying a customs officer to release goods faster, or a clerk to issue a permit already due. |
| Kickback | Part of a payment secretly returned to the person who arranged it. | A supplier adds 10% to an invoice and gives that amount back to the staff member who chose them. |
| Corruption | Abusing a position of trust for private gain. | Choosing a venue owned by a friend at an inflated price. |
| Extortion | Getting money, work or favours through threats or pressure. | An armed group demands a payment for a vehicle to pass a checkpoint. |
| Nepotism and favouritism | Giving jobs, contracts or support to relatives or friends because of the relationship, not merit. | Hiring a cousin without an open process, or adding a friend's family to a list of people receiving support. |
| Money laundering | Hiding the criminal origin of money by passing it through legitimate accounts or activities. | A “donor” gives a large sum in cash, then asks for most of it to be refunded to a different account. |
| Terrorist financing | Providing or collecting funds, knowing or suspecting they may be used for terrorism. | Funds or goods diverted to a group on a sanctions list. |
| Misuse of funds by partners | A partner or sub-grantee using our funds for purposes other than those agreed, or without proper records. | A partner charges the same staff salary to two funders, or uses training funds for unrelated costs without approval. |

# Policy

## 1. Bribery and facilitation payments

- Never offer, promise, give, ask for or accept a bribe, a kickback or any other improper advantage, directly or through someone else.
- Never make a facilitation payment, whatever the amount and however common it is locally.
- Pay official fees only when they are published or written in law, and always get an official receipt.
- If asked for an unofficial payment, politely refuse, ask for the official basis of the fee, and tell [[Name, role]] the same day.
- Never use an agent, driver or partner to make a payment you could not make yourself.
- Never donate to political parties or candidates on behalf of [[Organisation name]].

## 2. When your safety is at risk

Your personal safety always comes first. If you, or someone with you, faces a real and immediate threat to life, health or liberty unless a payment is made, you may pay. This is not a bribe under this policy. It is a payment made under duress.

1. Pay only what is needed to get yourself and others to safety.
2. Leave as soon as it is safe to do so.
3. Report the payment to [[Name, role]] within [[24 hours]], or as soon as you are safe. Use the form in the annex.
4. We record it honestly in our accounts as a duress payment, never hidden as another cost.
5. We decide whether to inform funders or authorities, and update our security plans.

Nobody is disciplined for a genuine duress payment reported promptly. Delay or inconvenience is not a threat to safety and never justifies a payment.

Funders generally accept genuine duress payments if they are reported and recorded; check whether your agreements set a reporting format. ## 3. Gifts and hospitality

Gifts and hospitality can be used to disguise a bribe. Follow our Gift Acceptance Policy. In short:

- Never accept or give anything that could influence, or look as if it influences, a decision.
- Never accept cash, mobile money credit or vouchers, or anything from a supplier during a tender.
- Record every gift or hospitality offered above [[amount and currency]] in the gifts and hospitality register.

## 4. Conflicts of interest and nepotism

Declare any personal, family, financial or professional link to a supplier, partner, job applicant, person receiving support, or funder before any related decision. Step out of the decision. Our Conflict of Interest Policy explains how we record and manage conflicts. Recruitment of relatives follows that policy.

## 5. Money laundering, terrorist financing and sanctions

The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) sets global standards on money laundering. Its Recommendation 8 recognises that some non-profits can be misused for terrorist financing, and calls for a risk-based approach that does not disrupt legitimate work. We take proportionate steps:

- We know who our funders, donors, partners and main suppliers are. We follow our Partner Due Diligence and Sanctions Screening Policy before we sign an agreement or make a significant payment.
- We check partners, sub-grantees, major suppliers and large donors against the UN Security Council Consolidated List, and the lists our funders require, such as the US Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) list, the EU consolidated list and the UK Sanctions List.
- We do not accept large donations in cash or from unknown sources without checks. Our Gift Acceptance Policy sets the threshold.
- We do not refund donations to a different person or account from the one that paid.
- We move funds through regulated banks or mobile money wherever possible, and record the reason for any cash transfer.
- We report suspicious transactions where the law requires it.

::: adapt Sanctions and money laundering
Name the lists your funders require and who runs the checks; record the date and result of each one. In some countries non-profits must register with the financial intelligence unit or a special supervisor, and these rules change. For example, Nigeria's 2022 money laundering law removed non-profits from the list of mandatory reporting entities, though banks may still apply extra checks. Check your local law on this. If you work near areas controlled by sanctioned groups, get specialist advice and talk to your funders first.

## 6. Partners, suppliers and sub-grantees

When we pass funds to a partner, or pay a supplier to act for us, we remain responsible to our funders. We:

- carry out due diligence first, using the Partner Due Diligence Questionnaire;
- include anti-fraud, anti-bribery, sanctions and audit clauses in every Sub-grant Agreement and Services Agreement;
- require partners to report any suspected fraud involving our funds to us within [[48 hours]];
- require partners to keep original receipts and records for [[7 years]] and to give us, our funders and auditors access to them;
- release funds in instalments, tied to financial and activity reports;
- check a sample of partner transactions at least [[once a year]];
- require partners to pass the same rules on to anyone they pay with our funds (this is called flow-down).

## 7. How we prevent fraud

Our main controls are set out in our Financial Controls Policy and Delegation of Authority Matrix. They include:

- **Separation of duties:** the person who requests a payment does not approve it or reconcile the account. Where we are too few, the treasurer reviews instead.
- **Two people for every payment**, and two signatories on every bank account.
- **Accounts in our name**, never in a personal name, including mobile money.
- **Receipts for everything.** Cash given to participants is recorded on a signed or thumbprinted list.
- **Monthly reconciliation** of bank, mobile money and cash, reviewed by [[the treasurer]].
- **Fair procurement** above set limits, following our Procurement Policy.
- **References** for anyone who will handle cash or approve payments.
- **Training** when people join and [[every year]].
- **Fraud risks in our Risk Register**, reviewed before any new project, partner or payment method.

## 8. Warning signs

Warning signs do not prove fraud, but they need a closer look. Report them if you notice:

- missing, altered, photocopied or handwritten receipts where printed ones are normal;
- suppliers with no address or registration number, or several sharing the same contact details;
- quotes that look alike, are always just below an approval limit, or always come from the same group of suppliers;
- payments split into smaller amounts to stay below an approval limit;
- changes to a supplier's or employee's bank or mobile money details, especially by email or message;
- repeated or unknown names on attendance or distribution lists, or many signatures in the same handwriting;
- someone who refuses to take leave or share tasks, or insists on handling a supplier alone;
- late reconciliations or missing bank statements;
- a partner that delays reports, will not share original documents, or asks for funds to be sent to a personal account;
- pressure to make an urgent payment, skip the usual checks, or keep something secret.

Report as soon as you suspect fraud, bribery, corruption or misuse of funds. You do not need proof. Use whichever route you feel safest with:

# Fraud response plan

This plan tells the fraud lead what to do once a concern is reported. If the fraud lead is involved, or has a conflict, the second contact follows it instead.

## Step 1. Receive and record (same day)

1. Open a confidential case record with a reference number.
2. Decide whether funds, people or evidence are at immediate risk.
3. If the concern also involves abuse or exploitation, follow our Safeguarding Policy and Procedure as well.

## Step 2. Secure assets and evidence (within 24 hours)

1. Stop further losses: freeze payments to those concerned, change passwords, and remove access to bank, mobile money and accounting systems.
2. Secure original documents, cash, devices and electronic records. Make dated copies of anything that could change or be deleted.
3. Log who handles each piece of evidence and when. If the police may be involved, ask their advice before handling devices.

## Step 3. Do not tip off

Share the case only with those who need to know. Do not warn the person concerned until evidence is secure. In some countries, tipping off someone about a money laundering report is itself an offence.

## Step 4. Inform the board and funders (within [[24–72 hours]])

1. Tell the [[chair / treasurer]] within [[24 hours]].
2. Check every grant agreement that may be affected. Many require notice within [[24–72 hours]] of first suspicion, not after it is proven.
3. Tell each affected funder in writing within that time: the facts known so far, the amount at risk, steps taken and next steps. Name the suspected person only if the agreement requires it.
4. Tell our insurer, and our bank if accounts may be compromised.

::: adapt Telling funders
List each funder's notice period, contact and format, here or in an annex. Common rules: notice of suspected fraud within 24, 48 or 72 hours; reporting to the funder's investigations office; repaying lost funds; and no use of grant funds to cover losses. Some require notice of attempted fraud even with no loss. If periods differ, meet the shortest. Early, honest notice protects the relationship.
:::

## Step 5. Investigate (aim to finish within [[30 days]])

1. Appoint an investigator independent of the people involved: a board member, an accountant or an external investigator.
2. Agree the scope and time frame in writing.
3. Review documents and transactions, and interview witnesses. Keep signed notes of interviews where possible.
4. Tell the person concerned about the allegation when this will not put evidence or anyone at risk. Give them a fair chance to respond, and let them be accompanied where the law or their contract allows.
5. Decide on the balance of probabilities (whether it is more likely than not) and record the reasons.
6. If the authorities are investigating, cooperate, and agree with them whether our own investigation should pause.

## Step 6. Act and recover

1. Take disciplinary or contractual action following our procedures and the law. This may include dismissal, ending an agreement or removal from the board.
2. Try to recover losses: from the person responsible, through insurance, or through legal proceedings.
3. Repay funders where our agreement requires it, using unrestricted funds, never another funder's grant.
4. Correct our accounting records and any reports already sent to funders.

## Step 7. Report to the authorities

Report to the police, anti-corruption agency or financial intelligence unit where the law requires it or it would help recovery. Record the decision and reasons. Our default is to report confirmed criminal fraud above [[amount and currency]] to the police.

## Step 8. Close and learn (within [[30 days]] of the outcome)

1. Send a final report to the board and affected funders.
2. Fix the control that failed, and update our Risk Register, this policy and training.

# Records, confidentiality and data

- The fraud lead keeps a confidential case register: reference, dates, summary, amount at risk, people informed, funders notified and when, actions taken, outcome, recovery and lessons learned.
- Only the fraud lead, the second contact and board members handling the case can see it.
- We keep case records for [[7 years]] after closure, or longer if legal proceedings are possible, following our Records Retention Schedule and funder rules.
- We handle personal information under our Data Protection Policy, on a need-to-know basis, and never put it into unapproved AI tools.

# If this policy is breached

Anyone who commits fraud, bribery or corruption, helps someone else do so, or knowingly fails to report it, may face:

- disciplinary action, up to and including dismissal, following our procedures and the law;
- the end of a volunteer role, board membership, contract or partnership;
- recovery of losses;
- referral to the police or other authorities.

Knowingly making a false report is also a breach of this policy. Anyone affected by a decision under this policy can ask for it to be reviewed within [[30 days]] by writing to [[Name, role]], who was not involved in the original decision.
