# Who this procedure applies to

This procedure applies to everyone employed by [[Organisation name]], including part-time, fixed-term and probationary employees. Part A covers concerns about conduct. Part B covers grievances employees raise about their own work.

It does not cover redundancy, the end of a fixed-term contract or ill health, which follow the law and a fair process of their own. Poor performance is handled first through support (section 1).

**Other policies come first in some cases.** Harassment, bullying or discrimination complaints follow the Anti-Harassment, Intimidation and Bullying Policy and Procedure. Safeguarding concerns are reported under the Safeguarding Policy and Procedure, and suspected fraud under the Fraud, Bribery and Corruption Policy and Procedure. Wider wrongdoing can be raised under the Whistleblowing Policy and Procedure. If one of those processes finds misconduct, any disciplinary action follows Part A. Its investigation report serves as the investigation in section 5, so the person who raised the concern is not asked to give their account again.

**Volunteers, interns, board members and contractors** are not disciplined as employees. We handle concerns about them fairly under their own agreement: the Volunteer Agreement, the Independent Contractor Agreement, an intern's agreement, or our governing document for board members.

**Law.** [[Organisation name]] employs people in [[country / countries]] and follows the employment law where each employee works. Where this procedure gives less protection than the law, the law applies. Nothing here limits anyone's right to go to a labour authority, conciliation service or court.

::: adapt Law and countries
Dismissal law is strict and local: have a local employment lawyer check this procedure. Country notes to confirm locally:

- **United Kingdom:** follow the Acas Code of Practice on disciplinary and grievance procedures. Tribunals can adjust awards by up to 25% for unreasonable failure to follow it. Workers have a statutory right to be accompanied at formal disciplinary and grievance hearings by a fellow worker or trade union official (Employment Relations Act 1999, section 10). Acas consulted on a revised draft Code from July to September 2026, which stresses informal resolution first and limits suspension: check which version is in force. The Employment Rights Act 2025 cuts the qualifying period for unfair dismissal to six months, for dismissals from 1 January 2027.
- **Kenya:** before terminating for misconduct, poor performance or physical incapacity, the employer must explain the reason in a language the employee understands, allow another employee or shop floor union representative of their choice to be present, and hear their representations (Employment Act 2007, section 41). Courts treat skipping these steps as making a dismissal unfair.
- **South Africa:** a new Code of Good Practice: Dismissal came into force on 4 September 2025, replacing Schedule 8 to the Labour Relations Act 1995 and the separate code on dismissals for operational requirements. For misconduct, the employee must be told the allegations, have time to prepare and be able to respond, and be told the decision. Small employers may use simpler processes. A hearing remains wise if a dismissal may be challenged at the CCMA.
- **Philippines:** dismissal for a just cause follows the twin-notice rule. A first written notice states the specific grounds and facts and gives at least five calendar days to reply. The employee has an ample opportunity to be heard, with a representative if they wish. A second written notice gives the decision and reasons.
- **Uganda:** the Employment Act 2006 (section 66) requires the employer to explain the reason in a language the employee understands, allow a person of their choice to be present, and hear their representations before dismissing for misconduct or poor performance.
- **India:** the four Labour Codes took effect on 21 November 2025. Under the Industrial Relations Code 2020, standing orders apply to establishments with 300 or more workers, and those with 20 or more need a grievance redressal mechanism. Check your state's current rules.
- **Brazil:** dismissal for just cause (justa causa) is possible only on the grounds in Article 482 of the Consolidation of Labour Laws (CLT), and the employer must act promptly.
- **Elsewhere,** for example Nigeria, Ghana, Indonesia, Peru or Argentina: check your local law on notice before dismissal, hearings, representation, warnings and severance.
:::

::: adapt Volunteers, interns, board members and contractors
Keep these groups out of the disciplinary procedure: warnings and hearings can make a volunteer or contractor look like an employee in law. Their agreements should say how concerns are raised and how the arrangement can be paused or ended. Paid interns can be employees in some countries: check your local law on internships.
:::

# Principles

1. **Fairness and consistency.** Similar cases are treated in a similar way, whoever is involved.
2. **Proportionality.** The response matches the seriousness of the conduct, the person's record and any mitigating factors.
3. **Presumption of innocence.** Nobody is treated as having done wrong until the facts are established on the balance of probabilities (whether it is more likely than not).
4. **The right to know and respond.** You are told the allegation in writing, see the evidence and have a real chance to respond before any decision.
5. **The right to be accompanied** at formal meetings, as the law provides and as good practice in all cases.
6. **Reasonable adjustments** for disability, health, language, caring responsibilities or religious observance: for example interpretation, breaks, or a different time, venue or format.
7. **Independence.** Nobody investigates, decides or hears an appeal in a case they are involved in or have a conflict of interest in.
8. **Promptness, confidentiality and no retaliation** against anyone who raises a grievance, helps an investigation or acts as a companion.

# Part A: Disciplinary procedure

## 1. Performance or conduct?

**Performance** is whether someone can do the job to the standard required. **Conduct** is how someone behaves. When performance falls short, the manager explains the concern with examples, listens, and agrees an improvement plan with targets, support and a review date within [[4 to 8 weeks]]. We check whether health, disability or workload play a part. Only if performance does not improve after a fair chance do we use the formal stages below.

## 2. Informal resolution

Minor misconduct, such as occasional lateness or a careless breach of a rule, is usually dealt with in a private conversation. The manager explains what went wrong and what is expected, listens, and keeps a short dated note shared with the employee. This is not a disciplinary sanction. If the conduct continues or is more serious, we use the formal procedure.

## 3. Misconduct and gross misconduct

**Misconduct** is a breach of our Code of Conduct, policies or reasonable instructions that is not serious enough for dismissal on a first occasion: for example repeated lateness, minor breaches of financial or IT rules, or disrespectful behaviour.

**Gross misconduct** is conduct so serious that it may justify dismissal without notice for a first offence, after a fair procedure. Examples:

- sexual exploitation, abuse or harassment of anyone, including any breach of the IASC six core principles in our Code of Conduct;
- abuse, neglect or exploitation of a child or adult at risk, or failing to report a safeguarding concern;
- fraud, theft, bribery, false expense claims, or falsifying records, timesheets or reports;
- serious data breaches: deliberate or reckless disclosure of personal or confidential information, including safeguarding records;
- violence, threats, or serious bullying or harassment;
- retaliating against someone who raised a concern;
- working under the influence of alcohol or drugs;
- serious negligence causing harm or loss;
- false information about qualifications, criminal record or misconduct in a recruitment self-declaration.

::: adapt Gross misconduct list
Check the list against clauses 19 and 23 of your Employment Contract and your Code of Conduct, so all three match. Add risks specific to your work, such as misuse of vehicles or breaches of security rules. In some countries only grounds listed in law justify summary dismissal, for example Kenya (Employment Act 2007, section 44) or Brazil (CLT, Article 482): use the legal wording where it differs.
:::

## 4. Precautionary suspension

If a concern is serious, we may suspend the employee while we investigate, for example where there is a risk to people, evidence or money. We first consider alternatives, such as changing duties or removing access to funds or systems.

Suspension is a neutral act, not a sanction or a finding, and is on full pay and benefits. We confirm it in writing within [[1 working day]], with the reason, expected length, any rules (such as not contacting witnesses) and a named support contact. We review it every [[10 working days]] and keep it short. The safeguarding lead is consulted on any safeguarding case.

## 5. Investigation

Before any hearing, we establish the facts without unreasonable delay. Where possible, the investigator is not the person who will decide the case, and has no involvement in the matter and no close relationship with anyone involved. The investigator:

1. agrees written terms of reference: the allegations, the questions to answer and a target date, usually [[15 working days]];
2. invites the employee to an investigation meeting (Annex A), explains the allegations and invites their account, witnesses and evidence;
3. interviews witnesses separately and keeps notes, which each person can check;
4. collects relevant documents, messages and financial records;
5. writes a short report of the facts found and whether there is a case to answer, without recommending a sanction.

An investigation meeting leads to no sanction. We allow a companion at it [[where the employee asks / in all cases]], even where the law does not require it.

::: adapt Who investigates in a small team
In a small team, everyone may be too close to the case. Options: an uninvolved board member; a manager from a peer organisation, bound by confidentiality; an external HR consultant; or, for serious safeguarding, fraud or senior cases, a specialist investigator. Budget for this in advance, and ask funders whether they must approve the investigator.
:::

## 6. Invitation to a disciplinary hearing

If there is a case to answer, the decision-maker writes to the employee (Annex B) at least [[5 working days]] before the hearing. The letter:

- sets out each allegation, with dates and facts;
- encloses the investigation report and evidence, including witness statements (anonymised only where needed to protect someone);
- says whether the allegations could be gross misconduct and whether dismissal is possible;
- gives the date, time, place or link, and who will attend;
- explains the right to be accompanied, submit evidence and ask for witnesses, and offers adjustments and interpretation.

## 7. The right to be accompanied

At any formal disciplinary or grievance hearing, and at appeals, the employee may bring one companion: [[a colleague, a trade union representative or official, or another person we agree to as a reasonable adjustment]]. The companion may speak, put the employee's case and confer with them, but not answer questions for them. If the companion cannot attend, the employee may propose another time within [[5 working days]]. Colleagues get paid time off to act as companions.

## 8. The hearing

The decision-maker leads the hearing, with a note-taker (Annex F). The employee has a full chance to respond, present evidence, call relevant witnesses and raise mitigating factors. The decision-maker adjourns before deciding, and checks any new points first.

If the employee does not attend without good reason, we rearrange once, then may decide on the evidence and any written submission. If illness is the reason, we offer a written response or an online meeting.

## 9. Outcomes

We give the decision in writing within [[5 working days]] of the hearing (Annex C), with reasons and the right of appeal. Before dismissing anyone, the decision-maker consults the chair, as our Delegation of Authority Matrix requires. Only the board can dismiss the director: board members who investigated the case, or will hear the appeal, take no part in that decision.

{widths: 26 46 28}
| Outcome | When it is used | Stays live for |
|---|---|---|
| **No action** | The allegation is not upheld. We may still give guidance or training. | Not a sanction |
| **First written warning** | Misconduct, or performance that has not improved after support. | [[6 months]] |
| **Final written warning** | Serious misconduct, or further misconduct during a live warning. | [[12 months]] |
| **Dismissal** | Gross misconduct, or further misconduct during a live final warning. With notice, or without notice for gross misconduct. | Not applicable |
| **Alternatives to dismissal** | Where proportionate: demotion, transfer or removal from contact or finance duties, with a final written warning, if the contract and the law allow. | As for the warning |

A warning states the problem, the improvement expected, any support and the likely result of further misconduct. Once expired, it is disregarded for future sanctions. The decision-maker considers the person's record, mitigating factors and how similar cases were treated.

## 10. Appeals

The employee may appeal any warning or dismissal within [[10 working days]], by writing to [[Name, role]] with the grounds: for example unfair procedure, new evidence or a disproportionate sanction. Someone senior to, or independent of, the decision-maker hears it, or a board member or external adviser if nobody inside qualifies.

The appeal hearing takes place within [[10 working days]] of receiving the appeal, with the right to be accompanied. The written decision follows within [[5 working days]] (Annex D). The appeal may confirm, reduce or overturn the sanction, but not increase it. It is final within [[Organisation name]].

## 11. Safeguarding, fraud and criminal matters

**Safeguarding.** When a case arises from a safeguarding concern, the safeguarding lead is involved from the start and advises on suspension and risks. A survivor is never asked to face the employee, and their account is taken once, by the safeguarding lead or an investigator with safeguarding experience. They are not called to the hearing: the employee may put written questions, which the decision-maker passes on only if they are relevant and appropriate. The survivor's identity is protected in the hearing papers where needed. They are told the outcome as far as the law and others' privacy allow.

**Fraud and whistleblowing.** In fraud cases we protect evidence first, for example by removing access to bank accounts. A whistleblower is not called as a witness without their agreement.

**Criminal matters.** Where conduct may be a crime, we follow the law on reporting it and our Safeguarding Policy and Procedure. Our process asks whether our rules were broken, on the balance of probabilities, not whether a crime is proven. Where the law allows, we do not wait for a police or court outcome, especially where people are at risk. We pause if the police ask us to, or if continuing would prejudice a criminal case. A charge or conviction is not by itself a reason for dismissal: we consider whether it affects suitability for the role.

**Resignation during a case.** If an employee resigns while being investigated for sexual exploitation, abuse or harassment, fraud or another serious matter, we complete the investigation where possible and record the outcome.

::: adapt Criminal investigations and mandatory reporting
Take legal advice early in any case involving a possible crime. Some countries require employers to report certain offences, such as sexual offences against children or corruption, within a short time. Check your local law on mandatory reporting, on continuing an internal process during a criminal case, and on how long suspension may last. If an employee is held in custody, check whether local law treats this as incapacity: South Africa's 2025 Code of Good Practice: Dismissal does.
:::

## 12. References and informing others

**References.** We give factual references and follow the principles of the Misconduct Disclosure Scheme, an inter-agency scheme whose signatories check with, and answer checks from, other employers about sexual exploitation, abuse and harassment. Where the law allows, we disclose confirmed findings of sexual exploitation, abuse or harassment, and whether the person left during an investigation. We never present unproven allegations as findings.

**Funders, regulators and partners.** Some funders, regulators and insurers require us to report safeguarding incidents, fraud or serious misconduct, sometimes within 24 to 72 hours, and the outcome. [[Name, role]] makes these reports under the Incident Reporting Procedure, sharing only what is needed.

::: adapt Funders, regulators and references
List each funder, regulator and insurer you must notify about misconduct and their deadlines: read every grant agreement. In England and Wales, registered charities report serious incidents to the Charity Commission. If you join the Misconduct Disclosure Scheme, use its current guidance and forms. Check your local data protection law on misconduct information in references. Never agree a settlement that stops you disclosing a confirmed safeguarding finding.
:::

# Part B: Grievance procedure

A grievance is a concern an employee raises about their own work, working conditions or relationships at work: for example pay, workload, hours, changes to duties, or how a manager treats them.

## 14. Informal first

Most grievances are best raised early. Talk to your manager, or to [[Name, role]] if it is about your manager. They will listen and try to agree a solution within [[10 working days]]. You never have to use the informal route.

## 15. Formal grievance, meeting and outcome

If the informal route does not work, or the matter is serious, write to [[Name, role]] using Annex E or an email, saying what happened and what outcome you want. Someone not involved meets you, with a companion if you wish (section 7), checks any facts confidentially and gives you a written decision with reasons. You can appeal to [[Name, role]], who was not involved.

## 16. Grievances during a disciplinary process

We may pause a disciplinary process briefly to deal with a grievance raised during it, or deal with both together where closely linked. If the grievance alleges bias by the person handling the case, we consider appointing someone else.

## 17. Collective grievances

Where two or more employees share a grievance, they may raise it together or through a trade union or elected representative. We meet the group or its representatives and follow the same steps and timescales. Where we recognise a trade union, any agreed collective procedure applies instead.

## 18. Grievances about the director, founder or board

A grievance about the director, the founder or a board member goes directly to [[Name, role, for example the chair of the board]], or to our independent contact at [[email]]. They handle it under this procedure, with an external adviser if needed. The person concerned takes no part in handling it.

::: adapt When the founder is the manager
In many small organisations every employee reports to the founder. Make sure the grievance route reaches someone the founder does not control: a board chair, an independent board member or an outside independent contact. If you have no board yet, name an external person now and agree their role in writing, as described in the Independent Contact Role Note.
:::

# Records, confidentiality and data

We keep written records of every formal case, including notes, evidence, decisions with reasons and appeals. The employee receives the notes of their meetings and every decision.

Case files are kept in [[a restricted, encrypted folder]], separate from the personnel file, which holds only outcome letters and warnings. We keep records for [[6 years]] after the case closes or the employment ends, whichever is later, or longer if legal proceedings are ongoing or expected. Cases involving a child are kept until the child turns [[25]] or for [[6 years]] after closure, whichever is later. Safeguarding allegations against staff are kept for [[10 years]] or longer, as our Records Retention Schedule sets out, so that we can answer reference requests where the law allows. We then delete records securely, following our Data Protection Policy. We never put case information into AI tools or online services we have not approved, and do not record meetings unless everyone agrees.

::: adapt Records and retention
Check your local employment and data protection law on how long you may keep disciplinary records, employees' rights to see them, and recording meetings. Six years is common where it matches the time limit for legal claims, but limitation periods differ. Add your periods to your Records Retention Schedule, and mention any longer retention of misconduct findings in your Privacy Notice.
:::
