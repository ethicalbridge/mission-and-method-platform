::: adapt Before you use this checklist
Use the full checklist for any grant above [[amount]], any new funder, any grant with partners or sub-grants, and any agreement under foreign law. For small, repeat grants from a funder you know well, a short version is often enough: sections 3, 5 and 7 and the decision box. Do not delete the red flags: they are the point of the checklist. This checklist helps you ask the right questions. It is not legal advice, and for large or unusual agreements you should ask a lawyer to review the terms.

# How to use this checklist

1. Start as soon as you receive the draft agreement. Ask the funder for every document it refers to (general conditions, annexes, funder policies). These form part of the contract.
2. Fill in the grant summary and agree who reviews each section. Give reviewers at least [[5 working days]].
3. Tick each item that is true and that you have checked in the agreement itself, not only in the proposal. Leave an item unticked if the answer is no or unclear.
4. Write any red flag, unticked item or unclear term into the questions for the funder or the negotiation points.
5. Ask the funder your questions in writing and keep the answers in the grant file.
6. Record the decision, the reasons and the signatures in the decision box. Sign the agreement only after that.
7. After signing, complete the set-up list at the end.

# The checklist

## 1. Fit with mission and strategy

[ ] The funded work falls within the purposes in our governing document and the countries where we are registered to work.
[ ] It fits our current strategy and theory of change, and we can explain in two sentences why we would do this work anyway.
[ ] The communities we serve, or our partners, helped shape the activities.
[ ] We have the skills and experience to deliver it, or a realistic plan to get them.
[ ] Taking this grant will not pull people away from commitments we have already made to other funders or communities.
[ ] We know what happens when the grant ends: the work stops responsibly, continues with other funding, or passes to a partner.

#### RED FLAGS

- We changed our plans to fit the funder's priorities, not the needs we exist to meet (mission drift).
- The grant would make this funder more than [[40%]] of our annual income.
- The work is outside our registered purposes or in a country where we are not registered.
- We are under pressure to sign before we have had time to read the agreement.

## 2. The funder

[ ] We know the funder's full legal name, legal form, country and the person who will sign for it.
[ ] We know where the money comes from. If the funder is passing on money from another donor (for example a government or a larger foundation), we know which of that donor's conditions apply to us.
[ ] We have screened the funder against the sanctions lists in our Partner Due Diligence and Sanctions Screening Policy and recorded the result.
[ ] We have checked the funder's reputation: a news search, its website and published grants, and a conversation with at least one other grantee where possible.
[ ] Accepting money from this funder is consistent with our Gift Acceptance Policy and our values.
[ ] Every board member and staff member involved has declared any link with the funder, as our Conflict of Interest Policy requires.
[ ] We understand any restrictions on our work: on advocacy or public statements, political or religious activity, working with certain groups or in certain places, or taking money from other funders.
[ ] Where our law requires approval to receive foreign funds, we hold it, and it covers this funder and this purpose.

#### RED FLAGS

- The funder, its owners or its directors appear on a sanctions list, or face credible reports of fraud, corruption or human rights abuses.
- The funder's business harms the communities we serve, or conflicts with our mission.
- Terms that limit what we can say in public, or that make us less independent.
- A request for a fee, a "processing charge" or bank details before any agreement is signed. This is a common grant scam.
- The source of the money is unclear, or payments would come from a third party or a personal account.

::: adapt Checking funders
Keep the check proportionate. A well-known government agency or a long-established foundation usually needs only a sanctions check and a reading of its grant terms. Companies, private individuals, new foundations and intermediaries need more: who owns or controls them, where the money comes from and what they gain from the grant. Screen against the UN Security Council consolidated list and the lists your other funders use (often US OFAC, EU and UK). Several countries regulate foreign funding closely, for example India under the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act 2010. The Legal Review Checklist covers this in more detail. Check your local law on foreign funding before you sign.
:::

## 3. The money

### Amount, currency and exchange risk

[ ] The amount in the agreement matches the budget we submitted, or we have agreed every change in writing.
[ ] We know the grant currency, the currency we spend in, and the exchange rate method for reporting (for example the rate on the day funds arrive, a monthly average or the funder's own rate).
[ ] We know who carries exchange losses, and whether exchange gains must be spent on the project or returned.
[ ] Our bank can receive the grant currency, and we have budgeted for transfer and conversion fees.

### Payments and cash flow

[ ] We know whether the funder pays in advance, in tranches or by reimbursement after we spend, and what triggers each payment.
[ ] We know whether part of the grant is held back until the final report is approved, and how much.
[ ] Our cash flow forecast shows we can pay staff and suppliers between payments, even if a payment arrives [[2 months]] late.
[ ] If the funder pays in arrears, we have reserves or bridging funds to cover the gap, within our Reserves Policy.
[ ] Any match funding or co-funding requirement is clear: the amount, whether in-kind contributions count, and the deadline. We have secured it, or have a realistic plan.

### Costs covered

[ ] The budget covers the full cost of the work: direct costs and a fair share of core costs such as rent, finance, management, audit, insurance and IT.
[ ] We know the funder's rule on indirect costs (overheads), how the rate is calculated, and whether it covers our real core costs.
[ ] Staff costs include the true cost of each person: salary, employer contributions, benefits and any end-of-contract payments required by law.
[ ] We know which costs are not eligible (for example taxes we can recover, fines, entertainment, costs before the start date) and which need the funder's prior approval.
[ ] We have checked whether any part of the grant could count as payment for services and so attract VAT or other tax.

### Budget changes

[ ] We know how much we can move between budget lines without approval (often a set percentage per line), and how to ask for larger changes.
[ ] We know how to ask for a no-cost extension if the work takes longer, and how far ahead we must ask.

### Interest, unspent funds and refunds

[ ] We know whether we may keep interest earned on advance payments, must spend it on the project, or must report or return it.
[ ] We know what happens to unspent funds at the end: return them, keep them, or use them for agreed activities.
[ ] We know when the funder can ask for money back (clawback), for example for ineligible costs, breach of the agreement or missed targets, and for how long after the grant ends.
[ ] We know whether we must hold the grant in a separate bank account, or only under a separate cost code.

#### RED FLAGS

- Payment only by reimbursement, and our reserves would cover less than [[3 months]] of grant costs.
- No contribution to core costs, or an indirect rate well below our real rate, with no other funding to cover the gap.
- The funder can claw back money for missed targets that depend on things outside our control.
- We would have to refund money a partner misused, without limit, even where we followed every agreed check.
- Match funding is required but not yet secured.
- All exchange losses fall on us, in a currency that moves a lot.

::: adapt Full cost recovery and indirect rates
"Full cost recovery" means each grant pays its fair share of the core costs that make the work possible. Before you negotiate, work out your real indirect rate: total core costs divided by total direct project costs over a year. Funders set different rules. For example, under the US federal Uniform Guidance (2 CFR 200.414), an organisation without a negotiated rate may charge a de minimis indirect rate of up to 15% of modified total direct costs since the 2024 revisions. Many foundations set their own cap, and some now fund core costs in full. If the funder caps overheads, ask to include some core costs as direct budget lines, such as a share of the finance officer's time or the audit fee. Check the funder's current policy rather than relying on these examples.
:::

::: adapt Currency and exchange risk
If you work in a currency that loses value quickly, exchange rates can wipe out a budget line or leave you with unexplained gains. Options: ask the funder to pay in local currency; include an exchange contingency line; agree a fixed or average reporting rate; or agree that gains can be spent on project activities. Some countries require foreign currency to be converted on arrival or restrict holding foreign currency accounts. Check your local foreign exchange rules with your bank before you agree a payment method.
:::

## 4. Delivery

[ ] The outputs, outcomes and targets in the agreement match our proposal, and any changes made by the funder are ones we agree with.
[ ] The targets are realistic for the budget, the team and the context, and we know our starting point (baseline).
[ ] The timeline allows for start-up: recruitment, procurement, permits, community agreement, seasons, holidays and elections.
[ ] The people named in the proposal are still available, and staff contracts can match the grant period.
[ ] We know whether we need the funder's approval to replace key staff named in the agreement.
[ ] Every partner has seen and agreed its activities, budget and conditions, and has passed our partner due diligence.
[ ] Sub-grants are allowed, we know whether each one needs the funder's approval, and we can pass on the funder's conditions through our Sub-grant Agreement.
[ ] We know whether we must follow the funder's procurement rules or our own Procurement Policy, and we can apply them.
[ ] We know who owns equipment and other assets bought with the grant, and what happens to them at the end.
[ ] The main risks to delivery are in our Risk Register with actions to reduce them.

#### RED FLAGS

- Targets were raised to win the grant and the team does not believe they are achievable.
- The start date has already passed, or the timeline assumes staff we have not yet recruited.
- A partner has not seen the terms that will apply to it.
- Procurement or approval rules we cannot run with our current team.

## 5. Reporting and evidence

[ ] We know the deadline and format for every narrative and financial report, in which language and currency.
[ ] We know which indicators we must report on, our MEAL system can produce them, and the cost of collecting the data is in the budget.
[ ] We know what breakdown the funder expects (for example by sex, age, disability or location), and we can collect it safely.
[ ] We know whether an evaluation is required, who chooses the evaluator and who pays.
[ ] We know whether the funder requires a project audit or expenditure verification, spot checks or visits, and the cost is in the budget.
[ ] We can provide the evidence the funder expects for costs (receipts, timesheets, attendance lists, photos) in the places where we work.
[ ] We know how long we must keep grant records after the grant ends, and our Records Retention Schedule reflects it.
[ ] We know how quickly we must tell the funder about serious incidents such as fraud, safeguarding concerns or security incidents.
[ ] The reporting burden is proportionate to the size of the grant.

#### RED FLAGS

- Monthly or quarterly reports for a small grant, with no budget for the work involved.
- A requirement to send the funder personal data about the people we serve, such as names or photos, without a clear reason.
- Audit or verification required, with no budget for it.
- Incident reporting deadlines we cannot meet, for example within [[24 hours]] from remote areas.

::: adapt Record retention periods
Grant agreements set their own retention periods, and they often run from the final report or final payment, not from the end of activities. For example, US federal awards generally require records to be kept for three years from the submission of the final financial report (2 CFR 200.334), with exceptions. Other funders, including EU-funded programmes, set periods in the agreement itself. Your local accounting and tax law may require longer. Keep financial records for the longest period that applies, but keep personal data only as long as you need it, as your data protection law requires.
:::

## 6. Legal terms

[ ] We know which country's law governs the agreement, and how disputes are settled (negotiation, mediation, arbitration or courts), and where.
[ ] Our liability is limited (for example to the amount of the grant) and excludes indirect losses.
[ ] Any indemnity we give is limited to losses caused by our own breach or negligence, and our insurance covers it where possible.
[ ] We know who will own what the grant produces (reports, training materials, data, software, photos), what licence the funder gets, and that we can keep using it.
[ ] Materials we created before the grant stay ours.
[ ] We can meet the branding and visibility rules, and we can ask for an exception where showing the funder's logo could put people at risk.
[ ] We know what personal data the funder may ask for, our lawful basis to share it, and whether we need a Data Sharing Agreement.
[ ] We can meet the safeguarding clause, including its standards and reporting deadlines, through our Safeguarding Policy and Procedure.
[ ] We understand the anti-fraud, anti-bribery, sanctions and counter-terrorism clauses: who we must screen, what we must report, and what we must pass on to partners.
[ ] We know when each party can end the agreement, the notice required, and whether the funder will pay costs we have already committed, such as staff notice periods and leases.
[ ] We know when the funder can suspend payments, and what happens to costs during a suspension.
[ ] Changes to the agreement must be made in writing and signed by both parties.
[ ] We have read every document the agreement refers to, including general conditions and funder policies.
[ ] Someone on the team can read and understand the full agreement in the language it is written in.

#### RED FLAGS

- An unlimited indemnity, or liability for losses we could not control.
- The funder owns everything, including our existing materials and data about the people we serve.
- A requirement to screen the people we serve against sanctions lists.
- The funder can end the agreement at any time without paying costs we have already committed.
- Documents referred to in the agreement that we have not been given.
- Confidentiality terms that would stop us reporting a concern to a regulator.

::: adapt Governing law and disputes
Funders usually choose the law and courts of their own country. This is often hard to change, but you can ask for negotiation and mediation before any formal dispute, or for neutral arbitration. Some international organisations, such as UN agencies, generally do not accept any national law or courts because of their privileges and immunities, and use arbitration instead. If the agreement is above [[amount]], lasts more than [[2 years]], or contains an indemnity or unusual terms, ask a lawyer to review it before you sign.
:::

::: adapt Counter-terrorism and sanctions clauses
These clauses commonly require you to make sure that no funds reach listed people or groups, to screen staff, partners and suppliers, and to pass the clause on to partners. FATF Recommendation 8 expects a risk-based approach that does not disrupt legitimate charitable work. UN Security Council Resolution 2664 (2022) created a humanitarian exemption to asset freezes under UN sanctions regimes; check whether it applies to your work and whether your funder's clause reflects it. Most funders do not expect you to screen the people you serve, so ask the funder to remove or clarify any wording that suggests this. Align what you agree with your Partner Due Diligence and Sanctions Screening Policy.
:::

## 7. Approvals and signing

[ ] The person who will sign is authorised to do so under our Delegation of Authority Matrix for this amount, duration and type of agreement.
[ ] Board approval is in place where the grant is above [[amount and currency]], above [[25%]] of annual income, longer than [[3 years]], in a new country or area of work, or carries high risk.
[ ] The signatory has no conflict of interest in this grant, or has declared it and stepped out.
[ ] Any government approval or registration needed to receive or use the funds is in place.
[ ] The documents the funder asked for (registration, bank letter, policies, budget) are accurate and current.
[ ] The version to be signed is the version we reviewed, with all agreed changes, and every page and annex is included.

#### RED FLAGS

- Pressure to sign from someone without authority, or before the board has approved.
- A signature page sent on its own, without the final text.
- Changes made by the funder after our review that nobody has checked.

::: adapt Delegation and board thresholds
Set the thresholds in your Delegation of Authority Matrix and copy them here so reviewers see them. Common triggers for board approval are a grant above a fixed amount, a grant above a share of annual income, a new funder type, a new country or a high-risk context, or terms such as an unlimited indemnity. Some governing documents or local laws require two signatures, a board resolution or a company seal on contracts. Check your governing document and your local law.
:::

# Guidance notes

- **Do not sign on the day you receive the agreement.** Funders expect a review, and most will wait a few days for good questions.
- **Negotiating is normal.** Asking for an advance, a fair share of core costs or a clearer clause is professional, not ungrateful. Ask early, in writing, and explain why.
- **Read the agreement, not only the budget.** Most problems come from general conditions and annexes that nobody read.
- **Think about the end from the start.** Refunds, retention periods and staff contracts often matter most after the money is spent.
- **It is fine to say no.** A grant that costs more than it brings, or pulls you away from your mission, weakens the organisation. Thank the funder and explain your reasons.
- **Keep the record.** Auditors and future funders may ask why you accepted a grant and who approved it. This checklist is your answer.
