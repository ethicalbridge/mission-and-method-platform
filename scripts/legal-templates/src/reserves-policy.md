# Who this policy applies to

This policy applies to the board, the director and the finance lead. It covers all unrestricted funds held by [[Organisation name]], in every currency and account.

**Law.** [[Organisation name]] is registered in [[country]] as a [[legal form]]. We follow any rules our regulator, our governing document or our funders set on reserves. Where these are stricter than this policy, we follow them.

::: adapt Law and regulators
Check what your regulator expects. For example, in England and Wales the Charity Commission expects charities to have a reserves policy and to explain it in their annual report (see its guidance on charity reserves, CC19). In other countries there may be no specific rule, but regulators, tax authorities or funders may ask why you hold large balances, or may limit surpluses kept by tax-exempt bodies. Some funders do not allow grant funds to be used to build reserves, but do allow a fair share of core costs. Check your local law and grant agreements.
:::

# Why we hold reserves

We hold reserves so that we can:

- **keep going when income is late**, for example when a grant payment is delayed by weeks or months;
- **manage the loss of a funder**, giving us time to find new funding or to reduce our work in a planned, responsible way;
- **meet our legal and moral duties if we have to close** part or all of our work: staff notice and termination pay, contracts and commitments to communities and partners;
- **respond to emergencies**, such as a security incident, a legal claim or a sudden repair;
- **cover losses** from exchange rate changes, or costs a funder refuses to pay;
- **pay first and claim later**, where funders pay in arrears;
- **take up opportunities**, such as matching funds or a pilot, without putting our core work at risk.

Holding too much has costs too: money in the bank is not being used for our mission. We aim for a balance and explain it openly.

# What we mean

{widths: 28 72}
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Restricted funds | Money given for a specific purpose set by the funder or donor. We can only use it for that purpose, so it is never part of our reserves. |
| Unrestricted funds | Money we can use for any of our purposes, as the board decides. |
| Designated funds | Unrestricted funds the board has set aside for a specific future purpose, such as replacing a vehicle. The board can undo a designation, but while it stands the money is not available as reserves. |
| Fixed assets used for our work | Equipment, vehicles, furniture or buildings we use to deliver our work, shown at their net book value (cost less depreciation). They are not cash and we could not sell them without harming our work. |
| Free reserves | The part of our unrestricted funds that we could actually spend. **Free reserves = unrestricted funds − designated funds − fixed assets used for our work.** |
| Core costs | The costs of running the organisation that continue whatever projects we run: core staff, rent, utilities, insurance, audit, finance and governance. |
| Months of cover | Free reserves divided by monthly core costs. It tells us how long we could keep the core running with no new income. |

::: adapt Definitions and your accounts
Ask your accountant to confirm these figures from your balance sheet and notes to the accounts. If some of your unrestricted funds are not cash, for example money owed to you by a funder, check that your free reserves are also available as cash or could be within a few weeks. If you hold loans or other long-term commitments, ask your accountant whether to deduct them too. Decide whether “core costs” means only your central costs or your total running costs; using total costs gives a more cautious target.
:::

# Policy

## 1. Our reserves target

We set our target using two methods and take the higher figure.

### Method 1: months of core costs

We aim to hold free reserves equal to between [[3]] and [[6]] months of core costs. The lower figure is our minimum. The upper figure is our maximum, unless the board decides there is a specific reason to hold more.

### Method 2: risk-based

We list the main financial risks we face, estimate how much each would cost us if it happened, and add up the amounts we would need to meet from reserves at the same time. We use our Risk Register to identify the risks. Section 2 shows the table we use.

::: adapt Choosing a target
Many small organisations aim for between three and six months of core costs, but there is no single right answer. Hold more if your income depends on one or two funders, if grants are often paid late, if you pay staff under contracts with long notice periods, or if you work in an unstable currency. Hold less if your income is varied, regular and paid in advance. The risk-based method gives a figure you can explain to your board and funders. If you are just starting, set a modest first target, such as one month of core costs, and raise it as you grow.
:::

## 2. How we calculate the risk-based figure

Every year, when we prepare the budget, the finance lead and treasurer complete this table and present it to the board.

{widths: 40 30 30}
| Risk | How we estimate it | Amount needed |
|---|---|---|
| Delay of grant payments | [[Number]] months of core costs | [[amount]] |
| Loss of our largest funder | Staff notice and termination pay, and contract exit costs, for the work it funds | [[amount]] |
| Closing in an orderly way, if we had to | Notice and termination pay, final audit, rent to the end of the lease, commitments to partners | [[amount]] |
| Exchange rate losses | [[Percentage]] of grants received in foreign currency | [[amount]] |
| Costs a funder may refuse to pay | [[Percentage]] of grant spending, based on past experience | [[amount]] |
| Emergencies (security, legal, repairs) | Our best estimate | [[amount]] |
| **Total risk-based figure** | | **[[amount]]** |

Not every risk will happen at once. The board may reduce the total to reflect how likely each risk is, and records its reasons. The orderly closure cost is our absolute minimum: we should always be able to meet our obligations to staff, partners and communities if we had to stop.

## 3. Where we hold reserves

- Reserves are held in [[a separate savings or deposit account]] in the name of [[Organisation name]], so that they are not used for day-to-day spending by mistake.
- We hold them in low-risk accounts that we can access within [[30 days]]. We do not invest reserves in shares, property, cryptocurrency or loans to individuals.
- We hold them in [[currency]], or in [[a mix of local and foreign currency]] where the law allows, to protect against devaluation.

::: adapt Currency and banks
If your local currency loses value quickly, holding part of your reserves in a stronger currency can protect them, but check your country's foreign exchange rules first: some countries restrict foreign currency accounts. Consider spreading reserves across more than one bank if you hold large amounts, and ask whether your country has a deposit protection scheme and its limit.
:::

## 5. Building our reserves

If our free reserves are below target, we will build them by:

1. budgeting for an unrestricted surplus of [[amount or percentage of income]] each year;
2. including a fair share of core costs in every grant budget, as each funder allows, so that grants pay for the costs they cause;
3. raising unrestricted income, for example from individual donors, membership, events or earned income;
4. putting [[a percentage, e.g. 50%]] of any unexpected unrestricted income into reserves;
5. reviewing our progress every [[quarter]].

We aim to reach the minimum target by [[date]] and the full target by [[date]].

## 6. Using our reserves

We use reserves only for the purposes set out in “Why we hold reserves”. We do not use reserves to cover ongoing losses without a plan to fix them.

{widths: 40 60}
| Use of reserves | Who authorises |
|---|---|
| Up to [[amount and currency]], to bridge a delayed grant payment that is confirmed in writing | Director, with the treasurer's agreement; reported to the next board meeting |
| Up to [[USD 2,000]] in an emergency, as in our Delegation of Authority Matrix | Director; tells the chair within [[48 hours]] |
| Any other use, or above those amounts | Board |

Every decision to use reserves is recorded, with the reason and a plan to replenish them, usually within [[12 months]]. Money used to bridge a late grant is returned to reserves when the grant arrives. These limits also appear in our Delegation of Authority Matrix.

## 7. If reserves are above the maximum

If free reserves stay above our maximum target for [[two]] consecutive years, the board decides how to use the surplus for our mission. It may designate funds for specific plans, such as a new programme, staff development or equipment, and explains its decision in our annual report.

## 8. If reserves fall below the minimum

If free reserves fall below the minimum target:

1. the finance lead tells the director and treasurer straight away;
2. the director prepares a recovery plan within [[30 days]], covering extra income, cost savings or a planned reduction of activities;
3. the board approves the plan and reviews progress at every meeting until the minimum is restored;
4. if reserves fall below the cost of an orderly closure, the board takes urgent advice on whether the organisation can continue and meet its debts as they fall due, and on its legal duties.

::: adapt When reserves run low
The board's duties when an organisation may not be able to pay its debts differ from country to country. In some, board members can become personally liable if they carry on when they knew, or should have known, that the organisation could not pay its debts. Check your local law on insolvency for your legal form, and take advice early.
:::

# Reporting and review

- The finance lead reports free reserves, months of cover and progress against target to the board every [[quarter]], as part of the management accounts described in our Financial Controls Policy.
- The board reviews the target and this policy every year, when it approves the budget or the annual accounts, and whenever our income, risks or size change significantly.
- We explain our reserves policy and position in our annual report or accounts. The annex gives example wording.

# Records, confidentiality and data

We keep each year's reserves calculation, board decisions on reserves and records of every use of reserves with our financial records for at least [[7 years]], following our Records Retention Schedule.

# If this policy is breached

Using reserves without the approval this policy requires is a breach of our Financial Controls Policy and our Delegation of Authority Matrix. The treasurer reports it to the board, which decides what action to take.

# Annex: Worked example

This example uses an imaginary organisation. **All numbers are illustrative.** Replace them with figures from your own accounts.

The organisation spends USD 240,000 a year. Of this, USD 96,000 is core costs, or USD 8,000 a month.

### Step 1: calculate free reserves

{widths: 60 40}
| Item | USD |
|---|---|
| Total funds at year end | 140,000 |
| Less: restricted funds | 85,000 |
| Unrestricted funds | 55,000 |
| Less: designated fund for replacing the vehicle | 10,000 |
| Less: fixed assets used for our work (net book value) | 7,000 |
| **Free reserves** | **38,000** |
| Months of cover (38,000 ÷ 8,000) | 4.75 months |

### Step 2: Method 1, months of core costs

Minimum: 3 months × USD 8,000 = USD 24,000. Maximum: 6 months × USD 8,000 = USD 48,000.

### Step 3: Method 2, risk-based figure

{widths: 60 40}
| Risk | USD |
|---|---|
| Delay of grant payments: 3 months of core costs | 24,000 |
| Loss of the largest funder: notice pay and contract exit costs | 12,000 |
| Emergencies | 5,000 |
| Exchange rate losses: 2% of USD 200,000 of foreign currency grants | 4,000 |
| Costs a funder may refuse to pay | 3,000 |
| **Total** | **48,000** |

The board also estimates the cost of an orderly closure at USD 20,000. Free reserves are well above this absolute minimum.

### Step 4: set the target and plan

The higher figure is USD 48,000, equal to 6 months of core costs. The board sets this as the target. Free reserves are USD 38,000, so the gap is USD 10,000. The organisation is above its minimum of USD 24,000, so there is no immediate concern.

The board agrees to close the gap over two years by budgeting an unrestricted surplus of USD 5,000 a year, mainly by recovering a fair share of core costs from two new grants and launching a small monthly giving scheme.

### Example wording for the annual report

“The board aims to hold free reserves of between three and six months of core costs, and has set a target of USD 48,000 based on an assessment of our main financial risks. At [[year end]], free reserves were USD 38,000, equal to 4.75 months of core costs. This is above our minimum but below our target. We plan to close the gap over the next two years through a budgeted unrestricted surplus. Restricted funds of USD 85,000 are not included, because they can only be used for the purposes set by our funders.”
