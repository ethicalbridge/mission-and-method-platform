The chair usually owns the matrix because it drives recruitment and succession. If the chair is new, or the board is reviewing the chair's own role, another experienced member or a nominations group can lead. The founder should not keep the matrix alone: it is the board's tool for reviewing its own strength.

::: glance
- We list the skills, experience and perspectives our board needs, based on our strategy and risks.
- Each member scores themselves from 0 to 3 for each area. The chair checks the scores for consistency.
- We record diversity only in broad, anonymous terms, and only with members' consent.
- A gap summary shows where we are strong and where we depend on one person or have no one.
- We use the gaps to write role descriptions, advertise vacancies and plan training.
- We review the matrix each year, alongside members' terms of office.
:::

# Purpose

This matrix helps the board to:

- check that, together, members have the knowledge needed to govern [[Organisation name]] well;
- see where we rely on a single person, so that we are not exposed if they leave;
- plan recruitment openly, based on what the board lacks rather than on who we already know;
- plan training for current members;
- show funders and regulators that board composition is managed deliberately.

The matrix looks at the board as a whole. No member is expected to score highly in every area. A board member with lived experience of the issues we work on brings expertise as important as a qualified accountant's.

# How to use it

1. **Agree the areas.** Each year the board checks the list in Part 1 against our strategy and the top risks in the Risk Register, and sets the level the board needs in each area.
2. **Score.** Each member scores themselves in Part 2, using the scale below. New members score themselves when they join.
3. **Check.** The chair reviews the scores with each member if they look unusually high or low. The aim is an honest picture, not a competition.
4. **Summarise.** The secretary completes the gap summary in Part 5.
5. **Discuss and decide.** The board discusses the gaps at its annual review and agrees actions: recruit, train, co-opt an adviser, or accept the gap for now.
6. **Act and record.** The chair records actions in the decisions and actions log of the board minutes and reports progress at each meeting.

# Scoring scale

{widths: 12 22 66}
| Score | Level | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | None | No knowledge or experience in this area |
| 1 | Basic | Understands the main ideas; can follow a discussion and ask simple questions |
| 2 | Good | Some practical experience; can question proposals and spot problems |
| 3 | Strong | Substantial professional, paid, voluntary or lived experience; can guide the board and challenge management with confidence |

::: adapt Scoring method
Self-assessment is quick and works for most small boards. To make scores more consistent, give one or two examples of what a "3" looks like for each area, or ask the chair to agree scores with each member. Count experience from any source: voluntary roles, community leadership and lived experience count as much as formal jobs. Some boards also score "willingness to develop" in an area, to plan training.
:::

# Part 3: Diversity and representation

A board that reflects the communities we serve makes better decisions and earns more trust. We look at representation as well as skills. We record it **only in broad, anonymous terms for the board as a whole**, and only with members' consent. No member has to share any personal characteristic.

{widths: 26 22 22 30}
| Consideration | Current board (whole board) | Our aim | Notes and actions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lived experience of the issues we work on | Example: 1 of 5 | At least [[2]] | Example: reserve the next vacancy for a member from our community advisory group |
| Based in the countries where we work | Example: 3 of 5 | [[Most members]] | Example: none needed |
| Gender | Example: 2 women, 3 men | [[Balanced; no more than 60% of any gender]] | Example: encourage applications from women for the treasurer role |
| Age | Example: no member under 35 | At least [[one]] member under [[35]] | Example: invite a young leader from our programmes to observe for a year |
| Languages spoken | Example: 3 of our 4 working languages | All working languages | Example: recruit a [[language]] speaker |
| Disability and accessibility | Not recorded unless members choose to share | Accessible to all | Example: offer papers in accessible formats and paid support to attend |
| [[Other, e.g. region, ethnicity or faith, where relevant and lawful]] | [[ ]] | [[ ]] | [[ ]] |

::: adapt Diversity data and the law
Collect diversity information only if you will use it, explain why, make it voluntary, and report it only as totals for the whole board. Many data protection laws treat ethnic origin, religion, health (including disability) and sexual orientation as sensitive data that needs extra protection: for example the GDPR (as a widely used benchmark), South Africa's POPIA, the Kenya Data Protection Act 2019, the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 and Brazil's LGPD. In some places, recording ethnicity, faith or political views can put people at risk: leave those categories out unless there is a clear reason. Follow your Data Protection Policy and Equality, Diversity and Inclusion Policy.
:::

::: adapt Composition rules in law and funding
Check whether your governing document, local law, regulator or funders set rules on board composition, for example a minimum number of citizens or residents, a share of independent members, gender balance, or seats for members or beneficiaries. These rules vary widely, so check your local law and your funding agreements, and add a row for each rule that applies to you.
:::

# Part 5: Gap summary

The secretary completes this summary from Parts 1, 2 and 4. The example rows below use the example scores in Table 2a.

{widths: 24 12 14 16 34}
| Area | Level needed | Highest score | Members at 2 or 3 | Gap and action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example: Safeguarding | 3 | 2 | 1 | **Gap.** Recruit a member with safeguarding experience; meanwhile, the chair and [[C. Member]] attend safeguarding training for boards by [[date]] |
| Example: Finance | 3 | 3 | 1 | **Single point of dependence.** Strong treasurer, but no back-up. Finance training for one other member |
| Example: Lived experience and community | 3 | 3 | 1 | **At risk.** [[C. Member]]'s term ends in [[2026]]. Recruit before they leave |
| Example: Legal and compliance | 2 | 2 | 1 | **Met.** Use pro bono legal advice for specific questions |
| Example: Communications | 1 | 3 | 1 | **Met.** No action |
| [[ ]] | [[ ]] | [[ ]] | [[ ]] | [[ ]] |

Read the summary this way:

- **Gap:** the highest score is below the level needed.
- **Single point of dependence:** only one member reaches the level needed. If they leave, the board has a gap.
- **At risk:** the only strong member's term ends within [[12 months]].
- **Met:** at least two members score 2 or more, or the level needed is reached and there is no term risk.

# Using the matrix in recruitment

1. Choose the [[one or two]] most important gaps from Part 5. Do not try to fill every gap with one person.
2. Write the vacancy using the Board Member Role Description, adding a short "what we are looking for" section based on those gaps.
3. Advertise openly, [[through our website, partners, community networks, professional bodies and volunteer platforms]], in [[language(s)]], and not only through personal contacts.
4. Ask candidates to score themselves against the areas you are recruiting for, with examples of their experience.
5. Interview with at least two board members. Check eligibility, take references and complete a Board Interest Declaration under our Safer Recruitment Procedure.
6. After appointment, add the new member to the matrix and update the gap summary.

::: adapt Filling gaps without recruiting
Recruitment is not the only answer. You can train an existing member; co-opt an adviser to a committee without a vote (if your governing document allows it); set up a community advisory group whose chair attends board meetings; or use pro bono advice for specific questions. A small board of five cannot hold every skill at level 3. Focus on the areas linked to your top risks.
:::

# Guidance notes

- **Be honest, not modest or generous.** A matrix full of 3s helps no one. Encourage members to score what they can actually do on the board today.
- **Value every kind of expertise.** Lived experience and community knowledge are skills, not a diversity tick box. Score them in the same way as finance or law.
- **Look for single points of dependence.** The most common risk on small boards is one person holding all the finance or all the safeguarding knowledge.
- **Link it to strategy.** If your strategy changes, for example a new country or a new income model, update the areas and levels.
- **Keep it confidential and keep it.** Store individual scores with restricted access, for the period in our Records Retention Schedule. Keep the approved gap summary with the board papers, so you can show how the board has changed over time.
