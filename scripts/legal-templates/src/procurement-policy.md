# Who this policy applies to

This policy applies to staff, volunteers, interns and board members who request, approve, make or pay for purchases for [[Organisation name]]. It also applies to consultants who buy on our behalf, and to partners and sub-grantees when they spend funds we pass to them, unless their own written procurement rules are at least as strict and we have agreed to rely on them.

It covers all goods, services and works paid for with our funds, such as equipment, venue hire, catering, transport, software, consultants and repairs. It does not cover salaries and employment contracts, which follow our recruitment process, or expenses claimed by individuals, which follow our Expenses and Per Diem Policy.

**Law and stricter rules.** [[Organisation name]] is registered in [[country]] and follows its laws, including tax, invoicing and anti-corruption law. When we buy in other countries we also follow the law there. Where a funder's grant agreement sets stricter procurement rules, those rules apply to every purchase charged to that grant. Where rules differ, we follow the stricter one.

::: adapt Law and funders' rules
Name the country where you are registered and any others where you buy. Public procurement laws, such as Kenya's Public Procurement and Asset Disposal Act 2015, usually apply to government bodies rather than to NGOs, but some apply when you spend public money or act for a public body. Check your local law on this, and on invoice and tax receipt rules (for example, whether suppliers must issue official tax invoices and whether you must withhold tax on payments to individuals). Government funders often have strict rules. US federal awards follow the procurement standards in 2 CFR 200.317 to 200.327 (the Uniform Guidance), with thresholds linked to US federal acquisition rules. EU-funded grants for work outside the EU usually include procurement rules in an annex to the grant contract. UK government funders set their own requirements in grant terms. Always read the procurement clause of each grant agreement and record its thresholds in the table in section 3.
:::

# Policy

## 1. Our principles

1. **Value for money.** We buy what we need, of the right quality, at the right time, at the best total cost. We consider price, quality, delivery time, running costs, after-sales support and how long the item will last. The cheapest offer is not always the best value.
2. **Fairness.** We give suppliers an equal chance. We share the same information with all of them, judge them against the same criteria and do not favour anyone because of a personal connection.
3. **Transparency.** We record what we asked for, who we asked, what they offered and why we chose one. Anyone checking the file later can follow the decision.
4. **Ethics.** We never offer or accept bribes, kickbacks or improper gifts. We declare conflicts of interest. We do not buy from suppliers that use child labour or forced labour, or that we know abuse workers' rights.
5. **Sustainability.** Where it makes sense and is affordable, we choose goods that last, can be repaired, use less energy or create less waste, as set out in our Environmental Policy.
6. **Local suppliers.** Where quality and value are comparable, we prefer suppliers based in the communities where we work, including businesses led by women, young people or people from under-represented groups. This keeps money in local economies. It never overrides fairness or value for money.

::: adapt Local and ethical preferences
Decide how strong your local preference should be. Options: a tie-breaker only (shown above); a small scoring weight, for example 5–10%; or a rule to invite at least one local supplier every time. If your funder requires open international competition above a certain value, a local preference may not be allowed for that grant. Check before you apply it.
:::

## 2. Plan before you buy

Before buying anything above [[amount]], the person requesting it:

- confirms the item is needed and is in the approved budget or grant budget line;
- checks whether we already have it, or can borrow, share or reuse it;
- writes a short description of what is needed: quantity, quality, delivery date and place;
- for services, writes short terms of reference: tasks, deadlines and deliverables.

At the start of each year or grant, the procurement lead lists the main planned purchases, so that larger ones start early.

## 3. Thresholds and quotes

The total value of a purchase decides the process. Value means the full amount we expect to pay, including taxes, delivery and installation, over the whole period of the order or contract.

{widths: 25 40 35}
| Total value | What we do | Minimum evidence on file |
|---|---|---|
| Up to [[amount, e.g. USD 500]] | One quote or a published price. The requester checks the price is reasonable. | Receipt or invoice; note of price check |
| [[amount, e.g. USD 501]] to [[amount, e.g. USD 5,000]] | At least three written quotes from suppliers able to deliver. Choose using the comparison record in Annex 1. | Three quotes (email, letter or a supplier's written price); signed comparison record; purchase order |
| [[amount, e.g. USD 5,001]] to [[amount, e.g. USD 20,000]] | Written request for quotations sent to at least [[three to five]] suppliers, with the same specification and deadline for all. Scored by at least two people. | Request sent; all offers received; scoring sheet signed by the panel; contract or purchase order |
| Above [[amount, e.g. USD 20,000]] | Formal tender: advertised [[on our website, in a local newspaper, on a sector job and tender board]], with published criteria, a deadline of at least [[14 days]], sealed or emailed offers opened together, and a panel of at least three people. | Advert; tender documents; opening record; individual and combined scores; panel report; board approval where required; signed contract |

These figures are illustrative examples for a small organisation. If you cannot get three quotes, for example because only one or two suppliers exist in the area, record who you asked, why there were fewer quotes and how you checked the price was fair. The approver signs that note.

::: adapt Setting your thresholds
Set amounts that fit your budget and local prices, in the currency you use. Often the lowest band covers everyday items, such as stationery or airtime, and the tender level is a significant share of your annual budget. Then compare your figures with each funder's procurement rules and use the lower figure for that grant. Write the funder thresholds here, for example: "For [[Funder name]] grant [[reference]], three quotes are required above [[amount]] and a tender above [[amount]]." Some funders also require their approval before you buy items above a certain value, or before you buy vehicles or equipment at all.
:::

## 4. Who approves

Our Delegation of Authority Matrix sets who approves purchases, and up to what amount. In short:

- the requester and the approver are different people;
- the approver checks that the right process was followed and that there is budget, before the order is placed;
- purchases above [[USD 5,000]] also need second approval from [[the treasurer]];
- purchases above [[USD 20,000]], and all contracts lasting more than [[2 years]] or with no clear end date, need approval from [[the board]];
- the person who approves a purchase does not also make the payment, wherever our team size allows.

Approval by email is fine if saved in the procurement file.

## 5. Conflicts of interest

A conflict of interest exists when a personal, family, financial or other link could affect, or look as if it affects, a fair procurement decision. Examples: a supplier is owned by your relative, partner or close friend; you hold shares in, work for or advise a bidding company; or a supplier has offered you a job or a gift.

- Declare any conflict in writing to [[Name, role]] as soon as you know about it, and before the purchase starts.
- Step out of the whole process: writing the specification, choosing suppliers to invite, scoring, approving and paying.
- Everyone on a scoring panel signs the declaration in Annex 1 before reading the offers.
- A supplier connected to a staff member, volunteer or board member may still be chosen if the process was fully competitive, the connected person took no part, and the approver records why the choice is best value.

Our Conflict of Interest Policy explains how conflicts are recorded and managed.

## 6. Gifts, bribes and fair dealing

Never ask for or accept gifts, hospitality, commissions, discounts for yourself or any other benefit from a current or possible supplier, apart from small tokens below the lower threshold in our Gift Acceptance Policy. During a quote or tender process, accept nothing at all from any bidder.

Never offer or pay a bribe or a facilitation payment, which is a small unofficial payment to speed up a routine action, such as customs clearance. Never share one supplier's prices or information with another supplier. Our Fraud, Bribery and Corruption Policy and Procedure applies to every purchase. Laws such as the UK Bribery Act 2010 and the US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act can apply to organisations and funders well beyond those countries' borders.

## 7. Checking suppliers

Before we sign a contract or place an order above [[amount]], the procurement lead checks that the supplier:

- is a real, registered business or a genuine individual trader, using a registration certificate, tax number or other official evidence;
- can deliver what we need, using references, past work or a visit where the value justifies it;
- has a bank account in the name of the business or person we contract with;
- is not on the UN Security Council consolidated sanctions list, or on other lists our funders require, such as the US OFAC Specially Designated Nationals list, the EU consolidated financial sanctions list or the UK Sanctions List;
- is not barred from funder contracts, where a funder publishes an exclusion or debarment list.

We record each check, with the date and the list searched, on the procurement file. We screen again whenever we sign a new contract with the supplier, and at least every [[12 months]] for regular suppliers. If a name may match a sanctions list, we stop, do not pay, and follow the steps in our Partner Due Diligence and Sanctions Screening Policy.

::: adapt Depth of supplier checks
Keep checks proportionate. For a small one-off purchase from a market trader, a receipt and a sanctions search on the trader's name may be enough. For a construction contractor or a consultant handling personal data, ask for registration, tax clearance, references and, where relevant, insurance and a signed copy of your Code of Conduct and safeguarding commitments. In many countries the business registry and tax authority offer online checks: use them where they exist. Never ask a small supplier for documents they cannot reasonably obtain; record what you checked instead.
:::

## 8. Never split a purchase

Do not divide a purchase into smaller orders, or spread it over several invoices or dates, to keep it below a threshold or avoid an approval. If you need several items of the same kind at the same time, or the same service over the year, add up the total value and follow the process for that total. Splitting a purchase deliberately is a breach of this policy and may be treated as fraud.

If you buy the same item regularly, the procurement lead may set up a framework agreement: a competitive process once, then repeated orders at the agreed prices for an agreed period, usually up to [[12 months / 24 months]].

## 9. Single-source and emergency purchases

Sometimes competition is not possible or not sensible. A purchase may be made from one supplier without the usual quotes only if:

- only one supplier can provide it, for example a licence for software we already use, or the only venue in a remote area;
- a funder has named the supplier in writing;
- the value is within the lowest threshold; or
- there is a genuine emergency: a sudden event, such as a flood, conflict or medical crisis, that puts people at risk or would cause serious loss if we waited.

In an emergency, buy only what is needed to deal with the immediate situation, for no longer than [[30 days]]. Get verbal approval from [[Name, role]] if written approval is impossible, and confirm it in writing within [[2 working days]]. Record what was bought, from whom, at what price, why it could not wait and who approved. Return to the normal process as soon as possible.

Every single-source or emergency purchase above [[amount]] needs a signed waiver, with reasons, from [[the independent check]]. Lack of planning is not an emergency.

::: adapt Emergency procurement
If you work in humanitarian or crisis settings, check each funder's emergency procurement provisions. Some funders allow simplified procedures for a limited period after an emergency is declared; others still require prior written approval. Write any funder-specific rule here. If you run an emergency response regularly, consider agreeing framework agreements with key suppliers in advance, so that you can buy quickly at agreed prices.
:::

## 10. Orders and contracts

Once a supplier is chosen and approved:

- For goods and simple services above [[amount]], issue a written purchase order before delivery. It states the supplier, items or services, quantity, unit price, total, delivery date and place, payment terms and our reference.
- For services lasting more than [[a few days]] or worth more than [[amount]], sign a written contract, such as our Services Agreement or Independent Contractor Agreement. It includes the scope of work, deliverables, timeline, price and payment schedule, and our requirements on anti-corruption, safeguarding, data protection and the funder's audit rights.
- For contracts we pass funds through to another organisation for project activities, use a Sub-grant Agreement, not a supplier contract.
- Pay in advance only where it is normal market practice and [[Name, role]] approves. Limit advances to [[30%]] of the contract value unless the approver records a reason, and ask for a guarantee or staged delivery for large advances.

::: adapt Contracts and local law
Check whether your country requires stamp duty, registration or particular wording for contracts, and whether you must withhold tax on payments to suppliers or individual consultants. When you engage individuals, make sure the contract reflects a genuine independent relationship: in many countries, a long-term consultant who works like an employee may legally be treated as one.
:::

## 11. Receiving goods and paying

1. On arrival, someone other than the approver, where possible, checks goods or services against the order: quantity, quality and condition.
2. They sign and date a delivery note or a short confirmation, and note any problem.
3. The supplier sends an invoice in the name of [[Organisation name]], matching the order.
4. [[Name, role]] matches the order, the delivery confirmation and the invoice before payment. This is called a three-way match.
5. Payment is made by bank transfer or mobile money to an account in the supplier's own name, within [[30 days]] of a correct invoice. Cash payments are only allowed up to [[USD 50]], the petty cash limit in our Financial Controls Policy, and need a signed receipt.
6. Any request from a supplier to change bank details is checked by phone, using a number already on file, before any payment. Fraudsters often send fake change-of-bank-details emails.

## 12. Records for funders and audits

The procurement lead keeps a procurement file, paper or digital, for every purchase above [[amount]]. It contains the request and budget check, the specification or terms of reference, who was invited, all quotes or offers received (including unsuccessful ones), the comparison or scoring record, conflict of interest declarations, the supplier checks and sanctions screening, approvals, the order or contract, delivery confirmation, invoice and proof of payment.

We keep these records for [[7 years]] after the end of the grant or financial year, or longer if a funder or the law requires it, as set out in our Records Retention Schedule. We make them available to funders, auditors and regulators who have a right to see them.

# Roles and responsibilities

- **Requesters** plan purchases, describe what is needed, declare conflicts and confirm delivery.
- **The procurement lead, [[Name, role]],** runs quote and tender processes, checks suppliers, keeps the procurement files and supplier list, and advises the team.
- **Approvers** approve purchases within their delegated limits after checking the process and budget.
- **Finance, [[Name, role]],** checks documents before payment, pays suppliers and keeps the accounts.
- **The board** approves this policy, approves purchases above [[USD 20,000]] and receives a short procurement report at least [[once a year]], including all waivers and emergency purchases.
- **Everyone** reports concerns, such as suspected collusion or fake quotes, using our Whistleblowing Policy and Procedure.

# Records, confidentiality and data

Suppliers' offers are confidential. Only the people deciding, the approvers, and funders or auditors entitled to see them, may see them.

Supplier files may hold personal data, such as a consultant's bank details. We handle it under our Data Protection Policy.

# If this policy is breached

If a purchase was made without following this policy, the person involved tells the procurement lead straight away. The procurement lead records what happened, the reasons and any risk to the grant, and reports it to [[Name, role]]. Where a funder's rules were broken, we may need to tell the funder and may have to repay the cost from unrestricted funds.

Deliberate breaches, such as splitting purchases, inventing quotes, hiding a conflict of interest or accepting a bribe, are serious misconduct. We deal with them under our Disciplinary and Grievance Procedure, our Fraud, Bribery and Corruption Policy and Procedure and the law. We may end contracts with suppliers who offer bribes, collude with other bidders or give false information, and exclude them from future work.

# Monitoring and review

The independent check, [[Name, role]], reviews a sample of at least [[five]] procurement files every [[quarter]], and all waivers and emergency purchases. The procurement lead reviews the supplier list every [[12 months]], removes inactive suppliers and repeats the sanctions screening for active ones.

We review this policy at least once a year, after any audit finding or suspected fraud, and when a new funder's rules require changes.
