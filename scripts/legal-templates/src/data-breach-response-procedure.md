# Purpose and scope

This procedure applies to personal information held by [[Organisation name]], or by others for us, in any form: phones, laptops, cloud systems, email, messaging apps, spreadsheets, paper files and photos. It also applies when a partner or service provider has a breach involving information we share with them.

**Law.** We follow [[name of the national data protection law and year]] and the rules of [[name of regulator]]. Where we hold information about people in other countries, we also follow the law that applies there. We use the GDPR as our benchmark. Where rules differ, we follow the stricter one.

# What a breach is

A personal data breach is a security incident that leads to personal information being lost, destroyed, changed, disclosed or accessed without permission, or becoming unavailable. It can be an accident or deliberate. Examples:

- A work phone with participant contacts and WhatsApp groups is lost or stolen.
- An email goes to the wrong person, or a group email shows everyone's address in the "To" field instead of "Bcc".
- Ransomware, a type of malicious software, locks our files and demands payment.
- Paper registration forms are left on a bus, or stolen from a field office.
- Someone's email account is taken over after a phishing message, a fake email that tricks people into giving passwords.
- A shared folder link is set to "anyone with the link" and passed on.
- A staff member looks at case files they have no reason to see.
- A photo or story is published that reveals where someone lives.
- A provider or partner who holds our data has a breach.
- Records are deleted by mistake and there is no backup.

A **near miss** is an incident that could have caused a breach but did not, for example a lost laptop found unopened an hour later. We record near misses too.

# Step 1: Report it at once

If you discover or suspect a breach, at any time and whoever caused it:

1. Phone the breach lead on [[phone]] within [[1 hour]]. If you cannot reach them within [[30 minutes]], phone the deputy on [[phone]]. Then email [[privacy@yourorganisation.org]] with the details.
2. Say what happened, when, what information is involved, and what you have already done.
3. Do not delete anything, try to investigate, or contact the people affected yourself.
4. If anyone may be in danger as a result, follow the Safeguarding Policy and Procedure at the same time.

Report even if you caused the breach. We treat honest mistakes as a chance to learn. Hiding a breach, or delaying a report, is a serious matter.

# Step 2: Contain it in the first hour

The breach lead takes charge and opens an entry in the breach log (Annex A). They record the time we became aware: legal deadlines usually run from this moment. They then act to stop the breach and limit harm.

[ ] Lost or stolen device: lock, locate or wipe it remotely; sign the device out of email, cloud and messaging accounts; change passwords; remove it from WhatsApp groups.
[ ] Wrong email recipient: try to recall the message; contact the recipient, ask them to delete it and confirm in writing.
[ ] Account taken over: change the password, sign out all sessions, turn on two-step sign-in, check forwarding rules.
[ ] Ransomware or malware: disconnect affected devices from the internet and network; do not pay any ransom without advice; call IT support.
[ ] Paper files lost or stolen: search the last known location; report theft to the police; secure remaining files.
[ ] Shared link or wrong access: remove the link or access, and check who opened the file.
[ ] Provider or partner breach: ask for details in writing and what they are doing.
[ ] Keep evidence: logs, screenshots, emails and notes of what was done, with times.

::: adapt Containment tools
Check now that you can do these things before a breach happens. Can you wipe work phones remotely? Who holds the administrator password for email and cloud accounts? Are backups kept separately from the main system, so that ransomware cannot reach them? Write the steps for your own tools here, and add a short contact list for IT support and your insurer. The IT and Information Security Policy should set the underlying controls.
:::

# Step 3: Assess the risk to people

Within [[24 hours]], the breach lead assesses the likely effect on the people whose information is involved, using the questions in Annex B. The question is not how embarrassing the breach is for us. It is what could happen to the people affected. Harms include physical harm, discrimination, identity theft, financial loss, damage to reputation, loss of confidentiality and distress.

They rate the breach:

- **No likely risk:** for example, an encrypted laptop lost with a strong password, or a list of public email addresses sent to a partner who deleted it.
- **Risk:** some harm is possible, but not serious or not likely.
- **High risk:** serious harm is likely, for example when health, HIV status, sexual orientation, immigration status, survivor or safeguarding information, children's data, location of people at risk, or bank details is exposed.

For serious or high-risk breaches, the breach lead brings together a small response team: [[director / founder]], the safeguarding lead, IT support and, where needed, a lawyer and the insurer.

# Step 4: Decide on notification

The breach lead decides who must be told and records the reasons for each decision, including decisions not to notify.

## The regulator

Under the GDPR, we must notify the regulator within 72 hours of becoming aware of a breach, unless it is unlikely to result in a risk to people. If we do not have all the facts, we notify what we know and send the rest later. We notify [[name of regulator]] through [[their online form / email]] within [[72 hours]].

## The people affected

We tell the people affected without undue delay when the breach is likely to result in a high risk to them, so they can protect themselves. We may also tell them when the risk is lower, if it helps them. We use plain language, their own language, and a channel that reaches them safely: a phone call or visit may be better than a letter for people with low literacy or where post is not private.

## Funders, partners and others

- **Funders:** many grant agreements require notice of a data breach within a set time, often [[24 to 72 hours]]. Check each agreement.
- **Partners:** if a partner shared the data with us, our Data Sharing Agreement says how fast we must tell them. If we are acting on their behalf, they make the notification decisions and we support them.
- **Police:** for theft, fraud, extortion or ransomware.
- **Bank:** if financial details or accounts are involved.
- **Insurer:** as the policy requires, often before taking other steps.
- **Board:** the chair is told of every high-risk breach within [[24 hours]].

::: adapt Notification rules by country
Check the deadlines and thresholds that apply to you. GDPR and UK GDPR: the regulator within 72 hours unless unlikely to result in a risk; people affected without undue delay if high risk. Kenya, Data Protection Act 2019: the Data Commissioner within 72 hours, and people affected within a reasonably practical period. Nigeria, Data Protection Act 2023: the Nigeria Data Protection Commission within 72 hours, and people affected immediately where the risk is high. South Africa, POPIA: the Information Regulator and the people affected as soon as reasonably possible, with no fixed number of hours. Brazil, LGPD: the ANPD and people affected where there is relevant risk; ANPD Resolution 15/2024 sets 3 working days from becoming aware, doubled for small processing agents. India, DPDP Act 2023 and its 2025 Rules: the Data Protection Board and each affected person, with a detailed report to the Board within 72 hours; these Rules were set to apply 18 months after their notification in November 2025, so check whether they are in force. Indonesia, Law 27 of 2022: written notice to people affected and the authority within 3 x 24 hours. Peru, Law 29733 and its 2024 regulation: the national authority within 48 hours of detecting the incident. Argentina: check current rules, which have been changing. If you hold data on donors in the United States, state breach laws may also apply.
:::

# Step 5: Communicate

- Only the breach lead, or someone they authorise, speaks about a breach outside the organisation.
- Everyone involved keeps the details confidential, including on personal social media.
- Messages to people affected say what happened, what information was involved, what harm could follow, what we have done, what they can do, and who to contact. Annex C has wording.
- If the media ask, we use a short, honest holding statement agreed by [[Name, role]] and do not name anyone affected.
- Where people could be at risk of physical harm, the safeguarding lead decides how and when they are told, so that the message itself does not put them in danger.

# Step 6: Recover

Within [[2 weeks]], or sooner for serious breaches, the breach lead makes sure that:

- systems are restored from clean backups and checked;
- passwords and access are reset and lost devices are replaced and set up securely;
- recipients have confirmed deletion, and we have checked for any misuse of the data;
- people affected have the support we promised, such as a call from a staff member they know.

# Step 7: Learn

Within [[30 days]], the breach lead reviews what happened with the people involved. The review asks what caused the breach, what worked and what did not, and what we will change. Actions are recorded in the log with an owner and a date, and added to the Risk Register where relevant. The board receives a summary of all breaches [[every six months / once a year]], and a full report on any high-risk breach.

# Record every breach

The breach lead records every breach and near miss in the breach log, whether or not anyone is notified. The record shows the facts, the effects, the actions taken and the reasons for each notification decision. Regulators can ask to see it. The log is kept for [[6 years]] and is visible only to the breach lead, the deputy and the director.

::: adapt Breach log storage
Keep the log somewhere that would survive the breach, for example not on the laptop that was stolen or the account that was hacked. A protected spreadsheet in a separate account, or a paper register in a locked drawer, works for a small team. Match the retention period to your Records Retention Schedule.
:::

# Example: Lost phone with participant contacts

A field officer leaves a work phone in a taxi on Friday at 5pm. It holds the names and numbers of 40 programme participants, and a WhatsApp group for a support group for survivors of violence.

{widths: 18 62 20}
| When | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| 5:40pm | Officer notices, tries calling the phone, then phones the breach lead | Field officer |
| 5:50pm | Breach lead opens a log entry; asks if the phone had a screen lock (yes, 4-digit PIN) | Breach lead |
| 6:00pm | Phone is located remotely, then locked and wiped; the officer's accounts are signed out and passwords changed; the officer's number is removed from the WhatsApp group by another admin | Breach lead, IT support |
| Saturday | Risk assessed as high: a survivors' group could reveal who is in it, and a PIN is weak protection. Safeguarding lead joins | Breach lead, safeguarding lead |
| Sunday | Chair informed. Decision to notify the regulator and the funder. Safeguarding lead plans how to tell group members safely | Breach lead |
| Monday | Regulator notified within 72 hours; funder notified as the grant requires; theft or loss reported to the police for a reference | Breach lead |
| Mon–Tue | Safeguarding lead and a trusted facilitator call each group member individually, explain, and agree any safety steps | Safeguarding lead |
| Within 30 days | Review: all work phones get 6-digit PINs and remote wipe; sensitive groups move to a setting where members cannot see each other's numbers | Breach lead |

::: adapt Your own example
Replace this example with one that matches your work, for example paper forms from a community health survey, or a spreadsheet of donors sent to the wrong partner. Practise it once a year as a 30-minute desk exercise with the team.
:::

# Annex B: Risk assessment questions

1. What information is involved? Is any of it sensitive: health, sexual life, ethnicity, religion, political views, criminal records, immigration status, safeguarding or survivor information, biometric data, bank details?
2. Whose information is it? Children, adults at risk, people in conflict or displacement, activists or others who could be targeted?
3. How many people are affected?
4. Was the information protected, for example by encryption or a strong password?
5. Who now has it? A trusted partner who deleted it, a stranger, or someone who could misuse it?
6. Can it be recovered or the exposure reversed?
7. Could the people be identified from it, alone or combined with other information?
8. What harm could follow, and how likely and how serious is it?
9. Does the breach affect our ability to deliver services, for example lost case records?
10. Do any laws, funder agreements or data sharing agreements set specific duties?
