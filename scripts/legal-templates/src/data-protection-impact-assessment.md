# Purpose

A DPIA helps [[Organisation name]] look at a new activity from the point of view of the people whose information we will use. It asks four questions: what are we doing with their information, do we really need to, what could go wrong for them, and what will we do to prevent it. It is a planning tool, not a box-ticking exercise. Done early, it saves money and protects people.

# When we need a DPIA

The project lead completes the screening questions below before any new activity that uses personal information. If the answer to any question is yes, they complete the full assessment before the activity starts, unless the data protection lead decides the risk is clearly low and records why. If all answers are no, they keep the screening with the project file.

#### SCREENING QUESTIONS

[ ] We will introduce a new system, app, database or online tool that holds personal information.
[ ] We will collect sensitive information: health, HIV status, disability, sexual orientation, ethnicity, religion, political views, criminal records, immigration status, biometric data or survivor information.
[ ] We will collect information about children, or about adults who may be at risk.
[ ] We will monitor people on a large scale or systematically, for example location tracking, CCTV or tracking online behaviour.
[ ] We will use new technology, including AI tools that analyse, transcribe, translate, score or make decisions about people.
[ ] We will share personal information with a partner, funder, government body or researcher, or combine data sets from different sources.
[ ] We will transfer personal information to another country or to a provider abroad.
[ ] A decision that significantly affects people, such as eligibility for support, will be made or guided by automated means.
[ ] The people concerned would be surprised, or could be harmed, if they knew how we use their information.

::: adapt When a DPIA is legally required
Under the GDPR, a DPIA is required where processing is likely to result in a high risk to people, and many national regulators publish lists of activities that always need one. Kenya's Data Protection Act 2019 requires a DPIA for processing likely to result in high risk, and its regulations require it to be shared with the Data Commissioner before processing starts; check the current timing. Nigeria's Data Protection Act 2023 requires one where processing is likely to result in high risk. Indonesia's Law 27 of 2022 requires an impact assessment for high-risk processing. In Brazil, the ANPD can ask for a data protection impact report under the LGPD. South Africa's POPIA regulations require the information officer to ensure a personal information impact assessment is done. India's DPDP Act 2023 requires periodic DPIAs only from organisations designated as significant data fiduciaries. Even where it is not required, a DPIA is good practice for the activities in the list above.
:::

# How to use this form

- **Who completes it:** the project lead, with help from the data protection lead [[Name, role]].
- **When:** at the design stage, before data is collected or a contract is signed. Not after launch.
- **How long:** keep answers short. A paragraph per question is usually enough.
- **Who signs it:** [[the director / the data protection lead]] approves it. If the remaining risk is high, the board decides.
- **Where it is kept:** with the project file and our record of processing, for the life of the activity plus [[3 years]].
- **Review:** at the date set in Part 8, and whenever the activity changes.

## Part 2. Necessity and proportionality

Answer each question briefly.

1. What is our lawful basis for each use, for example consent, contract, legal obligation or legitimate interests? If we rely on consent, can people say no without losing services?
2. Could we achieve the purpose with less information, or with anonymous or aggregated data, meaning totals that do not identify anyone?
3. How will we tell people about the processing, in their language and in a format they understand?
4. How will people use their rights, such as access and deletion?
5. How will we keep the information accurate?
6. Do our service providers and partners have written agreements that protect the data?
7. How long do we really need the data, and does this match our Records Retention Schedule?

::: adapt Lawful basis and consent
In many communities we serve, people may feel unable to refuse consent because they depend on our support. If so, consent is not freely given, and you should consider another lawful basis, with clear information and a real way to opt out. Name the lawful bases your local law provides, as they differ: for example, India's DPDP Act 2023 relies mainly on consent and a short list of "legitimate uses", rather than the GDPR's six bases.
:::

Where it is practical, ask the people whose information will be used, or people like them, what they think. A short conversation with a community group, participant representatives or staff can reveal risks we would not see.

::: adapt Consulting safely
Consult in a way that does not itself create risk. For survivors of violence or people in conflict settings, ask experienced partners or staff who work closely with them rather than the people directly. For a staff system, consult staff or their representatives. Record a short note of what was said.
:::

## Part 4. Identify the risks

List the risks to people, not to the organisation. Consider physical harm, discrimination, identity theft, financial loss, loss of confidentiality, distress, and losing access to services. Then rate each risk.

{widths: 34 33 33}
| Likelihood | Severity | Overall |
|---|---|---|
| **Remote:** unlikely to happen | **Minimal:** short-term inconvenience | Low |
| **Possible:** could happen | **Significant:** serious but recoverable harm | Medium |
| **Probable:** likely to happen | **Severe:** lasting or irreversible harm, or danger to safety | High |

Overall risk is high if either rating is at the top level and the other is at least in the middle, and low if both are at the lowest level. Otherwise it is medium. Use judgement where this does not fit.

{widths: 46 18 18 18}
| Risk to people | Likelihood | Severity | Overall |
|---|---|---|---|
| Example: interview recordings of young people are kept by the AI transcription provider and used to train its models | Possible | Significant | Medium |
| Example: a participant list shared with a partner is forwarded to local authorities, exposing people to arrest | Remote | Severe | Medium |
| Example: field staff use personal phones, which are lost or shared with family | Probable | Significant | High |

::: adapt New technology and AI
For AI tools, check: where the data is processed, whether the provider keeps or trains on it, whether outputs could be wrong or biased against some groups, and whether a person reviews every decision that affects someone. If you cannot get clear answers from a provider, do not put personal or sensitive information into the tool.
:::

::: adapt Prior consultation
The GDPR requires prior consultation with the regulator where a DPIA shows high risk that cannot be reduced. Several other laws contain similar duties, but the rules differ. Check your local law and your regulator's guidance on when and how to consult.

# Guidance notes

- **Start small.** A one-page DPIA done before launch is better than a long one done after a problem.
- **Write for the reader.** A funder or regulator should understand what you do and why it is safe.
- **Be honest about risk.** A DPIA that shows no risks at all is not credible.
- **Link it to the rest of your system.** Add high risks to the Risk Register, update the Privacy Notice, and use a Data Sharing Agreement for partners.
- **Keep it alive.** Review it when things change, and record what changed.
