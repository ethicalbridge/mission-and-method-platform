::: glance
- A conflict of interest arises when a personal interest or loyalty could affect, or look as if it affects, a decision you make for us.
- Having a conflict is normal. Failing to declare it is a breach of this policy.
- Declare interests when you join, every year, at the start of each meeting, and as soon as a new conflict arises.
- If you are conflicted, you step out of the discussion and the vote. Others decide.
- Every declaration and decision is recorded in the register of interests and in the minutes.

# Who this policy applies to

This policy applies to:

- **Board members** [[trustees / directors / committee members]], including co-opted members and observers;
- **Staff**, full-time, part-time and temporary;
- **Volunteers** who make or influence decisions, such as selecting participants, suppliers or partners;
- **Advisers, contractors and consultants**, including members of grant or selection panels.

It covers decisions about money, people, partners, suppliers, the people we serve, and our strategy and reputation.

**Law.** [[Organisation name]] is registered in [[country]] as a [[legal form]]. We follow our [[constitution / articles / bylaws]] and the law on conflicts and related-party transactions. Where our governing document or the law sets a stricter rule than this policy, the stricter rule applies. If a funder sets stricter rules for its funds, those also apply.

::: adapt Law and your governing document
Read your governing document first: it may already set rules on conflicts, payment of board members or quorum, and this policy must not contradict it. Then check your local law. Examples to check against current texts: in England and Wales, company directors have duties under the Companies Act 2006 to avoid and declare conflicts, and charity trustees can only receive a benefit if the governing document, the law or the Charity Commission allows it (see the Commission's guidance on conflicts of interest, CC29). In the US, the IRS Form 990 asks whether a non-profit has a written conflict of interest policy, and excess benefit rules apply to transactions with insiders. In India, company directors must disclose interests under the Companies Act 2013, and in South Africa under the Companies Act 2008. For other countries, check your local law on NGO or non-profit board members and on related-party transactions.
:::

# What we mean

{widths: 25 75}
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Conflict of interest | A situation where your personal interests, or your duties to someone else, could affect, or look as if they affect, your judgement when acting for [[Organisation name]]. |
| Actual conflict | The conflict exists now. Example: you are asked to approve a contract with a company you own. |
| Potential conflict | A conflict that could arise in future. Example: your sister has applied to work with a partner we may fund next year. |
| Perceived conflict | A reasonable person could think a conflict exists, even if you are sure it does not affect you. Perceived conflicts damage trust too, so we treat them the same way. |
| Financial interest | Any direct or indirect chance to gain or lose money or something of value: a salary, a fee, shares, a loan, property, a contract or a job. |
| Non-financial interest | A personal, family, political, religious, community or professional link that could affect your judgement. Example: you sit on the board of another NGO that competes with us for the same grant. |
| Conflict of loyalty | Your duty to another organisation, such as an employer, funder or partner, pulls against your duty to us. |
| Related party | A person or organisation closely connected to us: board members, the director and senior staff, their close family, and businesses or organisations they own, control or help run. |
| Close associate | A relative or person you have a close personal, business or financial link with: a spouse or partner, children, parents, siblings, in-laws, members of your household, business partners and close friends. |

::: adapt Definitions in your context
In many communities, extended family and clan links are strong and widely known. Decide how far “close family” goes for you, for example including cousins, uncles and aunts. Make sure the definition of related party matches the one your accountant uses for the annual accounts, as many accounting standards require related-party transactions to be disclosed.
:::

# Policy

## 1. Our principles

1. Everyone acting for us puts the interests of [[Organisation name]] and the people we serve first.
2. Having an interest is not wrong. Hiding it, or using your role to benefit yourself or a close associate, is.
3. When in doubt, declare. It is always better to declare something that turns out not to matter.
4. The person with the conflict never decides how it is managed, and never takes part in the decision itself.
5. We record every declaration and every decision.

## 2. When to declare

Declare your interests:

- **when you join** us, before you take part in any decision;
- **every year**, by completing an annual declaration by [[date, e.g. 31 January]];
- **at the start of every board or committee meeting**, for each item on the agenda;
- **as soon as a new conflict arises**, and before any related decision, by telling the person named in the note at the start of this policy in writing (email is fine).

If you realise after a decision that you had an undeclared conflict, declare it straight away. The decision will be reviewed.

## 3. Annual declarations and the register of interests

Every board member, the director, staff who approve spending or select suppliers, partners or participants, and panel members complete a declaration each year. Board members use the Board Interest Declaration. Staff use the same form or a shorter version.

You declare:

- paid jobs, consultancies and businesses you or your close associates own or run;
- board, committee or advisory roles in other organisations;
- shareholdings or financial interests above [[amount and currency / a percentage]] in any organisation that deals with us;
- any link with our funders, partners, suppliers or the people we serve;
- political or public office, and candidacy for it;
- gifts or hospitality received in connection with your role (see section 9);
- any other interest a reasonable person might think relevant.

[[Name, role]] records declarations in the register of interests (Annex A). The board reviews the register [[once a year]]. We give funders, auditors and regulators access to it when they ask, and publish [[a summary / no part]] of it.

::: adapt Who declares and what you publish
Decide which staff must make an annual declaration. In a team of three or four, ask everyone. Set a realistic financial threshold, or ask people to declare all shareholdings in organisations that deal with you. Some regulators require board members' interests to be filed or published; check your local rules. Publishing a summary of board interests on your website builds trust, but only publish with the consent of the people concerned and in line with your Data Protection Policy.
:::

## 4. How we assess a conflict

When a conflict is declared, the person who decides (see the note at the start) asks:

1. What exactly is the interest, and who would benefit?
2. How significant is it: the amount, how close the relationship is, how often it arises?
3. Would a reasonable person, such as a funder, a journalist or a community member, think it could affect the decision?
4. Can the decision be made fairly by others without this person?
5. Is the conflict so central that the person cannot carry out their role, or the transaction should not go ahead?

They decide within [[5 working days]], or before the decision if that is sooner, and record the outcome and reasons in the register.

## 5. How we manage a conflict

Depending on the answers, we will take one or more of these steps:

{widths: 30 70}
| Level | How we manage it |
|---|---|
| No real risk | Record the declaration. The person can take part. Example: a board member's cousin buys a ticket to our public event. |
| Low | Record it. The person can share information but does not take part in the decision. |
| Moderate | The person leaves the discussion and does not vote. They may answer factual questions first, then leave the room or the call. |
| High | The person leaves the room for the whole item and receives none of the related papers. An independent person decides. We may need extra checks, such as quotes or an outside opinion. |
| Unmanageable | The person steps down from the role, or we do not go ahead with the transaction. |

For staff, an independent decision means one taken by a manager or board member who has no conflict. Where the director is conflicted, the chair or another board member decides.

::: adapt Managing conflicts in practice
Keep these levels or simplify them. Add examples from your work: a staff member whose relative lives in a community selected for support; a board member whose company offers a discount to supply laptops; a founder who is also paid as a consultant. A common and defensible rule for small teams is that the person with the conflict always leaves the room for the discussion and the vote, whatever the level.
:::

## 6. Board meetings: quorum and minutes

- Every agenda starts with a standing item: “Declarations of interest”.
- A board member with a conflict on an item leaves the discussion and the vote, as decided under section 5.
- A conflicted member does not count towards the quorum for that item. If removing them means the meeting is no longer quorate, the item is deferred, or decided under the procedure our governing document sets for this situation.
- The minutes record who declared what, what they did (for example left the room), who took part in the decision, and the reasons for the decision. Annex B gives example wording.
- If a conflict is declared between meetings, the chair or [[Name, role]] records it and reports it at the next meeting.

::: adapt Quorum
Check your governing document. Many say how many members form a quorum and whether conflicted members count. If most of your board is conflicted on an issue, for example on paying a founder board member, you may need to bring in independent members, ask members of the organisation to decide, or seek the regulator's consent. Check your local law on this.
:::

## 7. Related-party transactions

A related-party transaction is any contract, payment, loan, sale, lease, grant or job involving [[Organisation name]] and a related party. Examples: renting an office from the director's brother; paying a board member as a consultant; buying supplies from a company a staff member part-owns.

We enter a related-party transaction only if all of these apply:

1. Our governing document and the law allow it.
2. It is clearly in the best interests of [[Organisation name]].
3. The terms are as good as, or better than, those we could get elsewhere. We get [[at least two / three]] independent quotes or a market comparison, and keep them on file.
4. It is approved in advance by people with no conflict: the board for board members and the director, and the director for others, within the limits in our Delegation of Authority Matrix.
5. It is recorded in the minutes and the register, and disclosed in our annual accounts where the law or accounting standards require it.
6. We tell funders where our grant agreements require it.

Board members are not paid for serving on the board, except [[reasonable expenses / as our governing document allows]]. A board member who provides paid services must do so under a written contract approved by the rest of the board.

::: adapt Paying board members and founders
Rules on paying board members vary widely. In many countries, charity or non-profit board members may only be paid where the governing document or the regulator allows it, and paying them can affect tax status. Founders who sit on the board and are also employed should declare this standing conflict and leave any discussion of their own pay, performance or contract. Check your local law and governing document, and ask an accountant how related-party transactions must be shown in your accounts.
:::

## 8. Recruiting and managing relatives and close associates

- We recruit on merit, through an open process set out in our recruitment procedure.
- If a close associate of a board member or staff member applies for a job, volunteer role, consultancy or support from us, that person declares it straight away.
- They take no part in shortlisting, interviewing, selecting, setting pay or checking references.
- A close associate is never line-managed, supervised or appraised by their relative, and never approves their pay, expenses or timesheets.
- Two close associates may not be signatories or approvers on the same bank account or payment.
- If two staff members form a close personal relationship, they declare it so that reporting lines can be adjusted.

## 9. Gifts, hospitality and outside activities

Gifts and hospitality can create a conflict, or the appearance of one. Follow our Gift Acceptance Policy and record gifts in the gifts and hospitality register.

Staff may take on outside work or roles, provided they do not compete with us, use our time, name or resources, or conflict with their duties. Tell [[Name, role]] before accepting any paid work, board seat or public office, so that any conflict can be managed. Do not use information you gain through your role for personal benefit or to benefit anyone else.

# Records, confidentiality and data

- The register of interests and declarations are kept by [[Name, role]] in [[location / system]], with access limited to the board, the director and the register keeper.
- Declarations may contain personal information about close associates. We collect only what we need and handle it under our Data Protection Policy.
- We keep declarations and the register for [[6 years]] after the end of the person's role, in line with our Records Retention Schedule.
- Minutes recording conflicts are kept permanently with the board records.

# If this policy is breached

Failing to declare a conflict, taking part in a decision when conflicted, or using a role to benefit yourself or a close associate is a serious breach. Depending on the case, we may:

- review, reverse or cancel the decision or transaction where the law allows;
- recover any benefit gained;
- take disciplinary action, end a contract or ask a board member to step down, following our procedures and governing document;
- inform funders or regulators where our agreements or the law require it.

If a breach may also involve fraud or corruption, we follow our Fraud, Bribery and Corruption Policy and Procedure. Anyone can raise a concern about an undeclared conflict through our Whistleblowing Policy and Procedure.

# Monitoring and review

The register keeper reports to the board [[once a year]] on declarations received and any overdue. The board reviews this policy [[every year]], after any serious breach, and when our governing document or the law changes.
