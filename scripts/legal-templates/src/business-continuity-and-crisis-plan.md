# Purpose and scope

This plan helps us to protect people, keep our most important work going, decide quickly, communicate honestly, and return to normal as soon as it is safe. It applies to all of [[Organisation name]]'s work, in every location, including work delivered by volunteers and partners.

**Definitions.** **Business continuity** means keeping critical activities going during and after a disruption. A **crisis** is a serious event that needs urgent decisions beyond our normal ways of working. A **critical activity** is one whose loss would seriously harm people, our legal position or our survival. Its **maximum tolerable downtime** is the longest it can stop before that harm becomes unacceptable.

**Other procedures still apply.** If a crisis involves a safeguarding concern, a data breach, fraud or an accident, we also follow the Safeguarding Policy and Procedure, the Data Breach Response Procedure, the Fraud, Bribery and Corruption Policy and Procedure or the Incident Reporting Procedure. This plan coordinates the wider response around them.

**Law.** We follow the laws of [[country of registration]] and of each country where we work. Where a funder agreement sets a stricter rule, such as a shorter reporting deadline, we follow the stricter rule.

# How this plan links to the Risk Register

The **Risk Register** finds and scores the risks that could stop us achieving our mission, and sets out how we prevent them. This **plan** says how we keep going when one of those risks happens anyway. The two work together:

- The scenarios here match the register's highest-scoring risks. When those change, we check this plan still covers them.
- The register's "if it happens" plans for our top three risks are short versions of these scenarios, and must stay consistent.
- After any crisis, we update the register: rescore the risk, add new risks and record the lessons.

::: adapt Matching the register
Every register risk with a residual score of [[15]] or more should have a matching scenario here, or a reason why not. Delete scenarios that do not apply, such as loss of premises for a fully remote team. Keep the safeguarding, key person and funding scenarios: they apply to almost everyone.
:::

## 2. Authority to act

During a crisis the crisis lead may spend up to [[amount and currency]] on urgent costs, such as emergency travel, IT recovery or medical help, and may suspend non-essential activities. Larger decisions follow the Delegation of Authority Matrix. If the board cannot meet in time, the chair and [[one other board member]] may approve urgent decisions, which the full board confirms at its next meeting.

::: adapt Emergency spending authority
Choose an amount that covers a realistic emergency, such as evacuating staff or replacing laptops, often about [[two to four weeks]] of running costs. Check your governing document allows the board to delegate this, and use the same figure in your Delegation of Authority Matrix. Every emergency payment still needs a receipt and a log entry.
:::

# What counts as a crisis

## 4. Raising the alarm

Anyone who becomes aware of a serious event phones the crisis lead at once on [[phone number]]. If there is no answer within [[30 minutes]], they call the deputy. If anyone is in immediate danger, they call the emergency services first on [[local emergency number]]. Report even if you are not sure it is serious.

## 5. Severity levels

The crisis lead decides the level within [[1 hour]] of the first report, and can change it at any time.

{widths: 14 34 20 32}
| Level | Examples | Who decides | First steps |
|---|---|---|---|
| Level 1: Incident | Office without power for a day; a laptop stolen with no sensitive data; a minor complaint online | Line manager, informing the crisis lead | Use normal procedures; record it; watch whether it grows |
| Level 2: Serious disruption | Office closed over [[3 days]]; email down over [[24 hours]]; a key grant payment over [[1 month]] late; a safeguarding concern about staff; unrest stopping field work | Crisis lead | Activate the plan; call the crisis team within [[4 hours]]; tell the board link; open the log |
| Level 3: Crisis | Death or serious injury; kidnap or arrest of staff; a major allegation made public; bank account frozen; ransomware locking all systems; founder suddenly unable to work; disaster or epidemic | Crisis lead with the board link | All Level 2 steps; tell the chair within [[2 hours]]; consider an urgent board meeting; agree external messages before anyone speaks |

::: adapt Setting your thresholds
Change the examples and times to fit your work. A health clinic may treat a one-day power cut as Level 2; a research organisation may not. The test: Level 2 means normal procedures are not enough; Level 3 means lives, survival or reputation are at stake. If in doubt, choose the higher level and lower it later.
:::

# The first 24 hours

When the plan is activated at Level 2 or 3, the crisis lead works through the checklist in Annex B, in three stages:

- **First hour:** safety, setting the level, opening the log and containing the problem.
- **First four hours:** the crisis team, the board link, workarounds, staff briefing and the holding statement.
- **First 24 hours:** notifications, the people we serve, cash and payroll, and welfare.

# Critical activities and minimum service levels

These activities must keep going, or restart quickly, in any crisis. For each one we have agreed the maximum tolerable downtime, the minimum level we will keep and a workaround that does not depend on our usual office or systems.

| Activity | Maximum downtime | Minimum level | Owner | Workaround |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Paying staff and essential suppliers | [[5 working days]] | Salaries, rent and critical suppliers paid | [[Finance lead]] | Two online banking users in different places; mobile money; cash float of [[amount]] |
| [[Core service, e.g. food distribution]] | [[1 week]] | [[e.g. Most vulnerable households only]] | [[Programme manager]] | [[e.g. Partner premises; offline phone list]] |
| Email, files and records | [[48 hours]] | Crisis team email; contacts, contracts and case records | [[IT lead]] | Cloud access from any device; offline backup |
| Funder reporting | [[2 weeks]] | Funders told of any delay before the deadline | [[ED / founder]] | Grants calendar in the cloud and on paper |
| Legal and regulatory filings | Until the deadline | No missed filing | [[ED / founder]] | Compliance calendar shared with the deputy |
| [[Add your own]] | [[time]] | [[level]] | [[owner]] | [[workaround]] |

::: adapt Choosing critical activities
List everything you do and ask of each: who would be harmed, and how quickly, if this stopped? Keep only activities where the answer is "someone vulnerable", "we would break the law or a contract" or "we could not survive". Most small organisations have five to eight. An untested workaround is a hope, not a plan: test at least one each year, and add any gaps to your Risk Register.
:::

# Scenarios and response steps

Each scenario sets out the main steps. The first-24-hours checklist always applies as well.

## 6. Loss of premises or access

For example: fire, flood, eviction, a long power or water failure, or the area being closed by the authorities.

1. Get everyone out safely. Do not go back in until the authorities say it is safe.
2. Move staff to home working or [[alternative site, e.g. partner office]]. Tell the people we serve where to reach us.
3. Contact the landlord and insurer within [[24 hours]]. Photograph damage and keep receipts.
4. If personal data was lost or exposed, start the Data Breach Response Procedure.
5. If the closure will last over [[2 weeks]], agree a medium-term plan and budget with the board link.

## 7. IT failure, data loss or cyber attack

For example: ransomware, a hacked email account, a failed cloud service, or deleted records with no backup.

1. Disconnect affected devices from the internet. Do not switch them off unless IT support says so, as this can destroy evidence.
2. Call [[IT support]] on [[phone]], and the insurer's cyber helpline if we have one.
3. From a clean device, change passwords, sign out all sessions, and check email forwarding rules and bank payees.
4. If personal data may be affected, start the Data Breach Response Procedure at once. Its legal deadlines can be as short as 72 hours.
5. Warn the bank. Ask partners and suppliers to confirm any change of bank details by phone.
6. Restore from clean, checked backups.
7. Do not pay a ransom without advice from the board, the insurer and the police. Paying a group on a sanctions list can be illegal, for example under UK, EU or US sanctions. Report the crime to the police and any national cyber incident team.

Preventive controls, such as backups, two-step sign-in and encryption, are in the IT and Information Security Policy.

## 8. Loss of a key person, including the founder or director

For example: sudden illness, an accident, death, arrest, burnout, or the sudden resignation of the founder, director or the only person who runs finance.

1. Within [[48 hours]] the chair appoints an acting lead, normally the deputy crisis lead, and records the decision.
2. Use the emergency access arrangements for bank accounts, email and key systems. Never use the person's own login without a recorded decision.
3. Within [[1 week]], list and assign every deadline for the next [[3 months]]: payroll, reports, renewals, filings.
4. Tell funders and key partners who their new contact is, respecting the person's privacy.
5. Support the person and their family, and agree with them what is said.
6. If the person was a bank signatory or registered officer, update the bank mandate and registration records promptly.
7. Within [[1 month]], the board agrees interim arrangements and how to share knowledge and relationships more widely.

::: adapt Founder dependency and changing officers
Before a crisis, make sure at least two people can approve payments, a password manager or sealed envelope gives emergency access to key accounts, and key procedures are written down. Banks often restrict an account when a sole signatory dies or leaves, until the board provides minutes and a new mandate: check your bank's rules now. Many registration laws require you to notify the registrar or NGO regulator of changes in directors, trustees or officers within a set time. Check your local law for the deadline.
:::

## 9. Funding shock or frozen bank account

For example: a major funder suspends or ends a grant, a payment is months late, the currency falls sharply, or the bank freezes or closes our account.

1. Within [[2 days]], update the cash forecast for the next [[13 weeks]], showing how long we can pay salaries and essential costs.
2. Pause new commitments and non-essential spending. Protect payroll and the people we serve first.
3. For a frozen account, ask the bank in writing for the reason and the documents it needs, and provide them quickly. Use a second account or mobile money for essential payments.
4. Talk to the funder early about bridging payments, a no-cost extension or a phased exit.
5. The board meets within [[2 weeks]] to agree options: reserves under our Reserves Policy, reduced programmes, bridging finance or other funders.
6. If jobs must change, follow employment law and tell staff honestly and early.
7. Never leave communities without notice. Close or hand over activities responsibly.

::: adapt Bank freezes and de-risking
Banks sometimes freeze or close non-profit accounts because of anti-money laundering or counter-terrorist financing rules, especially when money moves to or from high-risk countries. FATF Recommendation 8 asks governments to protect non-profits from abuse without disrupting legitimate work, but small organisations are still often affected. Keep registration, governance and source-of-funds documents ready to send; screen partners under the Partner Due Diligence and Sanctions Screening Policy; and, where your law allows, hold a second account at a different bank. Set the cash float in line with your Financial Controls Policy.
:::

## 10. Safeguarding or reputational crisis

For example: an allegation of abuse by staff, volunteers or a partner; a complaint spreading online; fraud made public.

1. Put the safety and wishes of the survivor first. Follow the Safeguarding Policy and Procedure from the first minute. The safeguarding lead leads on the case; the crisis lead manages everything around it.
2. If the allegation concerns the crisis lead or founder, the deputy and board link take over, and the person concerned has no part in the response.
3. Take protective steps, such as suspending the person from contact with participants, without prejudging the outcome. Preserve evidence.
4. Report to the authorities, funders and regulators within their time limits.
5. Agree a holding statement before anyone speaks. Never identify a survivor, a child or a witness, even indirectly.
6. Do not delete posts in panic. Respond once, calmly and factually.
7. Arrange an independent review where the case is serious or involves senior people.

::: adapt Reporting serious incidents to regulators and funders
Many charity and NGO regulators expect serious incidents to be reported promptly. For example, the Charity Commission for England and Wales expects trustees to report serious incidents such as safeguarding incidents and significant fraud. Check your local law and regulator for what counts as serious and how fast to report. Most institutional funders require notice of safeguarding allegations and fraud within a short time, often [[24 to 72 hours]]. Record each funder's deadline in the contact sheet.
:::

## 11. Unrest, natural disaster or epidemic

For example: elections or protests turning violent, armed conflict, floods, earthquakes, drought, a disease outbreak or a pandemic affecting staff and communities.

1. Account for everyone in the area within [[2 hours]] where possible, using the check-in arrangements in the Travel Safety and Duty of Care Policy.
2. Stop travel into the area. Decide whether to pause activities, move people or evacuate. Never ask anyone to stay in danger to keep a programme running.
3. Follow the national authorities and, for health emergencies, the health ministry and the World Health Organization.
4. Move critical activities to remote or partner-led delivery only where it is safe for everyone, partners included.
5. Support staff whose families or homes are affected, with flexible leave.
6. Ask communities what they need. Talk to funders early about changing activities or budgets.

::: adapt Duty of care and pay during closures
Employers usually have a legal duty to take reasonable steps to keep staff safe, including when they travel or work in high-risk places. Rules on pay during emergency closures and reduced hours differ widely: check your local employment law. Decide in advance, for example full pay for [[2 weeks]] of closure and then a board review.
:::

## 12. Partner failure

For example: a delivery partner collapses, loses its registration, misuses funds, faces a serious allegation or stops delivering.

1. Get the facts in writing. Check the Sub-grant Agreement or Partnership Memorandum of Understanding for rights to suspend payments, see records and terminate.
2. Suspend further payments if there is a risk of misuse.
3. Decide whether we, another partner or the local authorities can continue essential support for the people the partner serves.
4. Secure our records and any data or assets the partner holds for us.
5. Tell the funder within the time the grant agreement sets, with a plan.
6. Review how we check and monitor partners.

# Communications in a crisis

## 13. Principles

- **People first.** We tell those most affected before the public, wherever it is safe.
- **Honest and early.** We say what we know, what we do not know and when we will update. We do not speculate.
- **One voice.** Only the spokesperson speaks to the media. Everyone else refers enquiries to [[Name, role]] on [[phone / email]].
- **Safety and privacy.** We never identify survivors, children or anyone at risk. Where speaking publicly could endanger people, their safety decides what we say.
- **Use channels people trust.** Community radio, WhatsApp groups, local leaders or a phone call may matter more than our website. Every message goes in the log.

## 15. Holding statement

A **holding statement** is a short, factual message we use while we gather the facts (Annex D). The spokesperson and crisis lead agree each version; for Level 3 the chair sees it first. Our wider rules are in the Social Media and Communications Policy.

# Recovery and returning to normal

The crisis lead decides, with the board link, when to move to recovery. Recovery means:

1. Restoring critical activities in the order of the critical activities table.
2. Checking that premises, systems and records are safe and complete.
3. Debriefing and supporting the people involved.
4. Closing insurance claims and emergency spending records, and agreeing grant changes with funders.
5. Telling everyone concerned that the crisis is over and what has changed.

The crisis lead then closes the crisis in the log. Within [[30 days]], they hold a **learning review** with the people involved: what worked, what did not, and what we will change. Actions get an owner and a date, go into the Risk Register where relevant and are reported to the board. We look for lessons, not blame.

# Testing and review

## 16. Annual tabletop exercise

Once a year, the crisis team and at least one board member hold a **tabletop exercise**: a 60 to 90 minute discussion of a realistic scenario, revealed in stages. We choose a different scenario each year, starting with our highest risks. We check roles, the contact sheet, workarounds and notification deadlines, and update the plan within [[1 month]].

::: adapt Running a simple exercise
You do not need a consultant. Pick one scenario, for example: "It is Friday evening. The founder is in hospital after a road accident. Payroll is due on Monday." Add a new fact every 15 minutes: the bank will not accept the deputy's instructions; a journalist calls. End by listing three things to fix, and keep the notes as evidence of testing. As you grow, ISO 22301, the international standard for business continuity management, is a useful benchmark.
:::

## 17. Review

We review this plan once a year after the exercise, after every Level 2 or 3 crisis, when the top risks in the Risk Register change, and when we open or close an office, country or major programme. The board approves the plan and major changes. The crisis lead may update contact details and minor points at any time.

# Records

- The crisis lead keeps a log (Annex C) for every Level 2 and 3 crisis: facts, decisions, reasons and who decided, with times. Level 1 incidents are recorded under the Incident Reporting Procedure.
- We keep logs, reports and learning reviews for [[6 years]], or longer if the law, a funder or an investigation requires, as set in our Records Retention Schedule.
- Logs may contain sensitive personal information. Only the crisis team and board can see them, under our Data Protection Policy.
- We keep copies of this plan and the contact sheet where they would survive the crisis: in the cloud, on phones and on paper.
