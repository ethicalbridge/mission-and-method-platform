# Who this policy applies to

This policy applies to staff, volunteers and interns, board members [[trustees / directors / committee members]], and contractors and consultants while they work on our premises or activities. It also protects visitors, participants and members of the public affected by our work.

It applies in **our office** at [[address]]; in **the field**, including project sites, community visits and partner premises; to **home working**; and at **events and community activities** we run or co-run. Work travel is also covered by our Travel Safety and Duty of Care Policy. Where both apply, follow both.

**Law and stricter rules.** [[Organisation name]] is registered in [[country]] and follows its laws on occupational safety and health. Abroad, we also follow local law. Where a landlord, venue, partner or funder has stricter safety rules, those apply while you work with them.

::: adapt Law in your country
Name the country where you are registered and each country where you have staff. Most countries have a general occupational safety and health law placing duties on employers. Examples to confirm locally:

- **Kenya:** the Occupational Safety and Health Act 2007; compensation for work injuries falls under the Work Injury Benefits Act 2007.
- **South Africa:** the Occupational Health and Safety Act 85 of 1993, and the Compensation for Occupational Injuries and Diseases Act 130 of 1993 (COIDA).
- **United Kingdom:** the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 and the Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999, enforced by the Health and Safety Executive (HSE).
- **India:** the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code 2020 consolidates earlier laws such as the Factories Act 1948. Check its current status and the rules in your state.
- **Brazil:** the regulatory norms (Normas Regulamentadoras, NRs) issued by the labour ministry under the labour code (CLT), starting with NR-1 on general provisions and risk management.
- **Elsewhere:** check your local law, including whether its duties also cover volunteers, interns and contractors.

The ILO Occupational Safety and Health Convention 1981 (No. 155) is a useful benchmark. It also protects workers who remove themselves from imminent and serious danger: check how your local law treats this right (section 3).
:::

# What we mean

{widths: 24 76}
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Hazard** | Anything that could cause harm: a wet floor, a frayed cable, a busy road, an aggressive dog, a heavy workload. |
| **Risk** | How likely it is that a hazard causes harm, and how serious that harm could be. |
| **Control** | A step that removes a hazard or reduces the risk, such as a handrail, a second person or a check-in. |
| **Near miss** | An event that could have caused harm but did not, this time. |
| **Lone worker** | Anyone working without a colleague close by who could help quickly (section 15). |

# Our commitments

## 1. What we will do

We take reasonable, practical steps to protect the health, safety and wellbeing of everyone this policy covers. We will:

1. assess risks before work starts, and put sensible controls in place;
2. provide safe premises, equipment and working arrangements, including at home;
3. keep first aid, fire and emergency arrangements in place and known;
4. give everyone the information, training and supervision they need;
5. make sure lone workers can always get help;
6. manage workload and stress, and support mental as well as physical health;
7. learn from every accident, near miss and case of work-related ill health;
8. consult the people doing the work, who often know the risks best;
9. hold the insurance the law requires, and [[any extra cover, e.g. personal accident cover for volunteers]].

## 2. What we expect from you

Take reasonable care of yourself and others. Follow this policy, risk assessments and safety instructions. Report faulty equipment, accidents, near misses and hazards promptly, and check in when you work alone. Tell us about anything that may affect your safety at work, such as medication that causes drowsiness. You choose what to share, and we keep it confidential.

## 3. Your right to stop unsafe work

You can stop work, refuse a task or leave a place if you reasonably believe there is a serious risk to yourself or others. Tell the health and safety lead as soon as you can. You will not be penalised for doing this in good faith. We will either make the work safe or find another way to do it. Managers must never pressure anyone to continue work they believe is unsafe.

# Risk assessment

## 4. When and how we assess risk

We complete a written risk assessment (Annex A) for our office, regular home working, each type of field activity, every event and lone work not covered by a general assessment. We also assess when someone is pregnant, has a relevant health condition or is under 18, and after an accident, near miss or important change. Most assessments fit on one page.

We follow five steps:

1. **Identify the hazards.** Walk round the place, or think through the activity, with the people who do the work. What could hurt someone here?
2. **Decide who could be harmed and how.** Think of everyone affected, especially new, young, lone or pregnant workers and people with disabilities.
3. **Evaluate the risk and decide on controls.** Rate each risk (section 5). Remove the hazard if you can. If not, change how the work is done, add equipment or a second person, give training, and use protective equipment as a last line of defence.
4. **Record** what you found, who is responsible for each control and by when, and share it with everyone affected.
5. **Review** it at least once a year, and sooner after an accident, near miss or change.

## 5. Rating a risk

{widths: 20 27 27 26}
| Likelihood / severity | Minor (first aid only) | Serious (medical treatment or time off) | Major (life-changing injury or death) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unlikely** | Low | Low | Medium |
| **Possible** | Low | Medium | High |
| **Likely** | Medium | High | High |

- **Low:** go ahead with normal care.
- **Medium:** go ahead only once the agreed controls are in place.
- **High:** do not start, or stop, until controls bring the risk down. If that is not possible, the activity does not go ahead without approval from [[Name, role]].

We add high risks, and medium risks that affect many people, to our Risk Register.

::: adapt Risk assessment and the law
Most occupational safety laws require employers to assess risks, and many require a written record. In the United Kingdom, employers with five or more employees must record the significant findings. In Brazil, NR-1 requires a documented occupational risk management programme (PGR), with simplified rules for some small businesses. Check your local law on what must be written down, and whether you need a safety committee or representative above a certain number of workers. For specialised hazards, such as construction or chemicals, get specialist advice.
:::

# First aid and emergencies

## 6. First aid

We keep a first aid kit at [[location]] and in every vehicle and field kit. [[Name, role]] checks and restocks them every [[3 months]]. At least [[one person per site]] with current first aid training is present whenever we run activities. If you work alone in the field, carry a personal kit. Record every use of first aid in Annex C.

## 7. Fire

- Know the fire exits, the assembly point at [[location]] and how to raise the alarm. Keep exits clear and never prop fire doors open. Do not overload sockets.
- If you discover a fire, raise the alarm, leave by the nearest safe exit and call [[fire service number]]. Use an extinguisher only if trained, the fire is small and your exit is behind you.
- [[Name, role]] checks fire equipment every [[6 months]], and we hold a fire drill at least [[once a year]].

## 8. Medical emergencies and evacuation

In a medical emergency: make the area safe; call [[ambulance number]], or take the person to [[nearest hospital or clinic]] if that is faster and safe; give first aid only within your training; stay with them; then tell the health and safety lead.

If told to evacuate, leave by the nearest safe route and go to the assembly point. The most senior person present checks everyone is out, using the attendance list or visitor book. For other emergencies, such as flood, unrest or a threat, follow the site risk assessment: move to safety, stay together and never take risks to protect property.

# Accidents, near misses and reporting

## 9. What to report and when

Report every accident, near miss, case of work-related ill health and unsafe situation, however small. Near misses show where the next accident may happen.

{widths: 55 45}
| What happened | Report to the health and safety lead |
|---|---|
| Death, serious injury, anyone taken to hospital, violence or threats | Immediately, after calling emergency services if needed |
| Any other injury, including first aid only | Within [[24 hours]] |
| Near miss or dangerous situation | Within [[24 hours]] |
| Work-related ill health, including stress | As soon as you are aware of it |

Use Annex C, or phone and complete the form afterwards. Our Incident Reporting Procedure sets out how the lead logs, rates and follows up the report, and who else must be told. Harm to a child or adult at risk is also reported under our Safeguarding Policy and Procedure. The lead follows up every report within [[5 working days]], updates the risk assessment and tells the reporter what changed.

::: adapt Reporting to authorities and insurers
Many countries require employers to report serious work accidents and some diseases to a labour or safety authority, often within days, and to notify a work injury insurer or compensation fund. Examples to confirm locally:

- **Kenya:** check the reporting duties under the Occupational Safety and Health Act 2007 and the Work Injury Benefits Act 2007.
- **South Africa:** reportable incidents under the Occupational Health and Safety Act 1993, and claims under COIDA.
- **United Kingdom:** certain injuries, diseases and dangerous occurrences are reported to HSE under the Reporting of Injuries, Diseases and Dangerous Occurrences Regulations 2013 (RIDDOR).
- **Brazil:** work accidents are notified to social security through a Comunicação de Acidente de Trabalho (CAT), with short deadlines.
- **Elsewhere:** check your local law on what is reportable, to whom and by when.

Add these deadlines to your Incident Reporting Procedure, and check your insurance policy's notice period.
:::

# Safe working

## 10. Premises, equipment and home working

We choose premises that are safe, clean, well lit, ventilated and accessible, with safe exits, toilets and drinking water. Keep walkways clear and clean up spills at once. Do not use damaged cables or equipment until repaired. Get help with heavy loads. If we share premises, we agree with the landlord or host who is responsible for fire safety, first aid and maintenance.

**Home working.** Before you work from home regularly, you complete the home working section of Annex A with your manager. It covers a safe space with adequate light and ventilation, safe sockets and cables, how you stay in touch, and protecting confidential information under our IT and Information Security Policy. We will not visit your home without your agreement.

**Display screens.** If you use a screen for much of your day, set the top at about eye level, with your feet flat and back supported. Use a separate keyboard and stand for long laptop work, and take a short break every [[hour]]. We provide [[basic equipment / an allowance of amount]], and pay for [[an eye test on request for regular screen users]].

::: adapt Home working and screens
Decide what equipment you provide and how you check home set-ups: a self-assessment checklist is usually enough for a small team. In the United Kingdom, the Health and Safety (Display Screen Equipment) Regulations 1992 require workstation assessments and eye tests on request for regular screen users. In Brazil, NR-17 covers ergonomics, and the labour code (CLT) has specific rules on teleworking. Check your local law on teleworking, including who pays for equipment, internet and electricity, and reflect it in your Employment Contract.
:::

## 11. Wellbeing, stress and workload

Stress, burnout and secondary trauma are real risks in purpose-led work, especially for people who hear difficult stories or support people in crisis. We:

- plan realistic workloads and review them in regular one-to-ones;
- expect people to take breaks and leave, and do not expect replies outside working hours except in agreed emergencies;
- include stress in risk assessments, and offer a debrief after distressing events;
- provide access to [[support, e.g. counselling or an employee assistance service]];
- make reasonable adjustments for health conditions or disabilities;
- treat harassment and violence as a safety issue too, under our Anti-Harassment, Intimidation and Bullying Policy and Procedure.

If work is affecting your health, talk to your manager or [[Name, role, someone outside your line management]]. It will not count against you.

::: adapt Wellbeing and psychosocial risks
Psychosocial risks are risks to mental health from how work is designed and managed: workload, isolation, conflict, unclear roles and exposure to distressing situations. ISO 45003:2021 gives international guidance on managing them, and the ILO Violence and Harassment Convention 2019 (No. 190) treats violence and harassment as a workplace risk. In Brazil, recent updates to NR-1 bring psychosocial risks into the occupational risk management programme: check the current requirements and dates.
:::

## 12. Driving and vehicles

Road crashes are among the most serious risks in our work. Drive only with a valid licence, and never under the influence of alcohol, drugs or medication that affects driving. Use roadworthy vehicles insured for work use, including your own. Everyone wears a seat belt, and a helmet on a motorcycle. Do not use a phone while driving unless hands-free and safe. Take a break every [[2 hours]], and drive no more than [[8 hours]] a day. Anyone in a vehicle can ask the driver to slow down or stop. The road safety rules in our Travel Safety and Duty of Care Policy also apply.

## 13. Events and community activities

The activity lead completes a risk assessment at least [[10 working days]] before each event. It covers the venue (capacity, exits, accessibility, water, shade), who is in charge, first aid cover, food safety, weather, transport and, where children or adults at risk take part, safeguarding. At the start, the lead briefs the team on exits, the assembly point and first aiders. Public events may need permission from the local authority.

## 14. Partners, contractors and visitors

For joint activities or work on a partner's premises, we agree in advance who is responsible for safety, first aid and emergencies. We check that contractors are competent and insured. Our Independent Contractor Agreement requires them to follow this policy, or an equally strong one, and to report accidents to us. Visitors sign in, are told about exits and hazards, and are accompanied where needed.

# Lone working

## 15. Who is a lone worker

You are a lone worker whenever no colleague is close by who could see or hear you and help quickly. This includes working alone in the office or at home; home visits and one-to-one meetings with people we serve; field visits done alone; driving alone; and closing a venue after an event. Lone working is not unsafe in itself, but help is harder to get, so someone must always know where you are.

## 16. Before lone work

Regular low-risk lone work, such as daytime office or home working, is covered by a general risk assessment. Other lone work needs a short specific assessment (Annex A), agreed with the lead or your manager: where you will be and who you will meet; any risk of aggression; mobile signal; how you will leave quickly; your experience; and whether a second person, a phone call or a safer venue would do.

**Never alone.** Some work must not be done alone:

- [[visits to people not known to us, or where there is a history of violence or aggression]];
- [[work at height above a set height]];
- [[carrying cash above amount]];
- [[visits to areas rated high risk under our Travel Safety and Duty of Care Policy]];
- any activity where the risk assessment says two people are needed.

People under 18, and new team members in their first [[4 weeks]], do not do lone fieldwork.

## 17. Check-in, check-out and buddies

Every lone worker has a check-in contact. For regular fieldwork or home visits, this is a named **buddy**: a colleague who knows the day's schedule, stays reachable throughout, knows the escalation steps and hands over to the deputy if unavailable. Otherwise it is the lead or deputy.

Before lone work away from your usual workplace, record in Annex B or [[our shared calendar / check-in app or group]]: where you are going, who you are meeting, how you are travelling (with vehicle details), your expected finish time and how to reach you.

{widths: 30 38 32}
| Type of lone work | When to check in | How |
|---|---|---|
| Office or home, in normal hours | Start and end of the day | Message to [[team group / check-in contact]] |
| Office out of hours | On arrival, every [[2 hours]], and on leaving | Message or call |
| Home visit or one-to-one meeting | Before going in, and within [[15 minutes]] of the expected finish | Call or message |
| Field visit or driving alone | On leaving, on arrival, every [[2 hours]], and on reaching home or accommodation | Call, message or location app |
| Late finish or night travel | On leaving, and on arriving home | Call or message; the contact waits for it |

If your plans change, tell your check-in contact before you move. Always check out when you finish: a forgotten check-out starts the escalation steps.

**Code word.** Agree a code phrase, such as "[[Can you send me the blue file?]]", that you can use in a normal-sounding call to say you are in danger. When your check-in contact hears it, they call the police and go straight to stage 4 below. Keep it confidential.

## 18. If someone does not check in

The check-in contact follows these steps. Do not wait longer in the hope that the person has simply forgotten.

{widths: 10 18 50 22}
| Stage | Time after missed check-in | Action | Who |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | [[15 minutes]] | Call and message the lone worker. Try again after [[5 minutes]]. | Check-in contact |
| 2 | [[30 minutes]] | Call the place they were visiting, their host or partner, and any other number in the log. | Check-in contact |
| 3 | [[45 minutes]] | Alert the health and safety lead or deputy. Decide whether two colleagues can safely go to the last known location. | Check-in contact, then lead |
| 4 | [[1 hour]] | If the person is still not found, call the police on [[number]] with all details from the log. | Lead or deputy |
| 5 | After calling the police | Inform [[Name, role]]. Contact the person's emergency contact, guided by the police. Record every action and time. | Lead |

For home visits with a known risk or high-risk field visits, shorten the times: stage 3 at [[15 minutes]] and stage 4 at [[30 minutes]].

When the person is found safe, the check-in contact tells everyone alerted, and the lead records it in Annex C. Missing a check-in by mistake is not a disciplinary matter. Repeatedly forgetting may be.

::: adapt Check-in times and escalation
Set times that match your work: 30 minutes may suit an office in a safe area, while 15 minutes is usual for home visits where aggression is possible. Where coverage is poor, agree fixed check-in points with signal. A buddy who is in a meeting or asleep is not a check-in contact. Check how the police respond in your area. Where they are slow or unsafe to involve, name an alternative, such as a partner organisation, community leader or security company. Test the steps with a practice run once a year.
:::

## 19. Home visits and fieldwork

- Arrange visits in advance, and check any risk information we hold about the address.
- On arrival, assess the situation. If you feel uneasy, do not go in, or leave politely. Trust your instincts.
- Sit near the exit, keep your phone and keys with you, and do not accept alcohol.
- If someone becomes aggressive, stay calm, do not argue and leave. Your safety matters more than finishing the visit.
- Park so you can leave easily, with nothing valuable or confidential on show.

If you felt unsafe, tell the lead the same day and complete Annex C. Future visits there may need two people.

## 20. Travelling at night

Plan work so you can travel home in daylight, or travel with someone. If you must travel alone at night, use [[a registered taxi or ride service / an approved driver]], share the vehicle details with your check-in contact and avoid walking through isolated areas. We pay for safe transport under our Expenses and Per Diem Policy. Check in when you leave and when you get home: your contact waits for your message and starts escalation if it does not arrive.

## 21. Personal safety devices and apps

Where the risk assessment shows a need, we provide a personal alarm, a location-sharing app or a device with an emergency button. These support, but never replace, check-ins and good judgement. Location sharing is on only during the visit or working time, visible only to the check-in contact and the lead, and deleted after [[30 days]] unless needed for an incident.

::: adapt Personal safety devices and data
For most small teams a charged phone and a clear check-in routine are enough. If you use an app or device, check that it works where you work and who receives its alerts. Location data is personal data: tell people what is collected and why, and cover it in your Data Protection Policy. Some data protection laws expect a Data Protection Impact Assessment before monitoring workers' location. Never use tracking to monitor performance.
:::

## 22. Training

Everyone has a health and safety induction within [[their first week]]. People who do lone fieldwork or home visits are trained in [[personal safety and de-escalation]] before they start, with a refresher every [[2 years]].

# Records, confidentiality and data

We keep risk assessments for [[3 years]] after they are replaced; lone working logs for [[6 months]] unless linked to an incident; and accident reports for [[6 years]], or longer if a claim is possible or the person injured was under 18, as set out in our Records Retention Schedule. These records contain personal data, sometimes about health. We store them securely in [[location or system]], share them only where needed to keep someone safe or meet a legal duty, and delete them on time, under our Data Protection Policy.

# If this policy is breached

If someone ignores safety instructions, skips lone working checks or fails to report an accident, the lead discusses it with them and their manager. Serious or repeated breaches that put people at risk may be dealt with under our Disciplinary and Grievance Procedure, or may end a volunteer role or contract. A manager who pressures someone into unsafe work, or punishes them for raising a safety concern, is also in breach. Concerns can be raised through our Whistleblowing Policy and Procedure.

# Monitoring and review

The lead reviews all accident, near-miss and lone working reports every [[quarter]] for patterns. We review this policy every [[12 months]], after any serious accident or near miss, and when our premises, activities or the law change.
