# Who this policy applies to

This policy applies to everyone who handles personal information for [[Organisation name]]: staff, volunteers, interns, board members [[trustees / directors / committee members]], contractors and consultants. Contractors and providers follow it through their contract.

It covers personal information in any form: databases, spreadsheets, email, messaging apps, cloud drives, paper, photos, voice notes and video. It applies in the office, in the field, at home and on personal devices used for work.

**Personal information** (also called personal data) is any information that identifies a living person, alone or combined with other information: a name, phone number, photo, voice recording, ID number, location, case note, or a story detailed enough for neighbours to recognise the person.

**Law.** [[Organisation name]] is registered in [[country]] and follows its data protection law, [[name of law and year]]. When we work in, or collect information from people in, other countries, we also follow the law that applies there. Where rules differ, we follow the one that gives people more protection. We use the principles of the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) as our benchmark, because it is widely recognised by funders and partners.

# Our commitments

## 1. The principles we follow

Every use of personal information follows these seven principles, and we keep records that show it.

1. **Lawful, fair and transparent.** We have a lawful reason for each use (section 2), avoid uses people would not expect, and explain what we do in our Privacy Notice.
2. **Purpose limitation.** We collect information for a specific purpose and do not reuse it for an unrelated one. We never sell personal information.
3. **Data minimisation.** We collect only what each activity needs. If a total, an age range or an anonymous code will do, we do not collect names or exact dates of birth.
4. **Accuracy.** We keep information up to date and correct mistakes quickly.
5. **Storage limitation.** We keep information only as long as we need it, following our Records Retention Schedule, then delete or anonymise it.
6. **Security.** We protect information against loss, theft and misuse (section 11 and our IT and Information Security Policy).
7. **Accountability.** We document our decisions: the data inventory (Annex A), our list of processors (Annex B), DPIAs, the rights request log and the breach log.

## 2. Our lawful reasons for using personal information

Each use of personal information needs one main lawful reason, sometimes called a lawful basis. We record it in Annex A before the activity starts.

{widths: 26 40 34}
| Lawful reason | In plain words | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Consent | The person has freely agreed, after a clear explanation, and can withdraw at any time. | Newsletters; photos and stories; optional research. |
| Contract | We need it to carry out an agreement with the person, or steps they ask for before one. | Employment and volunteer agreements; a service someone signed up for. |
| Legal obligation | A law requires us to use or keep it. | Tax, payroll and financial records. |
| Vital interests | We need it to protect someone's life or physical safety, and they cannot give consent. | Sharing medical details with emergency services after an accident. |
| Public task | We carry out a task set by law or for a public authority. | Statutory services delivered for government. |
| Legitimate interests | We have a genuine reason, the use is necessary, and it does not override the person's rights. | Securing our systems; handling a complaint. |

**Consent must be real.** Where we rely on consent, it is a clear, separate action: a tick box that is not pre-ticked, a signature, or a recorded verbal agreement. We record who agreed, when, how and to what. Withdrawing must be as easy as agreeing. Consent is not free if refusing means losing a service, so we never make assistance conditional on agreeing to photos, stories or research. We use our Photo and Story Consent Form, Research Participation Consent Form and Fundraising and Communications Consent Form.

**Legitimate interests need a check.** The data protection lead first writes a short note: why we need it, whether a less intrusive way exists, the impact on people and our safeguards. It is kept with Annex A.

::: adapt Lawful reasons: names differ by country
The six reasons follow the GDPR. Many laws use similar ones, but names and lists differ by country. For example, Brazil's LGPD has ten legal bases, including protection of credit and health protection. India's DPDP Act relies mainly on consent and a short list of "legitimate uses", and has no general legitimate interests basis. South Africa's POPIA sets out similar grounds in section 11. Check the list in your own law, and rename the reasons in the table if needed. Delete "Public task" if you do not deliver statutory services.
:::

## 3. Sensitive information and criminal records

Some information can lead to discrimination, violence or legal danger if misused. It includes a person's:

- physical or mental health, disability, HIV status or pregnancy;
- racial or ethnic origin, caste or tribe;
- religious or philosophical beliefs;
- political opinions or trade union membership;
- sexual life, sexual orientation or gender identity;
- genetic or biometric data used to identify them, such as fingerprints or face scans;
- criminal allegations, convictions or offences.

Other information can be just as dangerous in our contexts, such as refugee status, survivor status in gender-based violence cases, or exact location. We treat it as sensitive too.

We collect sensitive information only when necessary for a clear purpose, such as a health or protection service or a legal duty. Then we:

- rely on explicit consent or another condition the law allows, and record it in Annex A;
- restrict access to the people who need it for their task;
- store it separately or encrypted, never in shared spreadsheets or group chats;
- complete a DPIA before starting any large-scale or new use (section 14);
- share it only in line with our Safeguarding Policy and Procedure or where the law requires.

**Criminal records.** We ask about criminal records only where the role justifies it, such as work with children, and as far as the law allows. We record the outcome ("check completed, no concerns") and delete certificates after [[6 months]] unless the law requires otherwise.

::: adapt Sensitive information in your work
List the sensitive information you actually hold, such as clinic records, disability data or protection case notes. If you hold none, say so, but keep the rules for free text and messages, where it often appears. Check your law: POPIA, for example, requires prior authorisation from the Regulator for some uses of special or criminal information. Many countries restrict what employers may ask about criminal records.
:::

## 4. Children's information

Children may be less aware of the risks, so their information needs extra care. We collect it only when our activities require it. We:

- ask for the consent of a parent or guardian where consent is our lawful reason and the child is under [[age set by law]], and also explain to the child in words they understand;
- collect the minimum, and avoid names in reports, photos and stories wherever possible;
- never publish information or images that could identify a child at risk or reveal where they live or go to school;
- follow our Safeguarding Policy and Procedure, which sets out how child protection concerns are recorded and shared;
- complete a DPIA before any new activity that processes children's information on a large scale or online.

::: adapt Age of consent for data
The age at which a child can consent to the use of their own data varies. Under the GDPR it is 16 for online services, and EU countries may lower it to 13; in the UK it is 13. India's DPDP Act treats everyone under 18 as a child and requires verifiable parental consent. Brazil's LGPD requires a parent's specific consent where consent is used for children under 12. Check your law and insert the age. Even where the law allows a child to consent, ask a parent or guardian too when the activity involves sensitive information or any risk.
:::

## 5. Information about different groups of people

### The people we serve

This is often the most sensitive information we hold. We:

- explain at the point of collection, in a language and format people understand, what we collect and why, and read it aloud where literacy is low;
- use anonymous or coded data in reports to funders, unless a funder needs names and the person has been told;
- collect information for monitoring and evaluation only when we will use it, and prefer totals and percentages;
- never let a request from a funder, partner or authority override the safety of the people we serve.

### Staff, volunteers and applicants

We hold recruitment, contract, payroll, performance, health and emergency contact information about our team. Only [[Name, role]] and the person's manager see it, as needed.

### Donors and supporters

We hold contact details, donation history and preferences. We send fundraising messages only to people who agreed or, where the law allows, to existing supporters who have not opted out. Every message has an easy unsubscribe. We never buy or rent mailing lists.

::: adapt Fundraising and marketing rules
Marketing emails and texts are often regulated separately: in the UK by the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations (PECR), in the EU by national ePrivacy rules, and in South Africa by POPIA section 69, which generally requires opt-in consent for electronic direct marketing. In the UK, since 5 February 2026 charities may email or text people who gave their details while supporting or showing interest in the charity's purposes, if they offered a simple opt-out (the "charitable purpose soft opt-in"); check ICO guidance. Elsewhere, check whether a "soft opt-in" exists. If you do not fundraise from individuals, shorten this part.
:::

## 6. Privacy notices and consent records

We tell people what we do with their information when we collect it. Our Privacy Notice explains who we are, what we collect, why, who we share it with, how long we keep it and people's rights. We give a short version at the point of collection: on a form, a poster or a script read aloud. The data protection lead keeps a record of consents: who, when, how, the wording used, and any withdrawal.

## 7. Individuals' rights

People have rights over their information. Depending on the law that applies, they can ask to:

- **access** a copy of the information we hold about them;
- **correct** information that is wrong or incomplete;
- **delete** information we no longer need or have no lawful reason to keep;
- **restrict** or **object** to some uses, including direct marketing, which we always stop;
- **receive** information they gave us in a portable format;
- **withdraw consent** at any time, without affecting what we did before;
- ask for **human review** of any decision made about them by automated means alone;
- **complain** to the data protection regulator.

A request can be made in any way, to anyone in our team, without legal words. Whoever receives it passes it to the data protection lead within [[1 working day]]. We confirm identity proportionately, respond free of charge within [[one month]], and log every request. Our Data Rights Request Procedure sets out each step.

## 8. Sharing with partners, funders and others

We share personal information only when it is necessary, lawful and safe, and only the minimum needed.

- **Partners.** Before sharing personal information with a partner regularly, we sign a Data Sharing Agreement: what is shared, why, how it is protected, how long it is kept and what happens in a breach. For one-off sharing about an individual, we use the Information Sharing Consent Form or record another lawful reason.
- **Funders.** We send totals and anonymised case studies by default. If a funder requires names, for example for audit, we check the grant agreement, tell the people concerned and use a secure channel.
- **Authorities.** Only where the law requires it or someone is in immediate danger. The data protection lead decides, records the reason and shares the minimum. If a request could put people at risk, we take legal advice first where time allows.
- **Emergencies.** If a life is at risk, share what is needed and tell the data protection lead afterwards.

## 9. Processors and cloud services

We are the **controller** for our own activities: we decide why and how personal information is used. A **processor** is a provider that handles it only on our instructions. Many processors work for us: email, cloud drives, messaging apps, survey tools, accounting and payroll software, customer relationship management (CRM) systems and website hosts. Before we use a new provider that will hold personal information, the data protection lead:

1. checks that it has adequate security, such as encryption and multi-factor sign-in;
2. makes sure a data processing agreement is in place (often part of the provider's terms) that limits use of our data to our instructions;
3. records where it stores data and what transfer safeguard applies (section 10);
4. checks whether it uses our data to train its own AI models, and switches this off where possible;
5. adds it to the list of processors in Annex B.

We use organisational accounts, not personal ones, for any tool that holds personal information. Free consumer versions often give weaker protection, so we check their terms first.

## 10. International transfers

Personal information often leaves the country unnoticed. Cloud drives, email, survey tools and messaging apps may store data on servers abroad. Emailing a spreadsheet to a partner in another country is also a transfer. Many laws allow transfers only with safeguards, so before using such a provider or sending data abroad, we check that one of these applies:

- the destination country is recognised by our regulator as giving adequate protection;
- the provider or recipient has signed standard contractual clauses or another approved safeguard;
- the person has given informed consent, for occasional transfers;
- another exception in our law applies, recorded in Annex A.

::: adapt International transfers
Check the transfer rules in your law. Under the GDPR, transfers need an adequacy decision (for the United States, this covers companies certified under the EU–US Data Privacy Framework), standard contractual clauses or another safeguard. Other laws have their own rules: some require the regulator's approval or the person's consent, and a few require certain data to be stored in the country. If your team uses a messaging app to share participant information, check where it stores messages and backups, and move sensitive case work to an approved tool.
:::

## 11. Security

We protect personal information in proportion to the harm its loss would cause. Our IT and Information Security Policy sets out the detail. The basics:

- **Accounts.** Organisational accounts only, a password manager, unique passwords and multi-factor sign-in on email and every system that holds personal information.
- **Access.** Each person sees only what their task needs. We review access every [[6 months]] and remove it on the day someone leaves.
- **Devices.** Encrypted laptops and phones, screen lock, automatic updates and the ability to wipe a lost device remotely.
- **Sharing files.** Share with named people, not "anyone with the link", and never send sensitive information in group chats.
- **Paper.** Lock records away, take only what you need to the field, and shred what is no longer needed.
- **Field data.** Phones and tablets used to collect participant information are encrypted, and data is uploaded and removed from the device as soon as possible.

## 12. Retention and deletion

We keep personal information only as long as its purpose or the law requires. Our Records Retention Schedule sets the period for each type of record and what happens at the end: secure deletion, anonymisation or review. In short:

- **Participant and case records:** [[X years]] after our work with the person ends. Safeguarding records follow our Safeguarding Policy and Procedure and may be kept longer.
- **Staff and volunteer records:** [[X years]] after the person leaves.
- **Financial and donation records:** [[X years]], as tax and funder rules require.
- **Unsuccessful job applications:** [[6 months]].
- **Marketing contacts:** until the person unsubscribes; reviewed every [[2 years]].

Each year the data protection lead checks that deletion has happened, including copies in email, downloads and devices.

::: adapt Retention periods
Set real periods with your accountant and your funders' requirements in mind. Tax and accounting laws often require financial records to be kept for several years, and many grant agreements set their own periods. Employment, health and children's records may have legal periods too. Check your local law, record the reason for each period, and do not keep things "just in case".
:::

## 13. Data breaches

A data breach can be as simple as an email to the wrong person, a lost phone, a link anyone can open, or a hacked account. It can also happen at a provider.

**If you suspect a breach, report it immediately**, and within [[1 hour]] of noticing it at the latest, to the data protection lead at [[privacy@yourorganisation.org]] or [[phone]]. Do not wait until you are sure. Do not try to investigate alone, and do not delete evidence.

The data protection lead follows our Data Breach Response Procedure: contain the breach, assess the risk to people, decide whether to notify the regulator and those affected, and record every breach, even those not reported. Under the GDPR, a breach likely to put people at risk must be reported to the regulator within 72 hours of becoming aware of it. Other laws set similar or shorter deadlines (see the country notes). If anyone may be in danger, we follow our Safeguarding Policy and Procedure first.

## 14. Privacy by design and impact assessments

We think about privacy at the start of every new project or system. The data protection lead first does a short screening: what personal information is involved, is it all needed, who will see it, where it is stored and for how long.

We complete a full Data Protection Impact Assessment before any activity likely to create a high risk to people, for example:

- collecting sensitive information, or information about children or adults at risk, on a large scale;
- using biometrics, such as fingerprints or face scans, for registration or identity checks;
- tracking people's location, or monitoring people systematically;
- using AI or automated tools to assess, score or select people;
- sharing data about vulnerable groups with authorities or new partners.

[[Name, role]] approves each DPIA. If high risk remains after safeguards, we take advice and, where the law requires, consult the regulator before starting.

## 15. AI tools and automated decisions

Anything typed or uploaded into an AI tool may be stored, reviewed or used to train models.

- Use only AI tools approved in our IT and Information Security Policy, through organisational accounts, with training on our data switched off where possible.
- Never enter personal, safeguarding or confidential information into an AI tool not approved for that purpose.
- Check everything AI produces for accuracy and bias. You remain responsible for your work.
- No significant decision about anyone, such as selection for support or recruitment, is made by automated means alone.
- Any new use of AI that assesses people needs a DPIA first.

# Training and awareness

Everyone who handles personal information completes a short data protection briefing within [[their first two weeks]]: this policy, security basics and how to report a breach. Staff who collect information from the people we serve also learn how to explain privacy, take consent and keep field data safe. Everyone has a refresher each year, and after any significant breach. The data protection lead records who was trained and when.

# Records, confidentiality and data

The data protection lead keeps the data inventory, processor list, legitimate interests notes, DPIAs, consent records, request and breach logs and training records securely; only the lead and deputy can access them. Everyone who handles personal information for us keeps it confidential, during and after their time with us.

# If this policy is breached

Misusing personal information, ignoring security rules or failing to report a breach can cause real harm. We may give guidance or training, remove access, or act under our Disciplinary and Grievance Procedure, up to dismissal or ending a contract or volunteer role. Deliberate misuse can also be a criminal offence in some countries.

You can raise concerns about how personal information is handled with the data protection lead, the deputy, or through our Whistleblowing Policy and Procedure. Nobody is penalised for raising a genuine concern.

# Monitoring and review

The data protection lead reviews this policy and its annexes every year, and sooner after a serious breach, a new activity, system or provider, or a change in the law. Each year the lead reports to the board on requests, breaches and lessons learned, DPIAs, training, and any change in registration or DPO duties.
