# Part 1: The board member role

## 1. About the organisation

[[Organisation name]] is a [[non-profit association / non-profit company / trust / social enterprise / co-operative]] registered in [[country]] under [[registration number]]. Our mission is [[one sentence]]. We work in [[countries or regions]] with [[who we serve]]. We have [[number]] staff and [[number]] volunteers, and an annual budget of about [[amount and currency]].

## 2. Purpose of the role

Board members, together, make sure that [[Organisation name]] stays true to its mission, is well run, is solvent and keeps people safe. They set direction, approve key decisions, support the [[Executive Director / founder]] and hold them to account. A board member brings independent judgement, relevant skills and experience, and connections that help us serve our communities better.

## 3. Your legal duties

When you join the board you take on legal duties to the organisation. The exact wording differs between countries and legal forms, but the core duties are similar in most systems. You must:

1. **Act in the organisation's interests.** Act in good faith, for the purposes set out in our governing document and for the benefit of the people we serve. Do not act for yourself, your employer, a funder or whoever nominated you.
2. **Use reasonable care and skill.** Prepare for meetings, ask questions, read the accounts, take advice where you need it, and use the knowledge and experience you have. A member with professional skills, such as an accountant, is expected to use them.
3. **Avoid conflicts of interest.** Declare any interest that could affect, or seem to affect, your judgement, and step out of decisions where you are conflicted. Do not benefit personally from your position, and do not accept gifts or hospitality connected to your role except as our Gift Acceptance Policy allows.
4. **Comply with the governing document and the law.** Make sure the organisation acts within its powers and purposes, keeps its registration in good standing, files its returns, and complies with the law and its funding agreements.
5. **Manage resources responsibly.** Make sure money, property, data and reputation are protected and used only for our purposes; that proper accounts are kept; that risks are managed; and that the organisation does not take on commitments it cannot meet.
6. **Act collectively and be accountable.** Decisions belong to the board as a whole. Once the board decides, support the decision in public, even if you argued against it, unless it is unlawful or unsafe. Be ready to explain the board's decisions to members, funders, regulators and the communities we serve.

::: adapt Your legal duties: country notes
Check the duties that apply to your legal form and country, and name the law in the text if it helps. Examples:

- **England and Wales (charities):** the Charity Commission's guidance "The essential trustee" (CC3) sets out six main duties: ensure the charity carries out its purposes for the public benefit; comply with the governing document and the law; act in the charity's best interests; manage resources responsibly; act with reasonable care and skill; and ensure the charity is accountable. Charitable companies' directors also have the general duties in the Companies Act 2006, sections 171 to 177.
- **South Africa:** directors of a non-profit company must meet the standards of conduct in section 76 of the Companies Act 2008 (good faith, proper purpose, best interests, and care, skill and diligence).
- **India:** directors of a Section 8 company have the duties in section 166 of the Companies Act 2013. Trustees of a public trust and members of a society's governing body follow the trust or society law of their state: check your local law.
- **Kenya:** the Companies Act 2015 sets out directors' general duties for companies limited by guarantee. Public benefit organisations and societies follow other laws: check your local law.
- **Nigeria:** directors of companies have duties under the Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020. For incorporated trustees, check the trust deed and Part F of the same Act.
- **United States:** non-profit directors are generally described as owing duties of care, loyalty and obedience, under the law of the state of incorporation. Check your state law.
- **Latin America and civil-law countries:** duties of association board members usually come from the statute and the Civil Code. Check your local law.

If you are not sure, keep the six generic duties above, which reflect principles common to most systems, and do not add legal references you have not checked.
:::

## 4. Responsibilities

As a board member you will:

- help set and review our mission, strategy and annual plan, and monitor progress against them;
- approve the annual budget and accounts, and review the management accounts and cash position at every meeting;
- approve core policies and make sure they are followed, especially on safeguarding, finance, fraud, data protection and conduct;
- oversee the main risks to the organisation and to the people we serve, using the Risk Register;
- appoint, support, appraise and, if necessary, remove the [[Executive Director / founder]], and set their pay;
- take the decisions reserved to the board and delegate the rest through the Delegation of Authority Matrix;
- protect our reputation and act as an ambassador, opening doors to funders, partners and communities;
- help recruit new board members and plan succession;
- take part in the annual board review and keep your skills and knowledge up to date.

## 5. What board members do not do

Board members do not manage staff, give instructions to staff or volunteers, or take operational decisions, unless the board has delegated a specific task in writing. If you volunteer for us in another role, for example as a trainer, you work under the staff member who manages that activity, and you keep the two roles separate. Concerns about staff go to the [[Executive Director / founder]]; concerns about the [[Executive Director / founder]] go to the chair or the safeguarding lead board member.

::: adapt Time and accessibility
Be honest about the time involved: underestimating it is a common cause of disengaged boards. Make the role possible for people from the communities you serve: hold meetings at suitable times, offer video and phone access, send papers in plain language and in [[language(s)]], and pay costs such as transport, data or childcare in advance where needed.
:::

## 7. Skills and qualities

**Every board member should have:**

- commitment to our mission and values, and to the people we serve;
- willingness to give the time needed, to prepare and to attend;
- independent judgement, and the confidence to ask challenging questions respectfully;
- the ability to read and discuss financial information, with support where needed;
- integrity, discretion and respect for confidentiality;
- understanding of the difference between governance and management.

**The board as a whole needs** the skills and experience listed in our Board Skills Matrix. When we recruit, the advert says which of these we are looking for, for example finance, safeguarding, lived experience of the issues we work on, or knowledge of [[country or region]].

## 8. Eligibility and checks

You must be at least [[18]] years old and not disqualified by law from being a [[trustee / director]]. Before appointment you will complete a Board Interest Declaration, give [[two]] references and, where the law allows and the role requires it, complete a safeguarding or criminal record check under our Safer Recruitment Procedure. You must tell the chair at once if anything changes that could affect your eligibility.

::: adapt Disqualification and checks
Many countries bar certain people from serving on a company or charity board, for example people who are bankrupt, disqualified as company directors, or convicted of offences involving dishonesty. Some NGO laws also require a minimum number of citizens or residents on the board, or require board changes to be notified to the registrar. The rules differ widely, so check your local law. Criminal record checks are only lawful for some roles in some countries; follow your Safer Recruitment Procedure and your data protection law.
:::

## 9. Terms of office

Board members are appointed for [[3]] years and may be reappointed for up to [[two]] further terms, a maximum of [[9]] years in a row. You may resign at any time by writing to the chair, ideally giving [[3 months']] notice so we can plan. You will stop being a member if you are disqualified by law, miss [[three]] meetings in a row without reasons the board accepts, or are removed under the governing document.

## 10. Expenses and payment

The role is [[unpaid]]. We reimburse reasonable costs of board business, such as travel, accommodation, data or phone credit and care costs, under our Expenses and Per Diem Policy, with receipts. We can pay in advance where that makes it possible for you to take part. You may be paid for other services to us only if our governing document and the law allow it, the board approves it in advance with you absent, and it is recorded in the register of interests and disclosed in our accounts.

::: adapt Payment and liability
In many countries non-profit board members must be unpaid unless the governing document or the regulator allows payment. In England and Wales, for example, charity trustees cannot normally be paid for being trustees without authority, and the Charities Act 2011 allows payment for other services only under strict conditions. In some co-operatives and associations a small allowance is usual. Check your local law, governing document and funders' rules.

Board members who act honestly and reasonably are rarely held personally liable, but the risk depends on your legal form: members of an unincorporated association may carry more personal risk than directors of a company. Consider trustee indemnity or directors' and officers' insurance, and tell candidates whether you have it.
:::

## 11. Induction and support

Within [[3 months]] of joining you will receive:

- our governing document, the Governing Body Terms of Reference, the strategy, the latest accounts and budget, the Risk Register and our core policies;
- a meeting with the chair and the [[Executive Director / founder]];
- safeguarding training and an introduction to our Code of Conduct;
- where possible, a visit to our work or a meeting with people we serve;
- a "buddy" on the board for your first year, if you wish.

Each year the chair meets each member to discuss how the role is going and any training they would like.

# Part 2: Founder or staff on the board

If the [[Executive Director / founder]] or another staff member is a board member, they have the same legal duties as any other member. They do not chair the board or act as treasurer, and they step out of decisions on their own pay, appraisal, contract or conduct. They make clear, in meetings, when they speak as staff and when as a board member.

::: adapt Staff and founders as board members
Some laws and regulators do not allow paid staff to be voting board members of a charity or public benefit organisation; in others it is common. Many funders prefer that independent members are a clear majority. Decide whether your founder is a voting member, a non-voting attendee or staff only, and say so here. Check your local law and governing document.
:::

# Part 3: Officer roles

Officers are board members with extra responsibilities. The board elects them for [[3]] years, renewable [[once]].

## 12. Chair

**Purpose:** leads the board so that it works well as a team and makes clear, timely decisions.

**Main tasks:**

- plans the board calendar and agrees agendas with the [[Executive Director / founder]];
- chairs meetings fairly, makes sure every member is heard, and summarises each decision for the minutes;
- is the main link between the board and the [[Executive Director / founder]], meeting them at least [[monthly]], and leads their annual appraisal;
- takes urgent decisions between meetings only within the limits in the Governing Body Terms of Reference, and reports them;
- leads the annual board review, recruitment and succession planning, using the Board Skills Matrix;
- signs approved minutes and represents the board externally when needed.

**Extra time:** about [[3–5 hours a month]]. **Skills:** experience of leading meetings or groups, tact, and the ability to manage disagreement.

## 13. Treasurer

**Purpose:** leads the board's oversight of money, so that the board understands the finances and the organisation stays solvent.

**Main tasks:**

- reviews the management accounts and cash forecast before each meeting and presents them in plain language;
- leads the board's review of the budget, the Reserves Policy and the annual accounts;
- checks that the Financial Controls Policy is followed and that bank reconciliations are reviewed;
- is the board's contact with the auditor or independent examiner;
- acts as a bank signatory or approver [[if the board decides]], within the Delegation of Authority Matrix.

The treasurer does not keep the books or make payments: that is a management task.

**Extra time:** about [[3–4 hours a month]]. **Skills:** a finance or accounting background, or strong experience of budgets and accounts.

## 14. Secretary

**Purpose:** makes sure the board runs properly and its records are complete.

**Main tasks:**

- sends notice and papers on time and makes sure minutes are taken, using the Board Meeting Agenda and Minutes template;
- keeps the minute book, the decisions and actions log, the register of members and the register of interests;
- manages written resolutions between meetings;
- with the [[Executive Director / founder]], files returns and notices with the registrar and regulators on time;
- checks that board decisions follow the governing document.

**Extra time:** about [[2–4 hours a month]]. **Skills:** organisation, attention to detail and good writing.

::: adapt Officer titles and tasks
Use the titles in your governing document: many associations and societies must have a president, secretary and treasurer, and some have a vice-chair or a fiscal council. A staff member or company secretary may do the secretary's administrative tasks without voting; if so, say so. In a board of three to five, one person should not hold both the chair and treasurer roles.
:::

# Part 4: Code of expectations

As a board member of [[Organisation name]], I will:

1. put the mission and the people we serve first, and act only in the organisation's interests;
2. prepare for, attend and take an active part in meetings, and tell the chair in advance if I cannot attend;
3. declare any conflict of interest as soon as I am aware of it, and complete a Board Interest Declaration each year;
4. keep board business and personal information confidential, during and after my term;
5. respect the difference between governance and management, and not give instructions to staff;
6. treat everyone with respect and follow our Code of Conduct and Safeguarding Policy and Procedure;
7. support board decisions once they are made, raising concerns inside the board;
8. not use my position for personal gain, or accept gifts beyond our Gift Acceptance Policy;
9. tell the chair at once of anything that could affect my eligibility or our reputation;
10. take part in induction, training and the annual board review.

If I have a serious concern that the board will not address, I may raise it under our Whistleblowing Policy and Procedure or with a regulator.
