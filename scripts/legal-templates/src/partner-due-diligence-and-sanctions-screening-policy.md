# Who this policy applies to

This policy applies to staff, volunteers, interns and board members of [[Organisation name]] who start, manage or approve the relationships below.

It covers our relationships with:

- **implementing partners and sub-grantees**: organisations that receive our funds, goods or equipment to carry out activities;
- **strategic partners** that share responsibility for a project with us, even if no money passes between us;
- **major suppliers and consultants**, as set out in our Procurement Policy;
- **large donors and sponsors**: organisations or individuals who give us more than [[amount and currency]] in a year, as set out in our Gift Acceptance Policy;
- **anyone else** who will hold our funds, handle sensitive personal data for us or have contact with the people we serve on our behalf.

**Law and stricter rules.** [[Organisation name]] is registered in [[country]] and follows its laws, including any national sanctions and counter-terrorist financing laws. We also follow the sanctions regimes that apply to us because of where we operate, the currencies we use and the conditions of our funders. Where a funder's grant agreement sets stricter due diligence or screening rules, those rules apply to every relationship funded by that grant. Where rules differ, we follow the stricter one.

::: adapt Law and countries
Name the country where you are registered and any others where you work. Many countries have national laws on terrorist financing and sanctions, and some publish their own national designation lists in addition to the UN list. Check your local law on whether you must screen, which lists apply and whether you must report a match to a national authority, such as a financial intelligence unit. Your bank may also have requirements: ask them. If you receive US funding, pay in US dollars or have US citizens on your team, US sanctions rules may apply to you. EU and UK sanctions apply to EU and UK nationals and organisations, and to activity in those places. Take local legal advice if you are unsure.

# Policy

## 1. Why we carry out due diligence

We carry out due diligence to:

- make sure the people and organisations we work with are real, legally able to work with us and not involved in fraud, abuse, terrorism or sanctions breaches;
- protect the people we serve, by checking that partners in contact with them keep them safe;
- protect our funds and our funders' funds;
- understand a partner's strengths and gaps, so we can agree the right support and level of oversight;
- meet the law and our funders' conditions.

Due diligence is part of building a partnership, not a test to pass or fail. We explain why we ask each question. We accept that partners may also want to check us, and we answer their questions openly.

## 2. Our principles

1. **Proportionate.** The depth of checks matches the risk and the amount involved. We do not ask a small community group receiving [[amount]] for the same evidence as a partner managing [[amount]].
2. **Risk-based, not prejudiced.** We judge real risks, using evidence. We do not treat an organisation as high risk simply because it is small, informal, faith-based, led by a particular group or working in a fragile or conflict-affected area.
3. **Fair to local partners.** We do not push risks or costs onto partners without giving them the time, money and support to manage them.
4. **Support before exclusion.** Where we find gaps, we agree a plan to close them, unless the risk is too serious to manage.
5. **Reuse, don't repeat.** We accept recent assessments by other reputable funders or networks where they cover what we need, so partners are not asked the same questions again and again.
6. **Confidential and respectful.** We collect only the information we need, keep it securely and use it only for due diligence and partnership management.

## 3. FATF Recommendation 8 and avoiding unfair de-risking

The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) sets international standards against money laundering and terrorist financing. Its Recommendation 8 concerns non-profit organisations. It asks governments to take a focused, risk-based approach that protects non-profits from abuse for terrorist financing without disrupting legitimate charitable work. FATF has recognised that badly applied rules have led banks, funders and governments to cut off whole groups of non-profits, a practice known as de-risking, and that this harms communities and local civil society.

We apply the same thinking. Most non-profits face a low risk of terrorist financing. We concentrate our effort where there is a real, identified risk, for example cash-based activities in areas where a listed group operates. We do not refuse to work with a partner because of its location or profile alone. Where risks are higher, we manage them with sensible controls, such as payment in instalments, closer monitoring or extra screening, rather than withdrawing.

::: adapt FATF and your country
FATF updated Recommendation 8 and its guidance for non-profits in November 2023 to strengthen this risk-based approach. Check the current text on the FATF website. Your country's latest FATF mutual evaluation, or the national risk assessment for the non-profit sector if your government has published one, shows how your authorities view risk in your sector. Use it to explain your approach to funders. If a funder's requirement would exclude a capable local partner, say so, and propose proportionate controls instead.
:::

## 5. Proportionate levels of checks

We set the level using two questions: how much money or responsibility is involved, and how risky the activity and setting are. Where the answers point to different levels, we use the higher one.

{widths: 18 32 50}
| Level | Usually applies when | What we do |
|---|---|---|
| Light | Total value up to [[amount]] in a year; low-risk activity; no direct contact with children or adults at risk | Sanctions screening; proof of legal existence or, for informal groups, a reference from a trusted source; bank details in the organisation's name; signed commitment to our Code of Conduct |
| Standard | Value from [[amount]] to [[amount]]; or contact with children or adults at risk; or handling personal data for us | Everything in light, plus the Partner Due Diligence Questionnaire, key documents, a call or visit, and a written risk rating |

Set amounts that fit your budget and the size of your partners. Some funders also require their own approval of each sub-grantee: write that into your timeline.

## 6. What we check

For standard and enhanced levels, the Partner Due Diligence Questionnaire collects most of this information. We accept different kinds of evidence. What matters is that we understand the risk, not that a partner produces a particular document.

{widths: 22 43 35}
| Area | What we look at | Evidence we can accept |
|---|---|---|
| Legal registration | Legal name, registration, purpose, authority to receive funds | Registration certificate, constitution, tax number; or for informal groups, a letter from a local authority or a known network |
| Governance | Who leads and oversees the organisation, how decisions are made, conflicts of interest | List of board or committee members, minutes, conflict of interest records |
| Finance and controls | Bank account, budgeting, approvals, separation of duties, cash handling, accounts | Bank letter, latest accounts or audit, finance procedures, a sample of records |
| Safeguarding and PSEAH | Policy, safe recruitment, reporting routes, how concerns are handled | Safeguarding policy, code of conduct, a recent example of how a concern was handled |
| Fraud and corruption | Policy, past incidents, how fraud is reported | Anti-fraud policy or commitment, explanation of any past case |
| Data protection | What personal data they will handle for us and how they keep it safe | Data protection policy, description of storage and access |
| Capacity | Staff, systems and experience to deliver the planned work | Past projects, references, staff list |

PSEAH means protection from sexual exploitation, abuse and sexual harassment. For safeguarding, we follow the IASC six core principles and, where children are involved, the Keeping Children Safe standards. Where the partner is a member of the Misconduct Disclosure Scheme, or where the law allows, we ask about safe recruitment checks for staff in contact with the people we serve.

## 7. Sanctions and counter-terrorist financing screening

Sanctions are legal restrictions on dealing with named people, organisations, groups or countries. Making funds or goods available to a listed person or entity can be a criminal offence, even by mistake. Screening means checking names against official lists before we deal with someone.

### What we screen

- the organisation itself, by its full legal name and any other names it uses;
- for standard and enhanced levels, its board or committee chair, executive head and bank signatories; for enhanced, all board members and key staff;
- major suppliers and consultants, and large donors;
- any individual who receives our funds directly, other than the people we serve as part of a programme, unless a funder requires otherwise.

### Which lists

We always screen against the **UN Security Council consolidated sanctions list**. We also screen against the lists our funders, banks or the law require, usually:

- the **US OFAC Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons List** (SDN list), published by the US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control;
- the **EU consolidated list** of persons, groups and entities subject to EU financial sanctions;
- the **UK Sanctions List**, published by the UK government;
- [[any national list in the countries where we work]];
- [[any funder exclusion or debarment list]].

We use the free official search tools for each list, or [[a screening service we subscribe to]].

::: adapt Lists your funders require
Read the counter-terrorism and sanctions clause in every grant agreement and list here exactly which lists it requires. Some funders name the lists; others simply require you to ensure that no funds reach listed persons, which in practice means screening against the lists that apply to that funder. On 28 January 2026 the UK replaced the former OFSI Consolidated List with the UK Sanctions List as its single source of designations: check the UK government website for the current list before relying on it. Free search tools change their addresses from time to time, so record the website you used in each log entry. If you screen many names, a paid screening service can save time, but you remain responsible for the decision.
:::

### How we screen and record it

1. Search each name, including other spellings, abbreviations and names in other scripts where known.
2. Record every search in the screening log (Annex 1): name screened, lists searched, date, result and the person who checked.
3. Save a screenshot or printout of the result for every possible match, and for every enhanced-level check.
4. Screen before signing any agreement and before the first payment.

### If there is a possible match

A possible match does not mean a person is listed. Many names are common. Follow these steps:

1. **Stop.** Do not sign, pay, deliver goods or confirm the partnership until the match is resolved.
2. **Compare identifiers.** Check the list entry against what you know: full name, date of birth, nationality, address, registration number, aliases.
3. **If it is clearly a different person or organisation** (a false positive), record why, for example "date of birth and nationality differ". A second person, [[Name, role]], confirms. Then you can continue.
4. **If you cannot rule it out,** escalate to [[Name, role]] within [[1 working day]]. Do not tell the person or organisation that they may be listed until you have advice, because in some countries warning them could itself be an offence.
5. **Take advice** from our bank, the funder or a lawyer. [[Name, role]] decides, and records the decision and reasons.
6. **If it is a true match,** do not proceed and do not make any funds or goods available. Report to the relevant authority where the law requires it, and tell the funder as the grant agreement requires. Our legal adviser confirms what must be reported and by when.

::: adapt Reporting a true match
Reporting duties differ by country and by sanctions regime. For example, US persons who hold property of a listed person may have to block it and report to OFAC within a set time, and UK rules place reporting duties on some types of organisation. Many national laws also require reports to the financial intelligence unit. Check your local law on reporting, freezing and tipping off before you need it, and write the authority's name and contact details here.
:::

## 8. Fair to small local partners

Local organisations often know their communities best, yet complex due diligence excludes them most often. We will:

- explain our checks in plain language and in [[languages]], and help partners complete the questionnaire if they wish;
- accept the evidence partners can reasonably provide, and alternatives where formal documents do not exist;
- allow realistic time, at least [[10 working days]], to respond;
- not ask partners to sign obligations, such as unlimited liability for fraud or screening of all their own suppliers, that they cannot meet, unless we give them the means;
- include the cost of compliance, such as audits, policy development or training, in the budgets we agree with them and with our funders;
- share with partners the results of our assessment and the reasons for any conditions;
- speak up with funders when a requirement is disproportionate for a small local partner.

## 9. Capacity strengthening rather than exclusion

Where we find gaps, such as no written safeguarding policy, a single person controlling all payments or no annual accounts, we agree a capacity plan with the partner. It sets out what will change, the support we will give (for example templates, training, mentoring or a budget line), who is responsible and the deadline. We may add proportionate controls while the plan is under way, such as smaller payments in instalments, receipts before each new payment or extra monitoring visits.

We decline a partnership only where the risk is too serious to manage with reasonable support and controls: for example a confirmed sanctions match, credible evidence of fraud or abuse that has not been addressed, or refusal to adopt basic safeguarding measures where the partner works with children or adults at risk.

::: adapt Resources for capacity support
Decide what support you can realistically offer: for example sharing your own policy templates, a short training session, mentoring by a staff member or a budget line in the sub-grant for an audit or a safeguarding focal point. Funders increasingly accept these costs when they are planned in the budget. Record each commitment in the partner's capacity plan so it is not forgotten.
:::

## 10. Decision and approval

The due diligence lead writes a short assessment with a risk rating (low, medium or high) for each area and overall, and recommends one of four outcomes:

1. **Approve.**
2. **Approve with conditions**, for example payments in instalments or a policy adopted within [[3 months]].
3. **Approve with a capacity plan**, with support and review dates.
4. **Decline**, with reasons shared with the partner as far as the law allows.

The decision is taken by the person named in our Delegation of Authority Matrix. High-risk partnerships and every unresolved possible sanctions match go to [[Name, role]]. Nobody decides on a partner in which they, their family or close friends have an interest. Our Conflict of Interest Policy applies.

# Monitoring and re-screening

Due diligence is not a one-off check. We:

- re-screen every active partner, major supplier and large donor at least every [[6 months / 12 months]], and before renewing any agreement;
- re-screen when a partner tells us about new board members, executive head or bank signatories;
- review the capacity plan and conditions at the dates agreed, and at least every [[6 months]];
- repeat the full assessment every [[2 / 3]] years, or sooner if the relationship, the amount or the risk changes significantly;
- ask partners, in our agreements, to tell us within [[48 hours]] of any suspected fraud, safeguarding concern or possible sanctions link, and within [[5 working days]] of other serious changes, such as a change in legal status.

# Roles and responsibilities

- **Relationship managers** identify when due diligence is needed, explain it to partners and keep track of conditions.
- **The due diligence lead, [[Name, role]],** screens names, runs assessments, keeps the screening log and records, and sets re-screening reminders.
- **Approvers** decide on partnerships within their delegated limits.
- **[[Name, role]]** decides on high-risk partnerships and possible sanctions matches, and reports to the board.
- **The board** approves this policy and receives a short summary at least [[once a year]], covering partnerships approved with conditions, any declined and any possible matches.

# Records, confidentiality and data

We keep, for each relationship: the completed questionnaire and documents, the screening log entries and any screenshots, the assessment and risk rating, the decision and approver, any conditions or capacity plan, and monitoring notes. We keep them for [[7 years]] after the relationship ends, or longer if a funder or the law requires it, as set out in our Records Retention Schedule.

Due diligence records include personal data about partners' board members and staff, and sometimes sensitive information about past incidents. We collect only what we need, store it securely with access limited to [[the due diligence lead and the approver]], and share it only with funders or authorities entitled to see it. We tell partners how we use their information. Our Data Protection Policy applies.

::: adapt Data protection for screening
Screening individuals is processing of personal data, and information about alleged criminal activity is especially sensitive. GDPR principles are a useful benchmark: collect the minimum, explain why, keep it secure and delete it on time. Check your local law, for example the Kenya Data Protection Act 2019, Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023, South Africa's Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA), Brazil's LGPD or India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, on the lawful basis for screening and on handling information about offences. A short privacy statement on the questionnaire usually covers what partners need to know.
:::

# If this policy is breached

If someone enters a relationship without the required checks, or pays a partner before screening, they tell the due diligence lead at once. The lead completes the checks straight away and records what happened. If funds may have reached a listed person, follow the steps in section 7 immediately and tell [[Name, role]] the same day.

Deliberately skipping checks, hiding a possible match or a conflict of interest, or giving false information in an assessment is serious misconduct. We deal with it under our Disciplinary and Grievance Procedure and the law. Concerns can be raised through our Whistleblowing Policy and Procedure.

We ask partners for feedback on our process and use it to make our checks simpler and fairer.
