Planning System · Further reading

Ideas you can put into practice.

Use these short reading guides alongside the lessons. The explanations and fictional examples are original course content. External references offer further perspectives; they are not endorsements of this platform.

1. Strategic Foundation

Make five connected decisions: why you exist, the future you want, the work you do, how you behave and what you will prioritise. These choices help you decide which opportunities to pursue and which to decline.

Purpose

Purpose explains why your organisation needs to exist. Start with a specific unmet need and the people or environment affected by it. A personal motivation can start the journey, but an organisation needs a reason that matters beyond its founder.

Try this: Whose description of the problem have you heard? Who has not yet been included?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Vision

A vision describes the future you want to help create. It focuses on conditions outside your organisation: what will be different for people, communities or the environment if progress is made?

Try this: If your activities succeeded, what would people be able to do that they cannot do now?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Mission

A mission explains what your organisation does now, for whom and how. It turns your broad purpose into a practical scope. Staff, partners and supporters should be able to use it to understand your contribution.

Try this: Which attractive opportunity would fall outside your mission?

Read the full lesson and apply it →

2. Theory of Change

Explain how your work could contribute to change. Work backwards from the future you seek, then check the pathway forwards. Your map is a reasoned hypothesis to test with people affected, not proof that an intervention works.

Understanding Theory of Change

A Theory of Change makes your reasoning explicit: if we do this work, why might a particular change follow? It includes the context, people, sequence of changes and assumptions connecting them.

Try this: What has to happen outside your organisation for your idea to work?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Defining the problem

Describe the current situation, who experiences it and the context that sustains it. Distinguish symptoms from possible causes. A lack of workshops may be your observation, but it is rarely the underlying social problem.

Try this: Which cause is supported by evidence, and which is currently a guess?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Long-term impact

Impact is the longer-term change in people’s lives or environmental conditions to which your work aims to contribute. It may take years and depend on actors beyond your team.

Try this: What wider change matters even after a participant leaves your programme?

Read the full lesson and apply it →

Further reading: Theory of Change · BetterEvaluation ↗

3. Team, Structure & Coordination

Design the work and decision rights before adding job titles. A small team needs clarity about responsibility, support and escalation as much as a large organisation does.

Organisation structure

Start with the capabilities your mission and activities require. Group related work such as delivery, finance and partnerships, then decide how it should be coordinated.

Try this: Where would a concern go if it involved the person’s usual manager?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Roles and decision rights

A role describes a contribution, expected results and authority. A task list alone does not tell someone what success looks like or what they can decide.

Try this: Which recurring decision currently gets delayed or duplicated?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Coordination and work allocation

Coordination makes hand-offs reliable. A hand-off needs a named recipient, an agreed output and a date. Sharing a document is not the same as agreeing who will act on it.

Try this: Which hand-off is most likely to fail?

Read the full lesson and apply it →

Further reading: Delegating decisions · NCVO (UK context) ↗

4. Organisation Internal Systems

Choose simple routines for information, communication and learning. A system includes people and decisions as well as software. Start with the work that must be dependable.

Choosing internal systems

An internal system is a repeatable way of doing necessary work. Before choosing an application, identify the process, owner, information and decision it supports.

Try this: Which repeated task depends on someone remembering everything?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Brand and organisational identity

Your brand is the expectation people form about your organisation. Visual identity helps recognition, but the promise should match the experience you can deliver.

Try this: What do you want people to trust you to do consistently?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Audience and channel planning

Communication begins with what an audience needs to understand or do. A channel is how you reach them: a meeting, newsletter, local radio segment or website.

Try this: Where do your intended users already seek trusted information?

Read the full lesson and apply it →

Further reading: Theory of Change · BetterEvaluation ↗

5. Policies & Procedures

Prioritise controls that protect people, resources and trust. This module supports planning; the applicable legal duties and specialist review depend on your jurisdiction and activities.

Policy priorities

A policy states a principle or rule; a procedure explains how people apply it. A policy register helps you see what exists, who owns it and when it needs review.

Try this: Which gap could cause the greatest harm if ignored?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Writing a usable procedure

A procedure should help someone carry out a real task consistently. Define its trigger, responsible role, steps, records and exceptions.

Try this: Which everyday task is currently handled differently by each person?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Financial controls

Financial controls help prevent errors and misuse and make records dependable. Common principles include documented approvals, evidence for transactions and independent review where practical.

Try this: Where could an error pass unnoticed?

Read the full lesson and apply it →

6. Strategy to Action, Timeline & Gantt

Translate objectives into deliverables, tasks and a realistic sequence. Build a small initial work plan, then use the timeline to discuss trade-offs and dependencies.

From objectives to deliverables

A deliverable is a tangible result that someone can review: a tested service, approved training pack or published report. Start with what must exist to support your objectives.

Try this: Who needs to accept the work before it is useful?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Ownership and capacity

Assign an accountable owner to each deliverable and identify who contributes. An owner needs enough authority, time and support to coordinate the work.

Try this: Where is your plan relying on invisible overtime?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Milestones and dependencies

A milestone marks a significant decision or completed result. A dependency means one task needs something from another before it can proceed.

Try this: Which delay would affect the largest part of your plan?

Read the full lesson and apply it →

7. Business Model

Explain how your organisation creates value, delivers it and pays for the work. A purpose-led model must be useful to people and feasible to sustain.

Value and audiences

A business model explains who benefits, what value they receive, how delivery works and how resources are sustained. For a nonprofit, the person paying may differ from the person using the service.

Try this: Who benefits, who pays and who decides?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Delivery and partners

Your delivery model explains how people access and experience the offer. Include the activities, skills, partners and channels needed to make it reliable.

Try this: Which partner dependency needs a fallback?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Costs and income

Costs include the direct work of delivery and the support that makes it possible. Include coordination, accessibility, maintenance and review rather than counting only visible materials.

Try this: Which cost disappears from your plan because someone currently contributes it unpaid?

Read the full lesson and apply it →

Further reading: Business model design · Strategyzer ↗

8. Fundraising, Sponsors & Partnerships

Choose funding and relationships that fit your mission, stage and capacity. Build a focused approach instead of applying for every opportunity.

Choose a funding mix

Different income sources bring different expectations, restrictions and work. Individual giving, grants, earned income and sponsorship can each fit some organisations better than others.

Try this: Which income source could pull your work away from its purpose?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Donor and partner mapping

Research fit before making an approach. Look at published priorities, geography, eligibility, typical support and application timing. A large budget alone does not make a prospect suitable.

Try this: What would rule out an otherwise attractive prospect?

Read the full lesson and apply it →
Your case for support

A case for support connects the need, your response, credible evidence and a specific request. Help the reader understand why the work matters and what their support makes possible.

Try this: What evidence would a careful supporter reasonably ask to see?

Read the full lesson and apply it →

Further reading: Funding research · Candid ↗
