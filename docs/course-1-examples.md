# The five example organisations (fictional) — story bible for Course 1

Every Course 1 lesson shows how these five organisations complete that lesson's exercise. They are the SAME organisations throughout, so their answers must stay consistent from lesson to lesson (purpose → vision → mission → values → form → positioning → objectives → Theory of Change). They are fictional; never present them as real.

Gallery ids and labels (use exactly):
- `idea`   — "Community group · idea stage" · Indonesia
- `ngo`    — "Registered nonprofit · delivering" · Kenya
- `social` — "Social enterprise · company" · Peru
- `coop`   — "Cooperative · member-owned" · India
- `hybrid` — "Hybrid · charity with a trading company" · United Kingdom

## idea — Tide Line Youth Collective (working name), North Bali, Indonesia
- Profile: wants to run mangrove restoration and environmental education with young people (12–17) in two coastal villages. Not registered; choosing between an association (perkumpulan) and a foundation (yayasan). Idea stage: two co-founders (Wayan, a teacher; Sari, a marine biology graduate), four volunteers, no staff, first-year budget estimated under US$8,000. Will work with children and hold contact details.
- Need/purpose: mangroves along the two villages' coast have been cleared for shrimp ponds and building; erosion and flooding are worsening; young people have few chances to learn about or act on their own coast. Purpose: "We exist so that young people in coastal villages can protect and restore the coast they depend on."
- Vision: "Healthy mangrove coasts cared for by the young people who live beside them."
- Mission: "We help young people in two North Bali villages restore mangroves and learn about their coast through monthly planting days and school sessions."
- Values: Led by young people; Respect for village custom (adat) and leaders; Honest about results (survival rates, not just seedlings planted).
- Context: village heads supportive; one school interested; a provincial environment office runs occasional planting events; shrimp pond owners may resist; tourism growth; seedling survival depends on species/site; rainy season Nov–Mar.
- Stakeholders: young people (benefit, high interest, low influence); parents; village heads (decide on land use, high influence); school principal; shrimp pond owners (influence, possible opposition); provincial environment office; small local donors; nearby dive operators (possible funders).
- Form: leaning towards association (perkumpulan) because members (young people and volunteers) should have a voice; foundation considered for holding donations/land agreements. Needs local legal advice.
- Positioning: the only youth-led restoration effort in these villages; trusted because the co-founders are from the area; will not run tourist "plant-a-tree" experiences for now.
- Objectives: ESO1 Young people in the two villages lead regular restoration of degraded mangrove areas with at least two-thirds of seedlings surviving. ESO2 Young people understand and speak up for their coast in village decisions. ISO1 Register a legal entity with a small board. ISO2 Safe, trained volunteer team (safeguarding for work with children). ISO3 A small, reliable local funding base.
- ToC (ESO1): Problem: mangrove loss → erosion/flooding; causes: clearance for ponds and building, low awareness, no organised replanting, past plantings failed (wrong species/site). Impact: more resilient coasts and village livelihoods over 10+ years (contribution). Outcomes: short — young people gain planting/monitoring skills; village heads agree restoration sites; medium — youth teams run monthly planting and monitoring; seedling survival improves; long — restored mangrove areas grow; village rules protect them. Outputs: 10 planting days/year, 2 school sessions/month, survival monitoring twice a year, 1 site agreement per village. Activities: agree sites with village heads; source seedlings from a local nursery; train youth team leaders; run planting days; monitor survival; school sessions. Inputs: co-founders' time (2 days/week each), 4 volunteers, seedlings (~US$1,500/yr), boots/tools, a boat for one site, safeguarding training, permission from village heads. Assumptions: parents allow children to take part; seedlings survive at chosen sites (right species, tidal zone); pond owners do not clear restored areas; youth stay engaged beyond first months.

## ngo — Imara Girls in Science, Kisumu County, Kenya
- Profile: registered nonprofit since 2019; weekly after-school science clubs for girls aged 10–14 in 14 public schools in two sub-counties; holiday science camp; mentoring by women working in science; ~600 girls a year; 6 staff (director Achieng, programme manager, 3 club coordinators, finance officer), 25 volunteer mentors; budget ~US$140,000 from two foundation grants and local businesses.
- Need/purpose: girls in public schools get little hands-on science (few labs, large classes), fall behind in maths and science and rarely see women in science careers; many drop science subjects in secondary school. Purpose: "We exist so that girls in Kisumu can choose science, and the futures it opens, with confidence."
- Vision: "A Kisumu where girls who love science have the skills, support and role models to follow it."
- Mission: "We help girls aged 10 to 14 in Kisumu public schools build science confidence and skills through weekly hands-on clubs, holiday camps and mentoring by women in science."
- Values: Girls first (safeguarding and girls' voices shape decisions); Learn by doing; Partnership with schools and parents; Accountable (report results honestly, including what did not work).
- Context: national curriculum reform (competency-based curriculum) emphasises practical learning; school labs scarce; foundation funding concentrated in a few donors; mobile phone access growing; county education office supportive; other STEM NGOs work in Nairobi more than Kisumu.
- Stakeholders: girls (benefit, high interest, low influence — girls' advisory group); parents/guardians; head teachers (decide access); teachers; county education office (influence, consulted termly); two foundation funders (pay, decide on grants); local businesses (sponsor camp); volunteer mentors; universities (mentor source).
- Form: registered nonprofit (fits grants and donations); board of 7. Considering whether to register a separate trading arm for paid STEM kits — decided not now.
- Positioning: the only girls-only, club-based science programme in Kisumu public schools; mentors are local women scientists; will not run general tutoring or exam coaching.
- Objectives: ESO1 Girls in partner schools gain confidence and practical skills in science and keep science subjects into secondary school. ESO2 Partner schools sustain hands-on science for girls with trained teachers. ISO1 Diversify income so no funder exceeds 40%. ISO2 Strengthen safeguarding and volunteer mentor training. ISO3 Simple results data system.
- ToC (ESO1): Problem: girls drop science; causes: little practical teaching, low confidence, few role models, family expectations, cost of secondary science. Impact: more young women from Kisumu in science study and careers (contribution, 10+ years). Outcomes: short — girls' confidence and practical skills improve; parents support girls' science; medium — girls choose science subjects at secondary; teachers use hands-on methods; long — more girls complete secondary science and enter science-related study. Outputs: 14 weekly clubs (about 40 girls each, ~560 girls), 1 holiday camp (120 girls, mostly club members), 25 trained mentors, termly parent sessions, club kits per school. Activities: train coordinators/mentors; run clubs; camp; parent sessions; teacher workshops; track attendance and confidence. Inputs: 6 staff, 25 mentors, kits (~US$6,000/yr), school rooms, transport, safeguarding training, data tool. Assumptions: head teachers keep giving club time; mentors stay for a year; confidence translates into subject choices; families can afford secondary science.

## social — Pan del Barrio S.A.C., Lima, Peru
- Profile: private limited company (S.A.C.), social purpose in its bylaws; neighbourhood bakery and café in San Juan de Lurigancho, wholesale bread for three offices, six-month paid training programme for young people (18–24) who left school early; trading two years; 9 employees, 6 of them trainees; turnover ~US$210,000, near break-even. Founders: Lucía (pastry chef) and Diego (former NGO youth worker).
- Need/purpose: young people who left school early in the district struggle to find formal, safe work; informal jobs pay little and teach few skills. Purpose: "We exist so that young people who left school early can build a skilled, formal working life."
- Vision: "Neighbourhoods where leaving school early does not close the door to good work."
- Mission: "We train and employ young people aged 18 to 24 who left school early, through a paid six-month bakery apprenticeship and a business that sells good bread."
- Values: Real jobs, real pay (trainees are paid, on contract); Quality bread, every day; Second chances with clear expectations; Pay our way (the business must sustain itself).
- Context: high youth informality in Lima; growing demand for artisan bread among offices/cafés; rising flour prices; Peru's BIC law (benefit companies) available; municipal youth employment programmes; competition from supermarkets.
- Stakeholders: trainees (benefit, interest high, influence low — trainee voice); customers (pay); office clients (pay, decide on contracts); employers in food sector (hire graduates); families; municipality youth programme (referrals, possible funding); flour supplier; investors/lenders.
- Form: company (S.A.C.) because most income is sales; considering BIC status to lock in purpose; considering a partner nonprofit for training grants later.
- Positioning: the only bakery in the district that combines a paid apprenticeship with a recognised baking certificate; quality bread lets it compete on product, not pity; will not take trainees under 18 or run unpaid internships.
- Objectives: ESO1 Trainees complete the apprenticeship and move into formal jobs in food businesses within three months. ESO2 Graduates keep stable formal work a year later. ISO1 Reach break-even and a 3-month cash reserve. ISO2 Training costs funded without raising bread prices (grants or employer fees). ISO3 Decide on BIC status.
- ToC (ESO1): Problem: informal, low-skill work for early school leavers; causes: no qualifications, no work history, employers' distrust, need to earn while learning. Impact: more young people in stable formal work in the district (contribution). Outcomes: short — trainees gain baking and work skills, reliability; medium — trainees earn the certificate and get formal job offers; long — graduates keep and progress in formal jobs. Outputs: 12 trainees/year in two cohorts, 6-month paid apprenticeship, certificate assessment, job-matching with 8 employer partners. Activities: recruit trainees with municipality; train on the job; weekly mentoring; certificate preparation; employer partnerships; follow-up at 3 and 12 months. Inputs: bakery and equipment, master baker time, trainee wages, mentor (Diego), employer partners, certificate body. Assumptions: employers hire certified graduates; trainees can stay 6 months on the wage paid; bakery sales cover most costs; certificate is recognised.

## coop — Vanita Weavers Cooperative Society, Kerala, India
- Profile: registered cooperative society, 140 member weavers (mostly women) who elect a committee of nine; established 11 years; 4 staff (manager Meera, quality officer, designer, accounts clerk); handloom saris and fabric sold to shops and through a shared online shop; annual sales ~US$95,000.
- Need/purpose: handloom weavers earn little as middlemen take most of the price; demand for traditional handloom falling among the young; younger people are leaving weaving. Purpose: "We exist so that handloom weavers in our district earn a fair living from their craft."
- Vision: "A living handloom tradition in our district that pays weavers fairly and attracts young people."
- Mission: "We help our member weavers earn more and more reliably by buying yarn together, improving quality and design, and selling direct to shops and online buyers."
- Values: Members decide; Fair and on-time payment; Quality we are proud of; Open books (members can see the accounts).
- Context: government handloom schemes and the state cooperative department; online craft marketplaces growing; competition from power-loom copies; rising yarn prices; young people prefer other work; monsoon disrupts dyeing.
- Stakeholders: member weavers (benefit and own/govern — high interest, high influence); elected committee (decide); staff; retail shops (pay); online buyers (pay); state cooperative department (regulates, audits); government handloom schemes (possible grants); yarn suppliers; young people in weaving families (future members).
- Form: cooperative society (member-owned and governed, surplus shared with members as bonus); considering whether a grant for a dye unit would affect control.
- Positioning: the only member-owned weavers' group in the district selling online direct; authentic handloom with traceable weaver names; will not sell power-loom products or take orders below a fair piece rate.
- Objectives: ESO1 Member weavers' average income from weaving rises and is paid on time. ESO2 Young people join as apprentice weavers and stay. ISO1 Grow direct online sales to 40% of revenue. ISO2 Keep democratic control with any new funding. ISO3 Build a shared dye unit.
- ToC (ESO1): Problem: low and irregular weaver income; causes: middlemen margins, expensive yarn bought alone, uneven quality, weak market access, power-loom competition. Impact: secure livelihoods and a living craft (contribution). Outcomes: short — members buy yarn at lower cost; quality improves; medium — more sales direct at better prices; payments on time; long — higher, steadier income; young people see weaving as viable. Outputs: bulk yarn purchases monthly, quality checks on every piece, 2 new design collections/year, online shop listings, payment within 15 days. Activities: pool yarn orders; quality checks; design workshops; photography and online listing; shop partnerships; apprentice scheme. Inputs: member labour and looms, working capital for yarn, staff, designer, online shop fees, photography, committee time. Assumptions: members commit to quality standards; online buyers pay a premium for authentic handloom; working capital available; committee agrees fair rules.

## hybrid — Riverside Repair Hub, Leeds, UK
- Profile: registered charity (CIO) in England plus a trading company wholly owned by the charity, which runs a shop selling refurbished goods and passes its profits to the charity. Growing: 5 staff (director Tom, workshop lead Priya, volunteer coordinator, schools officer, shop manager), 40 volunteers; charity income ~£180,000; shop turnover ~£120,000. Weekly repair cafés, school workshops, a tool library, the refurbished goods shop.
- Need/purpose: households throw away repairable items, wasting money and resources; repair skills and affordable repair are disappearing, hitting low-income households hardest; people are isolated. Purpose: "We exist so that people in Leeds can repair, reuse and share instead of throwing away."
- Vision: "A Leeds where repairing and sharing are normal, affordable and open to everyone."
- Mission: "We help Leeds residents keep things in use and learn repair skills through free repair cafés, school workshops, a tool library and a shop selling refurbished goods."
- Values: Fix first; Everyone can learn; Welcoming and safe (electrical safety, inclusive); Every pound to the mission (trading profits go to the charity).
- Context: council waste reduction targets and right-to-repair rules; cost-of-living pressures; many volunteers with skills (retired engineers); rising rents; competition from cheap new goods; insurance and electrical safety (PAT testing) rules.
- Stakeholders: residents using repair cafés (benefit); shop customers (pay); volunteers; schools; Leeds City Council (waste team — funds/influences); trustees (decide, accountable); grant funders (National Lottery-type funders, local trusts); local businesses donating items; landlords.
- Form: charity (CIO) for the community work and grants; trading company for the shop to keep trading risk and tax separate; profits passed to the charity each year (a company donation, not Gift Aid; set-up confirmed with an accountant).
- Positioning: the only combined repair café, tool library and refurbished shop in the city; volunteers with trade skills; will not sell new goods or do paid commercial repairs that compete with local repairers.
- Objectives: ESO1 More residents, especially low-income households, repair and reuse items instead of discarding them. ESO2 Young people gain basic repair skills and confidence. ISO1 Shop profits cover at least 30% of charity costs. ISO2 Clear reporting between trading company and charity for trustees. ISO3 Volunteer safety and training (electrical safety).
- ToC (ESO1): Problem: repairable items discarded; causes: lack of skills/tools, cost of professional repair, convenience of cheap new goods, low awareness. Impact: less waste and lower household costs in Leeds (contribution). Outcomes: short — residents get items fixed and learn simple repairs; medium — residents repair or borrow tools before buying new; long — household waste falls and repair culture grows. Outputs: 48 repair cafés/year (~2,000 items), 300 tool loans, 30 school workshops, items diverted from landfill (kg), shop sales of refurbished goods. Activities: run repair cafés; volunteer training and PAT testing; tool library; school workshops; refurbish donated goods; shop. Inputs: 40 volunteers, 5 staff, workshop space, tools, insurance, donated goods, council and grant funding, shop income. Assumptions: residents trust volunteer repairs; enough skilled volunteers; repaired items last; people return to repair rather than buy new.
