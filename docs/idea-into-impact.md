# Idea into Impact — the starter course for entrepreneurs

The course for people at level 0: they have an idea for a project, a nonprofit, a social business or a cooperative, and nothing is built yet. It follows the same five Method into Impact steps as the organisation courses (Clarify, Organise, Prove & Show, Protect, Sustain & Run), at beginner depth, and ends with one shareable output: **Your Launch Plan**.

The five foundation courses (Planning System) stay as the track for organisations that are already running.

## Who it is for
- Individuals or small founding teams with an idea and no organisation yet (or an informal group that has just started).
- Any model: community project, nonprofit, social business, cooperative. The examples show all three kinds.
- Usually doing this alongside a job, studies or family; little money; first time building anything.

## Design rules
- Short: 14 lessons + 1 review, about 6 hours, 20–30 minutes each.
- Plain British English for second-language readers: sentences under 20 words where possible, one idea per paragraph, every term explained the first time it appears.
- Every exercise can be done by someone with only an idea. No board, team, budget or registration assumed.
- 2–3 fields per lesson. No "a | b | c" tables: lists are "one per line".
- Honest about limits: no promise of funding; legal points are "check locally".
- Each lesson shows how three example founders answer the same exercise.

### idea — Tide Line Youth Collective (working name), North Bali, Indonesia
- Founders: Wayan (secondary school teacher, 38) and Sari (marine biology graduate, 26), both from the area. Four volunteers interested. No money yet; each can give about two days a week (Wayan mainly weekends and school holidays).
- Problem: mangroves along the coast of two villages cleared for shrimp ponds and building; erosion and flooding getting worse; young people (12–17) grow up beside the coast with few chances to learn about it or act. Past plantings by outsiders failed (wrong species, wrong place).
- Talked to: 2 village heads, 11 young people, 6 parents, 1 shrimp pond owner, a local nursery owner. Surprise: young people wanted to monitor and measure, not just plant; parents worried about safety near water; the pond owner was open to a buffer strip if it did not cost him land.
- Purpose: "We exist so that young people in coastal villages can protect and restore the coast they depend on."
- Mission (first draft): "We help young people in two North Bali villages restore mangroves and learn about their coast through monthly planting days and school sessions."
- Solution and chain: monthly planting-and-monitoring days led by trained youth team leaders + school sessions. If young people learn to plant the right species in the right places and monitor them, more seedlings survive; restored areas protect the coast; young people speak up for it.
- Biggest assumption: parents let their children take part regularly.
- Pilot: 3 planting days over 3 months at one agreed site in one village, 15 young people, 500 seedlings; success = at least 12 young people come to all three and at least 60% of seedlings alive after 3 months.
- Team and form: Wayan (schools, families, village heads), Sari (species, sites, monitoring), 4 volunteers; gap: someone for money and records. Form for now: informal group hosted by the village youth organisation (karang taruna); decide later between association (perkumpulan) and foundation (yayasan) with local advice.
- Measures: young people attending each day; seedlings planted and % alive at 3 and 6 months; young people who can explain why mangroves matter (short quiz). Will not claim "flooding reduced" for years.
- Others: provincial environment office runs occasional tourist planting events; a dive operator does beach clean-ups. Different: youth-led, local, monitors survival. Possible partners: the school, the nursery, the dive operator.
- Pitch ask: US$600 for seedlings, boots, gloves and the boat for the pilot from a local dive operator (US$400) and two village shops (US$100 each); the school's permission for 2 sessions a month.
- Budget (pilot, 3 months): seedlings ~US$250 (500 × US$0.50), boots/gloves/tools US$200, boat hire for one site US$90, snacks and water US$60, first-aid kit and training US$50, spare money US$50 → about US$700. Income: confirmed US$100 (founders' own), hoped US$600 (dive operator + 2 shops). If not: plant 250 seedlings on land reachable on foot (about US$485).
- Safe and legal: children → parental consent forms, two adults always present, never alone with a child, life jackets near water, a named safeguarding person (Wayan); photos only with consent; village heads' written permission for sites; check local rules on planting in coastal zones.
- Risks: parents say no (talk at village meeting first); seedlings die (right species, Sari checks tides); pond owner clears the site (written agreement, buffer strip); founders burn out (cap at 2 days/week each).
- Money: first sources — founders' own US$100; local businesses (dive operator, 2 shops); a small grant from the regency environment fund later. Won't take money that requires tourist "plant-a-tree" photo events.
- 90 days (Nov 2026–Jan 2027): Month 1 site agreement, consent forms, volunteer training, planting day 1 (Sat 28 Nov); Month 2 planting day 2 (Sat 19 Dec); Month 3 planting day 3 (Sat 9 Jan) + survival count by 30 Jan; review with village heads in early February: continue, change site, or stop. Second survival count at month 6.

### huerta — Huerta Viva (working name), Medellín, Colombia
- Founder: Camila Restrepo (34), agronomist who works part-time for a seed company; her neighbour Luz Marina (52, community leader in Comuna 8) helps. Camila can give about 15 hours a week. Savings of about US$1,500 she is willing to risk.
- Problem: women heads of household in hillside neighbourhoods of Comuna 8 spend a large share of income on food, eat few fresh vegetables, and have few ways to earn from home. Many have small patios, rooftops or balconies that are unused.
- Talked to: 18 women in Comuna 8, 2 community kitchen leaders, 1 market stall holder, a local Junta de Acción Comunal (neighbourhood council) member. Surprise: most women wanted to save money on food first and only some wanted to sell; time was the biggest barrier (they could do 1 workshop a month, not weekly); several did not trust "projects" that promised income.
- Purpose: "We exist so that women in Medellín's hillside neighbourhoods can feed their families well and earn from what they grow."
- Mission (first draft): "We help women heads of household in Comuna 8 grow vegetables at home through low-cost garden kits, monthly workshops and a shared Saturday market stall."
- Model: social business — sells garden kits (containers, soil, seedlings) to women at a low price below cost (small instalments, about US$200 in total for 20 kits) and at full price to offices and schools; women can sell surplus at a shared stall; the extra from office/school sales covers the women's lower price and the workshops.
- Chain: if women get an affordable kit and monthly practical help, they grow vegetables at home; they spend less on food and eat more vegetables; some earn by selling surplus.
- Biggest assumption: women keep the gardens going after the first month (watering, pests, time).
- Pilot: 20 kits to women in one sector for 4 months + 4 monthly workshops + 2 Saturday stalls; success = 15 of 20 gardens still producing at month 4; women report saving on vegetables; at least 5 sell something.
- Team and form: Camila (growing, kits, sales), Luz Marina (recruitment, trust, workshops venue); gaps: bookkeeping, someone for sales to offices. Form for now: Camila sells as an individual (registered with the tax office, RUT) during the pilot; if it works, register a company (S.A.S.) and consider the BIC status (Colombia's benefit company); a foundation is not the right fit because most income will be sales.
- Measures: gardens producing at month 1, 2, 4; money women say they saved per week on vegetables; women who sold surplus and how much. Will not claim "food security improved".
- Others: the city's urban agriculture programme gives occasional free seeds and training (irregular); garden shops sell expensive kits; NGOs run food-parcel programmes. Different: low-cost kit + ongoing monthly help + a stall; run by people from the neighbourhood. Possible partners: city urban agriculture programme, a community kitchen, a school.
- Pitch ask: pre-orders of 30 kits from 2 schools and 1 office at full price (to fund 20 subsidised kits); a free room for workshops from the Junta.
- Budget (pilot, 4 months): 20 kits at cost ~US$35 each = US$700; workshop materials US$120; stall fees and transport US$100; Camila's time unpaid in pilot (noted); small contingency US$80 → about US$1,000. Income: confirmed US$1,500 savings (willing to use US$1,000); hoped: women's instalments ~US$200 and school/office pre-orders (the 30 pre-ordered kits are paid in advance and sit outside the pilot budget). If not: 10 kits instead of 20 (about US$650).
- Safe and legal: register with DIAN (RUT) before selling and ask what else applies; ask the chamber of commerce whether she must also register as a trader; ask about simple sales invoicing; WhatsApp reminders one by one, groups only with consent; data — names and phone numbers of women kept on her phone only with permission, not shared; photos only with consent; check that seedlings come from a registered nursery; workshops in a safe public venue.
- Risks: women drop out (monthly not weekly workshops, WhatsApp reminders); pests/rains destroy gardens (hardy crops, rainy-season timing); schools don't buy (start with a smaller pilot); Camila loses her savings (cap at US$1,000).
- Money: first sources — Camila's savings (capped); school and office kit sales; women's small instalments; later a city or chamber of commerce entrepreneurship fund. Won't take a loan before the pilot proves women keep gardens.
- 90 days (Feb–Apr 2027): Month 1 recruit 20 women, buy kits, book venue, school orders agreed; Month 2 deliver kits, workshop 1, deliver school orders; Month 3 workshop 2, first stall, month-1 garden visits; day 90 review (first week of May) with Luz Marina and 5 women: carry on, change or stop early. Workshops 3–4 and the second stall follow in May–June; the grow/change/stop decision comes after the month-4 count in early July.

### mentors — Next Step Mentors (working name), Accra, Ghana
- Founder: Kwame Mensah (24), graduated in economics, works full-time at a bank; grew up in Nima. Can give evenings and Saturdays (about 6 hours a week). Two university friends (Abena, a teaching student, and Yaw, an engineering student) interested.
- Problem: pupils in their final year of junior high school (about 14–15) in Nima have few people to help them prepare for the national exam (BECE) and choose a senior high school programme; many choose without guidance and some drop out of education.
- Talked to: 2 head teachers, 9 pupils, 7 parents, 1 church youth leader, 6 university students. Surprise: pupils wanted help choosing a school programme as much as exam help; parents worried about who the mentors were; university students were willing but needed transport money.
- Purpose: "We exist so that young people in Nima can finish school and choose their next step with confidence."
- Mission (first draft): "We help final-year junior high school pupils in Nima prepare for the BECE and choose their senior high school programme, through weekly Saturday mentoring by trained university students."
- Model: volunteer project; costs low; aims to be hosted by a church youth programme first and later register as an NGO.
- Chain: if pupils meet a trained mentor every Saturday for study help and guidance, they prepare better and make informed choices; more do well in the BECE and enrol in a programme that suits them.
- Biggest assumption: university students keep coming every Saturday for a whole term.
- Pilot: 8 Saturdays in one term at one school, 6 mentors, 24 pupils (groups of 4); success = 5 of 6 mentors attend at least 7 of 8 Saturdays; 20 of 24 pupils attend most sessions; pupils and parents say the guidance helped them choose.
- Team and form: Kwame (organiser, school relations), Abena (study sessions), Yaw (recruits mentors); gap: someone to handle money and parents' contact. Form for now: an unregistered project hosted by the church youth programme (which has a bank account and safeguarding policy); register later as an NGO (company limited by guarantee and registration with the Department of Social Welfare) once it runs a full year — check current steps locally.
- Measures: pupil attendance; mentor attendance; pupils who submit their school choices on time; BECE results compared with the school's previous year (reported carefully, not claimed as caused by the project).
- Others: paid private tutoring (too expensive for most families); a few NGOs run mentoring in other districts; churches run Sunday youth groups. Different: near-peer mentors from the same community, Saturday sessions in the school, focus on the choice as well as the exam. Partners: the school, the church, a university student association.
- Pitch ask: the head teacher's permission to use two classrooms on Saturdays; GH₵ 1,800 (about US$150) from the church and a local business for mentors' transport, snacks and printing (the budget is GH₵ 2,400; Kwame puts in GH₵ 600 himself).
- Budget (pilot, one term): mentor transport 6 × 8 × GH₵ 30 = GH₵ 1,440; snacks and water GH₵ 480; printing past papers and guides GH₵ 300; mentor training and police check fees GH₵ 180 → about GH₵ 2,400 (about US$200). Income: confirmed GH₵ 600 from Kwame; hoped GH₵ 1,800 from church and a business. If not: 4 mentors and 16 pupils.
- Safe and legal: working with children → parents' written consent, sessions only in the school in open classrooms, never one-to-one alone, mentors sign a code of conduct and get a police check where possible, a named safeguarding person (the church youth leader) and a way to report concerns; pupils' data on paper kept by the school; no photos without consent.
- Risks: mentors drop out (transport money, a WhatsApp group, a certificate for mentors); head teacher changes mind (written agreement); a safeguarding incident (rules above, church policy); Kwame overcommits (cap at 6 hours a week, Abena runs one Saturday a month).
- Money: first sources — Kwame's own GH₵ 600; the church; one local business; later a small grant from a youth or education fund. Won't take money that requires branding pupils' uniforms or photos of children.
- 90 days (Jan–Mar 2027): mentors recruited by 19 Dec; Month 1 school agreement (8 Jan), mentor training, consent forms (20 Jan); 8 Saturdays from 23 Jan to 20 Mar, skipping 6 Mar (Independence Day); feedback from pupils, parents, mentors by 27 Mar; review Sat 3 Apr with the head teacher, the church youth leader and Abena: continue next term, change, or stop. Money: Kwame's GH₵ 600 paid into the church account by 30 Nov; church GH₵ 1,200 by 30 Nov; local business GH₵ 600 by 15 Dec.
